\section{Summary of the Thesis}\label{sec:concl:summary}

This thesis set out to make physical agents robust to the conditions that the real world presents but their training data do not contain, a problem it called the curse of reality. Following the perceive--reason--act loop, it addressed the problem at each stage, and it recorded for each core chapter which shift was actually tested and at what level of fidelity (Table~\ref{tab:method:tests}).

For perception, Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} proposed to describe driving scenes by 3D semantic occupancy. The OccNet model, the OpenOcc benchmark and the large-scale OpenScene data showed that occupancy describes irregularly shaped objects of known classes better than boxes and transfers to several downstream tasks. In open-loop evaluation on nuScenes, planning on predicted occupancy gave slightly lower collision rates than planning on boxes predicted by the same network (0.06--0.24 percentage points, single runs; not statistically established), and at the higher fidelity of NAVSIM no planning benefit of occupancy has been shown. Occupancy opens the geometry of the representation but not its semantics, and its robustness to distribution shift was not tested.

For reasoning, Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} introduced Graph Visual Question Answering, the DriveLM datasets and a vision-language baseline. On a new sensor configuration, the baseline had a lower trajectory error than a driving-specific model, mostly owing to its pre-trained vision-language backbone, in a comparison confounded by camera inputs and model size. Graph-structured reasoning improved speed decisions under this shift, but in familiar situations it lowered open-loop accuracy and added latency, and on an unseen object class it helped only through a question written with knowledge of that class.

For acting, Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} showed, on logged frames in the non-reactive simulation of NAVSIM, that a trajectory-scoring planner can adapt its per-frame decisions at test time by minimising its own uncertainty, without test-time rules. Every test-time training variant improved its base planner by 0.4 to 2.3 PDMS in single runs, whereas the uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories implemented for comparison lowered it; behaviour in closed loop was not tested, and the hardest safety-critical scenarios remain far from human performance. Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} showed, on real robots, that a pre-trained policy post-trained with components that target inconsistencies between demonstrations, model and execution raised its success rate on one folding task from about 27\% to about 97\% over 30 trials, with a modest post-training budget. That inconsistency rather than scale is the main obstacle remains a hypothesis, since scale was not varied at a matched budget and the largest single gain came from targeted recovery data.

Across these chapters, Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} reconstructed, as a reflective synthesis rather than a tested contribution, the benchmark-driven framework behind the twenty publications to which the author contributed. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} proposed, as a research agenda graded by the strength of its evidence, five elements of a self-correcting physical agent: an open, queryable representation; fast and slow decision paths; a competence monitor; adaptation at deployment; and a learning loop that feeds residual failures back into data and benchmarks. Only adaptation at deployment is demonstrated, and only on logged frames in non-reactive simulation; the evidence for a competence monitor is weak, the learning loop is partially supported, and the other elements remain hypotheses.

\section{Future Directions}\label{sec:concl:future}

The limitations discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc:limits} point to five directions for future research.

\paragraph{From occupancy to open, predictive world representations.} Occupancy made the geometry of the scene category-free; the next step is to make its semantics open-vocabulary and its time axis predictive, so that an agent can represent not only unseen objects but also how the scene is likely to evolve. Occupancy forecasting and generative world models, for which OpenScene already provides training data, are natural vehicles for this step. Whether any of these representations helps planning should be tested under distribution shift and in closed loop, not only in open-loop evaluation.

\paragraph{Grounded and efficient reasoning.} The reasoning studied in Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} helped under a sensing shift but is slow and can be ungrounded. Future systems should decide \emph{when} to reason, using competence signals once such signals are shown to separate failures from successes at every operating point, and should be trained and evaluated on balanced data with input-ablation and majority-class controls, so that fluent answers cannot substitute for looking at the scene.

\paragraph{From test-time training to lifelong adaptation.} Centaur adapts with a single gradient step on a short buffer, and it has been evaluated only on logged frames. The first step is to evaluate it where its own actions determine what it sees next, in pseudo-simulation or closed-loop simulation. Extending it to continual adaptation over days of deployment then raises open questions about stability, forgetting, and how to bound the effect of an update so that adaptation remains verifiable.

\paragraph{Self-directed learning for robots.} \chiz still depends on human demonstrations and on human-triggered recovery data. The perspective of~\cite{chen2025robot} outlines the alternative: robots that identify their own sub-goals, acquire skills to reach them, and monitor their progress with learned value functions, improving without resets or engineered rewards. The Stage Advantage component of Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}, developed by a co-author, is a first, supervised step in this direction. A comparison at matched robot and compute budget between aligning and scaling data would test the hypothesis that inconsistency rather than scale limits robustness.

\paragraph{Benchmarks for the long tail.} The benchmark-driven methodology of this thesis relies on benchmarks that expose the conditions that matter. Rare events are, by definition, scarce in recorded data. Generative simulation, pseudo-simulation with synthetic perturbations, and real-world challenges with standardised hardware are promising ways to measure robustness where it is needed most, provided that stress tests are selected independently of the methods they evaluate and are reported with confidence intervals. Such benchmarks would also keep the research cycle of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} turning.

\section{Closing Remarks}\label{sec:concl:closing}

The real world will always contain conditions that no dataset has recorded. The work in this thesis suggests that this is not a reason to give up on learned physical agents, but a reason to build them differently: to measure the shifts they will face before designing methods for them, to represent the world without closing it, to reason explicitly where data are silent, and to let agents correct themselves where training ends. This thesis has taken first steps on each of these paths, most of them in open-loop or non-reactive evaluation and one on real robots. The curse of reality cannot be lifted; whether it can be tamed in closed loop and at deployment is the question these steps leave open.
