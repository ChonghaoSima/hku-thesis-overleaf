\chapter{Research Methodology}\label{ch:methodology}

The four core chapters of this thesis study 3D occupancy perception, language-based reasoning, test-time adaptation of driving policies and robust robot manipulation. This chapter describes the research framework behind them. The framework was not designed in advance but distilled after the fact from the author's projects, including co-authored projects that the author did not lead. It is therefore a reflective synthesis and research agenda, not a contribution on a par with those of the core chapters, and it has not been tested on other projects. It makes the methodological choices of Chapters~\ref{ch:occnet}--\ref{ch:kai0} explicit and records what each of them actually tested (Table~\ref{tab:method:tests}), so that their claims can be read at their proper strength.

The chapter describes the research paradigm (Section~\ref{sec:method:paradigm}), the conceptual framework of the ``curse of reality'' (Section~\ref{sec:method:framework}), the research cycle, the lineage of the publications and the design principles distilled from them (Sections~\ref{sec:method:cycle}--\ref{sec:method:principles}), the evaluation methodology (Section~\ref{sec:method:evaluation}), a decision procedure (Section~\ref{sec:method:decision}), and the practices for reproducibility, ethics and validity (Sections~\ref{sec:method:practice} and~\ref{sec:method:validity}).

\section{Research Paradigm}\label{sec:method:paradigm}

Research on physical agents is, in large part, a design science~\cite{simon1996sciences,hevner2004design}. The objects of study are artefacts (representations, models, datasets and evaluation protocols), and knowledge is produced by building such artefacts and testing them against the environment they are meant to operate in. The research in this thesis adopts that paradigm and, within it, two commitments.

\paragraph{Commitment 1: benchmarks accelerate the research cycle.} A research question becomes tractable for a community only when it is measurable: when there is a task definition, a public dataset and a metric that many groups can use. The construction of benchmarks, and of public challenges around them, is therefore treated as a research contribution in its own right, not as a preliminary to the ``real'' work. Half of the twenty publications summarised in Table~\ref{tab:method:papers} (ten, by its ``Role in cycle'' column) introduce a dataset, a benchmark split, a diagnostic metric or a data platform, and an eleventh, \chiz, releases its training data.

\paragraph{Commitment 2: a good module must scale to real-world use.} A method that works only at a small scale, only with privileged information, or only at a computational cost that no deployed system can afford does not solve the problem it claims to solve. Methods are therefore judged on whether they can be trained on large data, whether they use inputs available at deployment, and whether their cost fits the deployment budget. The commitment constrained the core chapters unevenly. It motivated camera-only perception in Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}, with LiDAR used only to generate labels, a preference also studied in the co-authored VCD~\cite{huang2023vcd}. In Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} it favoured a general vision-language model with web-scale pre-training, but the resulting agent runs at 0.16 frames per second and sees one low-resolution front camera because its base model accepts one image, not because of a deployment constraint. In Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} it shaped the label-free test-time objective and the single gradient step, but not the sensors: the base planner uses LiDAR at test time, and the measured latency with test-time training (about 280 to 357\,ms per frame on one A100 GPU, against 244\,ms without) has not been related to on-vehicle control rates. In Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} it appears as the explicit post-training budget.

\section{Conceptual Framework: the Curse of Reality}\label{sec:method:framework}

\subsection{Formulation}\label{sec:method:formulation}

Let $s$ denote the state of the world (the scene and the agents in it), $o$ the observation produced by the sensors, and $a$ the action. Demonstrations for imitation learning are drawn from
\begin{equation}
  p_{\mathrm{train}}(o,a) \;=\; \sum_{s} p_{\mathrm{train}}(s)\; p_{\mathrm{train}}(o \mid s)\; \pi^{*}(a \mid o),
  \label{eq:method:train}
\end{equation}
where $\pi^{*}$ is the demonstrator. A learned policy $\pi$ is deployed in a world with its own state distribution $p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(s)$ and sensing model $p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(o \mid s)$. In closed loop, the states it visits follow its own state distribution $d_{\pi}$ rather than the demonstrator's $d_{\pi^{*}}$, and its actions are executed by hardware as $a_{\mathrm{exec}} \sim p(a_{\mathrm{exec}} \mid a)$. Only the state distribution, the sensing model and the demonstrator appear in Eq.~\eqref{eq:method:train}; the policy's own state distribution and the execution model enter only at deployment. The curse of reality is the observation that, for any finite training set, training and deployment differ in ways that matter: in the state distribution and sensing model of Eq.~\eqref{eq:method:train}, in the states that the policy itself visits, and in how its actions are executed. Rare states, individually improbable but collectively frequent, are among the most consequential~\cite{liu2024curse}: the long tail is never exhausted, and in closed loop small errors compound~\cite{ross2011dagger}.

\subsection{Four kinds of shift}\label{sec:method:shifts}

The four kinds of shift below name parts of this mismatch between training and deployment (Fig.~\ref{fig:method:shift}). Only two correspond to factors of Eq.~\eqref{eq:method:train}: perceptual shift to a change of the sensing model $p(o \mid s)$, and semantic shift to a change of the state distribution $p(s)$. Behavioural shift arises only in closed loop, when $d_{\pi}$ departs from $d_{\pi^{*}}$, and deployment shift concerns the execution model $p(a_{\mathrm{exec}} \mid a)$. Two further sources of failure are not shifts and are named separately: \emph{representational limitations}, when the state contains entities that a representation cannot express however well it is trained, and \emph{demonstrator inconsistency}, when the demonstrator $\pi^{*}$ is noisy or multimodal. The categories overlap, so the taxonomy is an analytical vocabulary, not a partition.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ch-methodology/figures/shift_taxonomy.pdf}
  \caption[The curse of reality decomposed into four kinds of shift]{The curse of reality decomposed into four kinds of shift, each naming one part of the mismatch between training and deployment (Section~\ref{sec:method:formulation}); only perceptual and semantic shift correspond to factors of Eq.~\eqref{eq:method:train}. For each kind, the figure lists symptoms at deployment that \emph{motivated} the research, the methodological lever that responds to them, and the core chapter that develops the lever. The symptoms are not the conditions tested in that chapter: Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} tests no distribution shift, including no sensor or weather change; the main shift result of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}, from nuScenes to Waymo, is a perceptual shift; and Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} evaluates on logged frames, where closed-loop covariate shift cannot arise. Table~\ref{tab:method:tests} lists what each chapter actually tests.}
  \label{fig:method:shift}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Perceptual shift: what is out there?} The sensing model $p(o \mid s)$ changes between training and deployment. A camera-based detector loses much of its accuracy from day to night~\cite{li2023bevdevils}, a fusion detector must survive the partial failure of a sensor~\cite{gao2023sdf}, and a driving policy may be deployed on a vehicle whose camera configuration differs from that of its training data. The zero-shot transfer from nuScenes to Waymo in Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} is a shift of this kind, and it is the main shift result of that chapter. A related but distinct problem is a representational limitation: irregularly shaped vehicles such as trucks, trailers and construction vehicles are poorly described by bounding boxes, and Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} shows that occupancy describes them better~\cite{sima2023occnet}. These vehicles belong to classes seen in training, so this is evidence about the expressiveness of the representation, not about robustness to shift.

\paragraph{Semantic shift: what does it mean?} The state distribution $p(s)$ changes: situations or compositions of agents that are rare or absent in training call for knowledge that the training labels do not contain. The CARLA split of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} with pedestrians absent from training is a shift of this kind: behaviour accuracy collapsed for both the reasoning agent and a driving-specific model, and recovered partly only with a question written with knowledge of the missing class (experiment led by co-authors). Measuring such generalisation is itself difficult: the follow-up audit DriveBench (co-authored by the author) showed that vision-language models can keep most of their score when the image is removed~\cite{xie2025vlms}.

\paragraph{Behavioural shift: what will my action cause?} In closed loop, the policy's own state distribution $d_{\pi}$ departs from the demonstrator's, and rare, safety-critical states arise that demonstrations seldom contain. No chapter of this thesis tests behavioural shift in this sense. NAVSIM v1, on which Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} is evaluated, scores each planned trajectory from the logged state while the other agents replay the logs, so the planner never visits states caused by its own actions. Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} studies a precursor instead: the uncertainty of per-frame decisions on logged states, including a split of rare, safety-critical scenario types (\texttt{navsafe}). Its comparisons with hand-designed safeguards cover only two of them: the cost-function optimisation of UniAD, which lowered its driving score from 83.4 to 75.2 with lower safety sub-scores as well as lower progress, and an uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories, which lowered the score of every planner to which it was applied. Neither speaks for scene-aware, rule-based safeguards in general.

\paragraph{Deployment shift: does training match use?} The way a policy is trained differs from the way it is used. Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} formalises this for robot manipulation as a mismatch between three distributions: human demonstrations $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, which correspond to $p_{\mathrm{train}}$ above; the learned policy $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$; and the trajectories actually executed on hardware, $P_{\mathrm{test}}$, the executed part of the deployment distribution. It identifies three resulting failure mechanisms (coverage deficiency, temporal mismatch and failure cascade), and it also treats the inconsistency of the demonstrations themselves, the factor $\pi^{*}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:method:train}, so in this thesis the category is broader than its name.

\subsection{Methodological levers}

Each kind of shift, and each related source of failure, calls for a different response, called here a methodological lever. Representational limitations, and in part perceptual shift, call for representations that do not presuppose a closed world; Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} develops this lever but does not test robustness to shift. Semantic shift calls for making the reasoning from perception to action explicit and checkable (Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}). Behavioural shift calls for letting the policy correct itself after deployment with signals it can compute without labels; Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} develops this mechanism but evaluates it on logged frames, so it tests the lever and not its effect on behavioural shift. Deployment shift and demonstrator inconsistency call for aligning what the policy learns with how it is executed, under an explicit resource budget (Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}). Two further levers cut across all four: data, studied in the co-authored AgiBot World~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros} and, as targeted recovery data, the largest single source of improvement in Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}; and measurement, without which no lever can be pulled or its effect verified.

\section{The Research Cycle}\label{sec:method:cycle}

Looking back over the author's publications, the research can be described as a recurring cycle of six stages (Fig.~\ref{fig:method:cycle}). The cycle is a description of how the work proceeded, reconstructed in hindsight; it is suggested, not prescribed, as a framework for similar research, and its limits are discussed at the end of this section.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ch-methodology/figures/research_cycle.pdf}
  \caption[The benchmark-driven research cycle]{The benchmark-driven research cycle reconstructed from the author's publications. Grey annotations give concrete artefacts at each stage; works marked $^{*}$ are co-authored rather than led by the author. The cycle closes when residual failures motivate the next round of formalisation. Stage~6 is reached only partly in this thesis (Table~\ref{tab:method:stages}).}
  \label{fig:method:cycle}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Stage 1: observe.} A project starts from a failure observed, or anticipated, in the field rather than from a gap in a leaderboard. Examples are obstacles that bounding boxes cannot describe (Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}), driving decisions that cannot be explained or that break under a change of sensors or scenes (Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}), rare safety-critical scenes (Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}), and robot policies that fail after hours of operation because demonstrations were inconsistent (Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}).

\paragraph{Stage 2: formalise.} The failure is turned into a task with a representation and a metric: semantic occupancy with voxel-level mIoU and flow (Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}); graph visual question answering with language, behaviour and motion metrics (Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}); the PDM score of NAVSIM together with a safety-critical split (Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}); and real-world task success and recovery (Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}). The formalisation fixes what counts as progress, so it deserves as much care as the method.

\paragraph{Stage 3: benchmark.} Data are collected or re-annotated at a scale that makes the task meaningful, usually by an automatic labelling pipeline, with human annotation or verification where the task requires it. Benchmarks built in this way include OpenLane~\cite{chen2022persformer} and OpenLane-V2~\cite{wang2023openlanev2} for lanes and road topology; OpenOcc, labelled by an automatic pipeline~\cite{sima2023occnet}, and OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene} for occupancy; DriveLM-nuScenes, largely written by human annotators, and the rule-generated DriveLM-CARLA~\cite{sima2024drivelm} for reasoning; the \texttt{navsafe} split, whose frames were assigned to categories by human annotators (Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}); and AgiBot World~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros} for manipulation.

\paragraph{Stage 4: baseline.} A simple, unified and scalable baseline is built on the benchmark. It shows that the task is solvable and gives the community a reference point. Deliberately simple baselines are one choice, for example the BLIP-2-based DriveLM-Agent of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}; strong unified ones are another, for example the co-authored BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer} and UniAD, which chained all driving sub-tasks towards planning~\cite{hu2023uniad}.

\paragraph{Stage 5: community.} Code, data and models are released, and public challenges are organised around the task. As stated in the statements of contribution of Chapters~\ref{ch:occnet} and~\ref{ch:drivelm}, the author was the lead organiser of the CVPR 2023 3D Occupancy Prediction Challenge and of the ``Driving with Language'' track at CVPR 2024, and contributed the OpenOcc v2 benchmark to the Occupancy and Flow track at CVPR 2024. The CVPR 2023 challenge used the Occ3D-nuScenes labels~\cite{tian2023occ3d} rather than OpenOcc, so it was built on the occupancy task rather than on the benchmark of Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}; the Occupancy and Flow track of CVPR 2024 used OpenOcc v2, a revised version of the benchmark of Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}, scored with RayIoU~\cite{liu2024sparseocc} and the mean absolute velocity error of the flow. The ``Driving with Language'' track was built on DriveLM-nuScenes and attracted 152 teams from 14 countries and regions with 978 submissions.\footnote{Challenge pages: \url{https://opendrivelab.com/challenge2023/} and \url{https://opendrivelab.com/challenge2024/}.} OpenScene data were used by the end-to-end driving and predictive world-model tracks at CVPR 2024 and by the NAVSIM track at CVPR 2025, and AgiBot World hosted its own challenge in 2025. A challenge is not an end in itself: it stress-tests the benchmark design, exposes shortcuts, and produces strong baselines faster than a single group could.

\paragraph{Stage 6: deploy and self-correct.} Where affordable, methods are evaluated closer to deployment and made to correct themselves under the shift they meet there. In this thesis the stage is reached only partly. Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} adapts the per-frame decisions of a planner at test time, but on logged frames in non-reactive simulation, which tests the mechanism rather than its behaviour in deployment; Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} aligns training with execution and evaluates on real robots, but its policy does not adapt at deployment; and the co-authored ETA splits computation between a slow, knowledgeable model and a fast, reactive one~\cite{hamdan2025eta}.

\paragraph{Closing the loop.} The residual failures found in later stages feed the next iteration. Several iterations are visible. OpenLane was extended by OpenLane-V2 from lane lines to road topology, whose ground truth in turn generated the traffic-element questions of DriveLM. OpenOcc and the occupancy challenges led to OpenScene, whose logs and sensor data, rather than its occupancy labels, supply the NAVSIM benchmark on which Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} is evaluated. DriveLM-Data and its challenge were audited by DriveBench~\cite{xie2025vlms}, which found that 78.6\% of DriveLM behaviour answers are ``Going Straight'' and that models can score without images, motivating more balanced data and more grounded metrics; FLARE subsequently explored activating pre-trained VLMs on NAVSIM via future-aware latent representations and reinforcement post-training without language generation at inference~\cite{xie2026flare}. In manipulation, the data-scaling platform AgiBot World was followed by \chiz, which asked whether robustness could instead be obtained under a small, explicit post-training budget, and by PerturBot, which diagnosed and mitigated modality shortcuts in vision-language-action policies~\cite{liu2026perturbot}.

\paragraph{Coverage of the cycle.} Table~\ref{tab:method:stages} records which stages each project actually reached. No single project completed the whole cycle: the perception and reasoning projects stop at the community stage and are evaluated open-loop, whereas the acting projects reach deployment-like evaluation only partly and have not been opened to a public challenge. The cycle is therefore best read as a description of a research programme spread over several projects, not of each project.

\paragraph{Evidence and limits.} Two kinds of evidence suggest that the benchmark-driven part of the cycle had an effect beyond the author's own projects: the uptake of its artefacts, such as the reuse of OpenScene data in NAVSIM, whose CVPR 2024 competition drew 143 teams~\cite{dauner2024navsim}; and the cycle's ability to correct itself, as when DriveBench exposed shortcuts in DriveLM-Data. This evidence is observational: there is no counterfactual in which the same problems were studied without public benchmarks. The cycle is also not the only productive way to do research on physical agents; hypothesis-driven, method-first and deployment-first work each have their place. It is least appropriate when an adequate benchmark already exists, when building a benchmark costs more than it is likely to return, or when the failure cannot be captured in recorded data at all.

\section{Design Principles}\label{sec:method:principles}

Five design principles recur across the methods in Table~\ref{tab:method:papers}. Each is stated with the evidence that supports and qualifies it and with a prediction that future work could test. Several are illustrated with co-authored work, which is not presented as a contribution of this thesis, and because the principles were distilled from the projects that illustrate them, those projects cannot count as tests of them.

\paragraph{DP1: represent the world without closing it.} Representations should not presuppose a fixed set of categories or shapes, as in the progression from 3D boxes to bird's-eye-view features~\cite{li2022bevformer}, from lane lines to topology graphs~\cite{wang2023openlanev2}, and from boxes to semantic occupancy~\cite{sima2023occnet}. The evidence that open geometry helps downstream is weak: planning on predicted occupancy gave slightly lower open-loop collision rates than planning on boxes predicted by OccNet's detection head (0.06--0.24 percentage points, single runs; not statistically established), and in NAVSIM an occupancy head slightly lowered the driving score of PARA-Drive (84.0 to 83.6; Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}). Dense 3D labels are also expensive, and occupancy opens geometry but not semantics. \emph{Prediction:} under a controlled change in object shape or category, a planner that consumes occupancy should degrade less than one that consumes boxes from a detector of comparable quality.

\paragraph{DP2: make reasoning explicit and checkable.} When a decision depends on knowledge that the training labels do not contain, the reasoning from perception to action should be exposed as intermediate steps that can be supervised and inspected, as in DriveLM~\cite{sima2024drivelm} and the co-authored Hint-AD~\cite{ding2024hint}. The evidence is mixed: most of the zero-shot gain of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} comes from the pre-trained backbone, in a confounded comparison; graph context improves speed decisions under shift but degrades open-loop performance in-domain; and language outputs can look competent without being grounded~\cite{xie2025vlms}. \emph{Prediction:} if the graph carries the decision, corrupting a parent answer should change the behaviour answer, and the benefit of graph context should grow with the distance between training and test conditions.

\paragraph{DP3: optimise for the final task.} Every module should be judged by its contribution to acting well, the organising principle of the co-authored UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad}, whose planning benefited only when motion forecasting and occupancy prediction were both present. The same principle appears in the planning experiments of Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} and in the behaviour and motion questions that end every DriveLM graph. \emph{Prediction:} intermediate outputs that improve their own metrics without improving the final task, such as the perception answers of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}, whose improvement had little effect on behaviour, should not improve robustness either.

\paragraph{DP4: close the loop at deployment.} Because training data can never cover the deployment distribution, a deployed system should keep correcting itself. On logged frames in non-reactive simulation, every test-time training variant of Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} improved its base planner by 0.4 to 2.3 PDMS (single runs), whereas the uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories lowered the score of every planner to which it was applied. Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} closes the loop on the training side, with recovery data and aligned timing, and the co-authored perspective of~\cite{chen2025robot} generalises the principle into self-directed learning. No adaptive policy in this thesis was run in closed loop, where repeated updates could drift or reinforce their own errors. \emph{Prediction:} the gain from test-time adaptation should grow with the distance from the training distribution, which could be tested with the synthetic perturbations of NAVSIM v2 pseudo-simulation~\cite{cao2025pseudo}, and should persist over many successive updates in closed loop.

\paragraph{DP5: respect the resource budget.} Methods must fit the data, compute and latency budget of deployment, as in efficient simulation~\cite{sima2021lshsmile}, the tenfold reduction of the nuPlan data volume in OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene}, the fast and slow split of the co-authored ETA (a closed-loop driving score of 69.53 at \SI{50}{\milli\second} per step, against 64.22 at \SI{931}{\milli\second} for the previous best~\cite{hamdan2025eta}), and the post-training budget of \chiz. That budget counts demonstration hours and GPUs but does not total the compute of all trained models or the robot and operator time of DAgger. The principle is qualified by AgiBot World, where more and better data did pay off~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros}. \emph{Prediction:} if alignment is the better use of a small budget, spending the same total robot and compute time on plain demonstrations should give a less robust policy than \chiz.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}\label{sec:method:evaluation}

\subsection{A ladder of evaluation fidelity}

Evaluating a physical agent involves a trade-off between fidelity to reality and cost, and four levels of fidelity are distinguished (Fig.~\ref{fig:method:ladder}). At level L0, models are evaluated open-loop on logged data with per-frame metrics such as mIoU, question-answering scores or displacement error; L0 is cheap and reproducible but blind to the policy's own mistakes, and for planning on nuScenes it can be matched by models that exploit the ego vehicle's own state~\cite{zhai2023rethinking,li2024ego}. At level L1, non-reactive simulation rolls a planned trajectory out from the logged state against logged agents and scores it with rules for collisions, drivable area, progress and comfort, as in NAVSIM~\cite{dauner2024navsim}; because every frame starts again from the logged state, errors cannot compound. At level L2, closed-loop simulation lets other agents react and errors compound, for example in CARLA~\cite{dosovitskiy2017carla}. At level L3, the policy runs on physical hardware and is judged by task success and recovery.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{ch-methodology/figures/evaluation_ladder.pdf}
  \caption[A ladder of evaluation fidelity]{A ladder of evaluation fidelity. Higher levels expose the policy more to the consequences of its own actions, at a higher cost per evaluation. The right-hand column lists where each level is used in this thesis; closed-loop simulation (L2) was used only to validate the rule-based expert that generates DriveLM-CARLA.}
  \label{fig:method:ladder}
\end{figure}

The guiding rule is to state the fidelity level of every claim and to evaluate each claim as high on the ladder as resources allow. Perception and reasoning claims (Chapters~\ref{ch:occnet} and~\ref{ch:drivelm}) are made at L0, driving-policy claims (Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}) at L1, and manipulation claims (Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}) at L3; no learned driving agent in this thesis was evaluated at L2 or on a real vehicle. The second half of the rule was not always met. For Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}, Appendix~\ref{app:drivelm} attributes the absence of closed-loop results to the 20 frames per second that CARLA requires, but in CARLA's synchronous mode the simulator waits for the agent, so a slow closed-loop evaluation at 0.16 frames per second was feasible in principle; it was not attempted. For Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}, NAVSIM v2 pseudo-simulation~\cite{cao2025pseudo} and closed-loop benchmarks such as Bench2Drive~\cite{jia2024bench2drive} offered higher fidelity before this thesis was submitted, and neither was attempted. The levels reported here are therefore the levels chosen for each project, not the highest that were possible.

\subsection{Testing for shift}\label{sec:method:testing}

Because the subject of this thesis is robustness under shift, in-distribution accuracy is necessary but not sufficient. Table~\ref{tab:method:tests}, the counterpart of the motivating symptoms of Fig.~\ref{fig:method:shift}, records what each core chapter actually tests. Of the four kinds of shift, the core chapters test perceptual shift (nuScenes to Waymo) and semantic shift (unseen pedestrians in CARLA), both in Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} and both at L0. Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} tests no distribution shift; Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} tests the tail of the logged distribution rather than behavioural shift; and Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} exercises closed-loop execution on real robots, with the same robots and scene as the demonstrations. Direct evidence that an agent copes with conditions absent from its training data therefore comes mainly from Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}, and compounding errors are exercised only in Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}.

\subsection{Metric validity and statistical practice}

Metrics are themselves artefacts that can fail. DriveBench showed that language-based driving metrics can reward answer templates rather than grounded reasoning~\cite{xie2025vlms}, which is why DriveLM reports behaviour and motion metrics alongside language metrics, and why the CVPR 2024 challenge combined a GPT-based score with language, matching and accuracy terms.

The statistical practice of the core chapters followed the conventions of the field at the time of each paper: most results are single training runs, as is common for large models, and no formal significance tests were performed. Variability is reported where it is available, for instance the three-seed mean of Centaur's leaderboard entry (92.10 $\pm$ 0.33). This falls short of the standard a thesis should meet. Following~\cite{agarwal2021precipice,kressgazit2024robot}, the thesis adopts the following protocol as its standard wherever it reports derived statistics, and as the practice for any new experiment: (i)~bootstrap confidence intervals over frames for per-frame metrics at L0 and L1, which require only the stored predictions; (ii)~paired tests or paired bootstrap intervals when methods are evaluated on the same frames; (iii)~Wilson intervals for success rates over a small number of real-robot trials; (iv)~several training seeds where training is cheap enough, each compared with a matched baseline; and (v)~awareness of multiple comparisons in ablation grids. In the chapters as written, claims that rest on single runs or small samples are marked as such, ``significant'' is reserved for tested differences, and derived statistics are added only where the thesis gives enough information to compute them transparently (for example the Wilson intervals of Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}). Recomputing intervals from the stored predictions of every chapter lies beyond the scope of the present text.

\subsection{Ethics and data governance}\label{sec:method:ethics}

No human subjects were recruited for any experiment in this thesis. People were, however, involved in producing the data. Annotators with driving experience wrote and checked question--answer pairs for DriveLM-nuScenes; their Chinese answers were translated into English through a vocabulary mapping and GPT-3.5, followed by manual checking (Appendix~\ref{app:drivelm}). Human annotators assigned the frames of \texttt{navsafe} to scenario categories (Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}), and teleoperators collected demonstrations and recovery data for \chiz. The DriveLM annotations and \chiz teleoperation demonstrations were produced by compensated annotators and team operators under the respective project protocols, while the OpenOcc labels were generated by an automatic pipeline (Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}). The datasets on which the benchmarks are built carry their own terms of use, several of which restrict commercial use (for example the non-commercial licence of the \chiz data), and derived benchmarks inherit these restrictions. Evaluation components that rely on commercial language models, such as the GPT-based score of DriveLM, are not fully reproducible, because the models change over time, and they incur costs for anyone who reproduces them. The driving data used here were collected by the providers of nuScenes, nuPlan and the Waymo Open Dataset, whose released data include privacy protections applied by those providers. The test-time adaptation studied in Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} was evaluated only on logged data in simulation; deploying online adaptation on public roads would raise safety and certification questions that are discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}. Because the work in this thesis consists of secondary analysis of existing datasets together with simulation and robot experiments without human research participants, it did not require separate review by the HKU Human Research Ethics Committee.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:method:validity}

Several threats qualify the conclusions of this thesis, and each is revisited where it applies.

\paragraph{Construct validity.} Benchmarks are proxies of reality, and metrics are proxies of what matters. Occupancy mIoU does not measure safety; language metrics may reward style over substance; and the PDM score of NAVSIM is computed from non-reactive rollouts that start from logged states. The last point matters most for Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}: because the planner never visits states caused by its own actions, non-reactive scoring cannot reveal the failure modes that test-time adaptation could cause in closed loop, such as growing conservatism, oscillation between driving modes, feedback between updates and the states they produce, or drift and forgetting over many updates. As formulated in Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ttt}, each update starts again from the trained parameters, so accumulated updates were not studied.

\paragraph{Internal validity.} Automatically generated labels contain errors, and a benchmark designed by the authors of a method may favour that method; OccNet, for example, is evaluated only on OpenOcc. Selection can also bias a stress test. \texttt{navsafe} was selected by sorting \texttt{navtest} clips on the scores of Centaur's own base planner, so any variant of that planner is expected to score higher on it by regression to the mean, and \texttt{navsafe} is a subset of the split on which Centaur was evaluated rather than a held-out set. No validation split for choosing the test-time hyper-parameters of Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} is described, and its buffer-length ablation is reported on \texttt{navtest}. Public release of benchmarks and challenges in which other teams compete on the same data are safeguards against some of these biases, but not against selection effects within one paper.

\paragraph{External validity.} Results obtained in simulation or on specific datasets may not transfer to other vehicles, cities, robots or tasks. The zero-shot evaluations of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} probe transfer directly; the \texttt{navsafe} evaluation of Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} probes the tail of the same distribution rather than transfer; and the real-robot evaluation of Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} removes the simulation gap for manipulation, though only for the tasks, robots and scene studied.

\paragraph{Conclusion validity.} Leaderboard comparisons are subject to random variation and to the ``benchmark lottery'' of which tasks and datasets are chosen~\cite{dehghani2021benchmark}. Most results in the core chapters are single runs, several evaluation sets are small (five \texttt{navsafe} categories contain 5 to 12 frames, and the real-robot results rest on 30 trials per configuration), and some reported values are approximate (such as the chart-read values in Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}); these cases are noted where they occur. Small margins may therefore not be meaningful (Section~\ref{sec:method:evaluation}).

\paragraph{Attribution.} Several experiments that carry answers to the research questions were led by co-authors: the detection pre-training, BEV segmentation and planning experiments of Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} and the CARLA experiments of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}. They are marked where they are reported, and Table~\ref{tab:method:tests} states who led each of the experiments it lists.

\paragraph{Hindsight.} The framework of this chapter was reconstructed after the research was done. Reconstructions tend to make a sequence of opportunistic decisions look more deliberate than it was, and the examples that fit a framework are easier to recall than those that do not. Table~\ref{tab:method:stages} records where the projects did not follow the cycle, and Table~\ref{tab:method:tests} where the chapters did not test the shift that motivated them, as partial guards against this bias.

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{sec:method:summary}

This chapter has described the research framework behind the thesis, reconstructed in hindsight and offered as a reflective synthesis rather than a tested contribution. The curse of reality was decomposed into perceptual, semantic, behavioural and deployment shift, of which only the first two correspond to factors of the training distribution, with representational limitations and demonstrator inconsistency as related sources of failure, each matched by a methodological lever. The research was described as a benchmark-driven cycle that no single project completed, together with five design principles, each with a testable prediction, a ladder of evaluation fidelity and a decision procedure. Table~\ref{tab:method:tests} records what the core chapters actually test: perceptual and semantic shift at L0 in Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}, no shift in Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}, the tail of logged driving data at L1 in Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}, and closed-loop execution on real robots in Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}. The next four chapters present the core contributions, starting with the perception end of the loop: how a physical agent should represent the 3D world it sees.
