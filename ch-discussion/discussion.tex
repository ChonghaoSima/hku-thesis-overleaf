\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}

The core chapters each answered a focused question with a specific method, benchmark and set of experiments. This chapter steps back and interprets their findings together, stating each at the fidelity at which it was obtained and with the qualifications recorded in Table~\ref{tab:method:tests}. Section~\ref{sec:disc:rq} answers the four research questions of Chapter~\ref{ch:intro}; the last of them, RQ4, is answered as a synthesis and research agenda whose elements are graded by the strength of their evidence. Section~\ref{sec:disc:method} reflects on the research framework of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}: what it delivered and where it fell short. Section~\ref{sec:disc:limits} states the limitations of the thesis, and Section~\ref{sec:disc:ethics} discusses its broader implications.

\section{Answering the Research Questions}\label{sec:disc:rq}

\subsection{RQ1: how should a physical agent represent the 3D world?}

Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} argued that a physical agent should describe its surroundings by 3D semantic occupancy rather than by lists of boxes. The evidence supports this in three respects, each with a qualification. First, occupancy describes shapes that boxes cannot: irregularly shaped vehicles are segmented better when supervised with occupancy than with voxelised boxes, and the gap widens as resolution increases (from 2.21 to 3.51 mIoU). These objects, however, belong to classes seen in training, so the evidence concerns shape, not novelty. Second, occupancy works as an interface to other tasks: the same representation improves semantic scene completion over the strongest baseline retrained on OpenOcc, supports LiDAR segmentation from cameras (60.46 mIoU, about 5 points below the LiDAR-based RangeNet++), serves as a pre-training target for detection when fine-tuning on small subsets of the training data, and, as the input of a planner, gave slightly lower open-loop collision rates. The planning evidence is the weakest of these. It comes from open-loop evaluation on nuScenes with the ST-P3 protocol, which can be exploited from the ego vehicle's own state~\cite{zhai2023rethinking,li2024ego}; planning on predicted occupancy gave slightly lower collision rates than planning on boxes predicted by OccNet's detection head (0.06--0.24 percentage points, single runs; not statistically established); the box input came from that auxiliary head rather than from a strong standalone detector; and the planning experiments were led by co-authors. The benefit also comes from occupancy as the planner's \emph{input}: features pre-trained on occupancy did not improve planning when fine-tuned. Third, the representation could be scaled: the OpenScene redistribution of nuPlan turned occupancy from a 5.5-hour academic benchmark into more than 120 hours of supervision, although the challenges that later used OpenScene drew on its logs and sensor data rather than on its occupancy labels, and no assessment of the quality of those labels has been published.

Evidence at a higher fidelity level does not show the planning benefit. In the NAVSIM comparison reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} (L1), adding an occupancy head to PARA-Drive slightly lowered its driving score (84.0 to 83.6), and the occupancy-based cost optimisation of UniAD lowered its score from 83.4 to 75.2, with every sub-score lower, safety as well as progress. These are different uses of occupancy from the one tested in Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}, an auxiliary training head and a hand-designed test-time cost rather than planning on predicted occupancy as input, so they do not contradict the open-loop result directly. They do show that a benefit of occupancy for planning is not established at L1, and that it depends on how occupancy is used.

The answer to RQ1 is therefore that occupancy makes the representation \emph{geometrically open}, in the sense that it can express the shape of whatever is in the scene, and \emph{queryable by downstream tasks}, and that it can be supervised at scale only through a deliberate investment in data. It does not make the representation semantically open: OpenOcc labels voxels with a fixed set of sixteen classes, so an unseen object is occupied space without a meaning. That occupancy makes planning safer is not established: the open-loop differences are small and untested, and the L1 evidence shows no benefit. Robustness of occupancy perception to distribution shift, for example across cities or sensor set-ups, was not evaluated in this thesis.

\subsection{RQ2: can structured reasoning with vision-language models help a driving agent generalise?}

Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} gave a qualified answer, and separating two effects is essential to reading it correctly. The first effect is that of the pre-trained vision-language backbone. In zero-shot transfer from nuScenes to the Waymo sensor configuration, the displacement error of the vision-language baseline was 2.78 without any question answering and 2.76 without reasoning context, against 4.16 for the driving-specific UniAD-Single; most of the transfer advantage over UniAD-Single therefore comes from the backbone. The comparison is confounded, however. UniAD-Single, trained on six cameras, loses its rear camera on Waymo, whereas the vision-language models use only the front camera in both datasets, and the two models differ about thirtyfold in size (3.9B against 132M parameters). The second effect is that of graph-structured reasoning, which is smaller: with graph context the displacement error fell further to 2.63, a gain matched by prediction-only context (2.62), and the gain specific to the full graph lay in the accuracy of speed decisions (from 43.90 to 54.29). In-domain on nuScenes, graph context degraded open-loop planning and behaviour accuracy: the variant without context predicted behaviour most accurately (61.45 against 57.49 with graph context), with the lowest displacement error (1.39 against 1.74) and collision rate (1.67 against 1.89). No majority-class baseline was reported for behaviour accuracy, although 78.6\% of DriveLM behaviour answers are ``Going Straight''~\cite{xie2025vlms}. On CARLA scenes with pedestrians unseen during training (experiments led by co-authors), behaviour accuracy collapsed (from 59.63 to 4.59) and recovered to 27.04 only when a question specifically about pedestrians, written with knowledge of the missing class, was added to the graph, at the cost of accuracy on regular scenes (from 59.63 to 52.17); the COCO and GQA co-training used in this experiment was not ablated. All of these are single runs.

The pattern is itself a finding, though a qualified one: in these experiments explicit reasoning helped under shift rather than in the average case, and it helped most when the reasoning was directed at the right entity, which in the pedestrian experiment was chosen by someone who knew the shift, an oracle-guided, injected human prior. It also exposes two costs. The first is latency: the reasoning agent ran at 0.16 frames per second, far too slow for control; the co-authored ETA later addressed this with a fast-and-slow design in which a large model reasons one step ahead while a small model reacts~\cite{hamdan2025eta}, and the co-authored FLARE bypassed language generation by distilling future-aware latent features and reinforcement post-training into a VLM~\cite{xie2026flare}. The second is reliability: DriveBench showed that vision-language models can retain most of their score with the image removed~\cite{xie2025vlms}, a shortcut phenomenon that the co-authored PerturBot likewise diagnosed and mitigated in vision-language-action manipulation policies~\cite{liu2026perturbot}. The answer to RQ2 is therefore that structured reasoning added measurable gains under a sensing shift on top of what a pre-trained backbone provides, mostly in speed decisions, and, with an oracle-guided question, on an unseen object class; in familiar situations it degraded open-loop performance, and it is not a substitute for broad pre-training. It is most likely to help when it is grounded, evaluated on balanced data and directed at the relevant entities, conditions that the experiments of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} met only partly.

\subsection{RQ3: how can a learned policy stay reliable under shift?}

For driving (RQ3a), Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} showed that a trajectory-scoring planner can adapt its per-frame decisions at test time without test-time rules or labels, on logged frames in non-reactive simulation. It did not show self-correction in closed loop: NAVSIM v1 restarts every frame from the logged state, so the planner's errors cannot compound, and behavioural shift and drift under repeated updates were not tested. Minimising Cluster Entropy with a single test-time gradient step raised the NAVSIM v1.0 driving score of Hydra-MDP from 90.3 to 92.6 in a single run, and the three-seed mean of 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 on NAVSIM v1.1 ranked first among three-seed entries at the time of submission, without a matched three-seed baseline. Three qualifications matter. First, the margin over the semantic-entropy variant of the same approach is small (0.23 PDMS over three seeds) and was not tested for significance. Second, every test-time training variant in the chapter improved its base planner by 0.4 to 2.3 PDMS (single runs), which suggests that much of the gain comes from minimising an uncertainty measure at test time in general rather than from the specific measure, possibly through a sharpening of score heads that were trained to predict the sub-scores of the metric itself. Third, the only safeguards compared were the uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories implemented in Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}, which lowered the score of every planner to which it was applied, and the cost-function optimisation of UniAD, which lowered every sub-score of UniAD rather than trading safety for progress. Within these limits, test-time adaptation improved the safety sub-scores with little loss of progress (ego progress changed from 86.5 to 85.9). It does not close the gap on the hardest cases. On \texttt{navsafe}, whose frames were selected with the help of the base planner's scores and are not held out, the adapted planner remains well below the logged human trajectories in every category; its frame-weighted mean PDMS is 68.9, against 49.4 for its base planner and 94.8 for the human trajectories (computed from Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navsafe}), and the gain over the base planner is inflated by the way the split was selected. It also scores below its base planner on yielding (59.3 against 71.6). Because Cluster Entropy groups candidate trajectories by lateral direction only, it cannot express uncertainty between proceeding and stopping in the same lane, which is a plausible but untested explanation of this regression.

For manipulation (RQ3b), Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} proposed that the problem lies not only in the amount of data or computation but in inconsistency between the distribution of demonstrations, the learned policy and what is executed on hardware. Addressing these inconsistencies with Model Arithmetic, Stage Advantage (a co-author's component) and Train--Deploy Alignment, on top of the heavily pre-trained $\pi_{0.5}$ policy and with about 20 hours of data per task, raised the success rate on the T-shirt folding task (Task~A) from about 27\% to about 97\% over 30 real-robot trials, with a roughly tenfold increase in throughput. These values are read from a bar chart (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple}). If they correspond to 8 and 29 successes out of 30, the Wilson 95\% intervals are about 14--44\% and 83--99\%, so the system-level difference is robust to sampling error even though the individual values are approximate. That inconsistency rather than scale is the main obstacle remains a hypothesis supported by this integrated result on one task: scale was not varied at a matched budget, the effects of the individual components differ from task to task (Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}), and the largest single gain came from targeted recovery data collected with DAgger. The co-authored AgiBot World reports, in contrast, that pre-training on more and verified robot data improves both in-distribution and out-of-distribution success~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros}; the same study found that its 528 verified trajectories alone outperformed all 1,010 trajectories including 482 unverified ones (0.59 against 0.41). Scale buys \emph{coverage} of the deployment distribution, whereas alignment buys \emph{consistency} between how a policy is trained and how it is used, but the two findings are not cleanly separable: the largest gain in \chiz came from adding the right data, which is itself a form of targeted data scaling.

The answer to RQ3 is therefore that, in the settings studied, reliability improved when the loop between training and deployment was closed: online, by adapting per-frame decisions on the planner's own uncertainty, on logged driving frames; or offline, by aligning data, model and execution, on real robots. The uncertainty-triggered fallback and UniAD's cost optimisation lowered the driving score in the experiments of Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}, but they do not represent rule-based safeguards in general. Alignment improved robustness at a modest post-training budget on one manipulation task, and the trade-off between alignment and further scaling was not tested.

\subsection{RQ4: synthesis and research agenda for self-correcting agents}

RQ4 asks what design lessons for self-correcting physical agents follow from the answers to RQ1--RQ3. The lessons can be assembled into the anatomy sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:disc:anatomy}. It is a synthesis, not a demonstrated system: each element is motivated by the findings of this thesis and by related work, but none has been evaluated as part of an integrated agent. \paragraph{An open, queryable representation.} Downstream modules should read the scene from a representation that does not presuppose which objects matter (Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}), and they should be trained towards the final task so that upstream errors can be absorbed downstream, as the co-authored UniAD demonstrated~\cite{hu2023uniad}. Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} shows the related capability of open geometry at L0, with closed semantics and a planning benefit that is small and untested. It does not show the function this element would have in a self-correcting agent, namely being queried again when a monitor doubts the current perception, so the element is graded as hypothesised.

\paragraph{Two speeds of decision.} Explicit reasoning helped under a sensing shift but is slow (Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}). An agent could therefore pair a fast policy, which acts on every step, with a slow reasoner that is consulted when the situation is unfamiliar or that thinks ahead asynchronously. This element rests mainly on co-authored work~\cite{hamdan2025eta}; no chapter of this thesis builds such a system, so it is hypothesised.

\paragraph{A competence monitor.} The agent needs a signal of its own reliability. Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} provides a label-free candidate: disagreement among the planner's behaviour modes, measured by Cluster Entropy. The evidence that it works as a monitor is weak. At the one threshold evaluated it flags 62.8\% of failures, but its accuracy (73.6\%) is below the 84.4\% of a trivial detector that never flags, because failures are rare, and no threshold-free measure such as AUROC was reported. Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} provides a different kind of signal, task progress by stage, which is supervised (it requires manually annotated stage labels), is a co-author's component, and is used during training rather than at deployment. A monitor that decides online when to adapt, when to reason slowly and when to gather more information remains hypothetical.

\paragraph{Adaptation at deployment.} The monitor must be connected to a mechanism that changes behaviour. Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} demonstrates one, a test-time update of the policy, in a limited setting: on logged frames in non-reactive simulation, where the effect of an adapted decision on the next state cannot be observed. Hand-designed fallbacks are the degenerate case of this mechanism, but the thesis compared learned adaptation only with an uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories and with the cost optimisation of UniAD. Whether learned adaptation outperforms scene-aware, rule-based safeguards, and whether it stays stable over many successive updates in closed loop, is untested. \chiz does not adapt at deployment; its robustness comes from training-side alignment.

\paragraph{A learning loop that outlives deployment.} Residual failures should flow back into data, into the alignment of training with execution, and into new benchmarks. At the level of the research community, this loop is the cycle of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}; at the level of a single agent, it would require the agent itself to collect and learn from its failures. The co-authored perspective of~\cite{chen2025robot} describes the agent-level version: a robot that identifies its own goals, acquires skills to reach them, and monitors progress with a learned value function. Neither Centaur nor \chiz reaches that point, since both depend on demonstrations, and \chiz on human-triggered recovery data; each implements a piece of it, so the evidence for this element is partial.

The answer to RQ4 is therefore a research agenda rather than a system: represent openly, decide at two speeds, monitor competence, adapt at deployment, and keep learning from residual failures. Only one element, adaptation at deployment, is demonstrated, and only in a limited setting: on logged frames in non-reactive simulation. The evidence for a competence monitor is weak, the learning loop is partially supported by training-side recovery data and alignment on real robots, and the open representation and the two speeds of decision remain hypotheses. The predictions attached to the design principles of Section~\ref{sec:method:principles} indicate how each element could be tested.

\section{Reflections on the Research Framework}\label{sec:disc:method}

The benchmark-driven cycle of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} delivered what it promised in one respect: it multiplied the effort spent on each problem beyond what one group could provide. The ``Driving with Language'' challenge attracted 152 teams, OpenScene became the data source of later benchmarks and challenges, and the lane, topology and occupancy benchmarks were each extended by a later project. Three lessons, however, qualify this success.

\paragraph{Benchmarks carry the biases of their construction.} DriveBench's audit of DriveLM-Data found strong answer imbalance and showed that models could score without looking at the image~\cite{xie2025vlms}; open-loop planning metrics on nuScenes were shown by others to be solvable from the ego vehicle's own state~\cite{zhai2023rethinking,li2024ego}. A benchmark built to expose one shift can hide another. In hindsight, balanced answer distributions, input-ablation baselines (``what does a model score without the image?''), majority-class baselines and dedicated shift splits should be part of a benchmark's first release rather than of a later audit. The \texttt{navsafe} split of Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} was an attempt to build the stress test in from the start, but it shows a further bias: it was selected by sorting on the scores of the base planner of the method it evaluates, and it is a subset of the test split rather than held out. A stress test fixed in advance, or selected with a planner outside the comparison, would have avoided this.

\paragraph{Fidelity must be matched to the claim, and stated.} Claims about perception and reasoning were made at the open-loop level, claims about driving policies in non-reactive simulation, and only claims about manipulation on real hardware. This matching was partly deliberate and partly a consequence of what was attempted: closed-loop evaluation of DriveLM-Agent in CARLA's synchronous mode and pseudo-simulation for Centaur were feasible but were not attempted (Section~\ref{sec:method:evaluation}). None of the driving results of this thesis has been validated in reactive closed loop or on a vehicle, and the one method whose purpose is self-correction at deployment was evaluated where its errors cannot compound. The ladder of Fig.~\ref{fig:method:ladder} is useful precisely because it makes this gap visible, and Table~\ref{tab:method:tests} states it for each chapter.

\paragraph{Scale and consistency are both resources.} The research began with a strong emphasis on scale: larger benchmarks, larger models, larger challenges. Its final chapter proposed that, for real-world manipulation, the consistency of data, model and execution matters as well as their amount. The evidence does not separate the two cleanly: the largest single gain in \chiz came from targeted recovery data collected with DAgger, and scale was not varied at a matched budget. A research framework for physical agents should therefore treat data quality and alignment as objects of measurement alongside dataset size, and compare them at matched cost.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:disc:limits}

The conclusions of this thesis are subject to the following limitations, in addition to the threats to validity of Section~\ref{sec:method:validity}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Domains.} The thesis studies two kinds of physical agents, road vehicles and bimanual manipulators, and within manipulation only three garment-handling tasks on two robot platforms. Other embodiments may face shifts not covered by the taxonomy of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation fidelity for driving.} No learned driving agent was evaluated in reactive closed loop or on a vehicle. DriveLM was evaluated open-loop, and Centaur on logged frames in the non-reactive simulation of NAVSIM, where errors cannot compound and repeated updates cannot drift. Higher-fidelity evaluation was feasible for both but was not attempted.
  \item \textbf{Shift coverage.} As Table~\ref{tab:method:tests} shows, Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} does not test distribution shift; the main shift result of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} is a sensing shift whose comparison is confounded by camera inputs and model scale; the unseen-object experiment of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} relies on a question written with knowledge of the shift and was led by co-authors; Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} tests the tail of the logged distribution rather than behavioural shift; and the real-robot evaluation of Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} uses the same robots and scene as the demonstrations.
  \item \textbf{Stress-test selection.} The \texttt{navsafe} split was selected with the scores of Centaur's base planner and is not held out, so gains of that planner's variants on it are inflated by regression to the mean, and five of its ten categories contain only 5 to 12 frames.
  \item \textbf{Baselines.} The comparison of learned adaptation with safeguards rests on an uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories and on the cost optimisation of UniAD; neither represents scene-aware, rule-based safeguards. The behaviour accuracies of Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} lack a majority-class baseline, and the box baseline of Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} is OccNet's own detection head.
  \item \textbf{Statistics.} Most results are single runs without significance tests, and several margins (for example between Cluster Entropy and semantic entropy) are within plausible run-to-run variation. The real-robot results of Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} were reported only as bar charts; the system-level comparison on Task~A is tabulated from values read from the chart, accurate to about one unit, and exact counts are needed to replace them. The statistical protocol of Section~\ref{sec:method:evaluation} has been applied only where the thesis gives enough information.
  \item \textbf{Semantic openness.} Occupancy removes the closed-world assumption from geometry but not from semantics, and the reasoning of DriveLM relies on a vision-language model whose knowledge and failure modes are inherited from its pre-training data.
  \item \textbf{Guarantees.} Test-time training and train--deploy alignment improve average behaviour and robustness, but they provide no formal safety guarantee. Entropy minimisation can only sharpen a prediction, so it cannot overturn a confident but wrong one, and an adaptation step can make the policy worse in some categories, as the yielding results on \texttt{navsafe} show.
  \item \textbf{Resources and dependence on pre-training.} Several results depend on large pre-trained models and on multi-GPU training, which limits their reproducibility for groups with fewer resources. The budget of Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} is a post-training budget on top of a heavily pre-trained policy, and its total compute and robot time are not accounted for.
\end{itemize}

\section{Broader Implications}\label{sec:disc:ethics}

Robust physical agents promise safer roads and useful robots, but they also concentrate responsibility in systems whose behaviour under rare conditions is hard to predict. Several implications of this thesis bear on that tension. First, explainability is not the same as reliability: an agent that explains its decisions in fluent language may still be answering from priors rather than from what it sees, so explanations should be audited as rigorously as actions. Second, self-correction at deployment changes the object that is certified: a policy that adapts online is no longer the policy that was tested. The regression of the adapted planner on yielding in \texttt{navsafe} is a reminder that such changes can affect other road users, such as pedestrians, in ways that aggregate scores hide. Regulation and testing practice will need to account for adaptive behaviour, for instance by bounding the size of test-time updates, by evaluating adapted policies per scenario category and in closed loop, or by monitoring competence signals once such signals are shown to be reliable. Third, the data behind these systems depend on human work: annotators who verify labels and write questions, and teleoperators who demonstrate and correct robot behaviour; their contribution, and their working conditions, deserve recognition in how such data are produced and credited. Finally, the benchmarks released in this thesis are derived from public datasets collected in a small number of cities; performance on them should not be taken as evidence of performance in regions or conditions they do not represent.
