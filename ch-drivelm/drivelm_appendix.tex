\section{Motivating Questions}\label{drivelm:app:questions}

Below we address several recurring questions prompted by Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}, such as the rationale for transferring general-purpose VLMs to autonomous driving. Because these topics remain active areas of inquiry, our responses reflect empirical observations and practical intuition rather than definitive conclusions.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q1.}~\emph{Under what conditions might VLM-based planning surpass traditional end-to-end driving pipelines?}

\noindent Handling rare, safety-critical long-tail events remains a central obstacle for self-driving systems. Because general VLMs are pre-trained on vast web corpora, they bring broad world knowledge and linguistic reasoning that could help when a traffic situation is unfamiliar in driving logs yet recognizable from general visual domains. Our findings in Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} offer only partial backing for this hypothesis: the VLM backbone adapted better than a specialist driving network when evaluated on an unseen camera rig (though differences in visual inputs and parameter counts confound that comparison), whereas zero-shot recognition of an unseen road-user category improved downstream decisions only after we explicitly inserted a question targeting that category.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q2.}~\emph{Why fine-tune general VLMs for driving instead of equipping specialist driving networks with language heads?}

\noindent Foundation VLMs draw on web-scale image--text pre-training covering billions of samples, and LoRA adaptation on a modest driving QA corpus such as DriveLM suffices to align them with traffic scenes. Specialist driving architectures, by contrast, are trained from scratch on comparatively small sensor logs, and retrofitting them with open-domain linguistic supervision is far from straightforward. Even so, hybrid designs that merge specialist geometric encoders with pre-trained VLM backbones remain a promising avenue.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q3.}~\emph{How informative is open-loop trajectory evaluation for planning?}

\noindent Its diagnostic value is restricted. When a planner receives past ego states in an open-loop benchmark, extrapolating kinematic history alone already yields small displacement errors, obscuring genuine scene understanding~\cite{li2024ego}. To mitigate this shortcut, DriveLM restricts evaluation to human-selected key frames that capture dense traffic interactions or impending manoeuvre changes (Section~\ref{drivelm:app:nus_collection}), and omits past ego states from the inputs of DriveLM-Agent. Even so, we did not run an ego-history-only baseline on those frames to verify that the shortcut is fully eliminated, and straight-line driving still dominates the behaviour labels of DriveLM-nuScenes (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:community}). The original UniAD model, whose BEV features encode ego status, is included solely as an anchor point. For closed-loop assessment, we provide the DriveLM-CARLA benchmark as a foundation for future simulator trials.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q4.}~\emph{What prevents reporting closed-loop CARLA benchmark scores at present?}

\noindent Compute budget, rather than technical incompatibility, is the bottleneck. Although CARLA steps at 20\,Hz, its synchronous execution mode pauses the world until the agent responds, removing hard real-time constraints. However, because DriveLM-Agent runs at 0.16 FPS (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:latency}), invoking the full model at every tick requires roughly 125\,s of wall-clock compute per second of simulated driving, making a full sweep across the 247.6 kilometres of official evaluation routes (Section~\ref{drivelm:app:carla_collection}) prohibitively costly. Model compression via distillation, weight quantisation, and KV-cache reuse could lower this overhead, as could running the upstream GVQA reasoning stages at a lower frequency while querying only the final motion head at every simulator tick.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q5.}~\emph{Can DriveLM-Agent meet the latency demands of on-vehicle deployment?}

\noindent Runtime measurements in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:latency} show that our unoptimised prototype runs roughly ten times slower than UniAD. Adopting the inference accelerations outlined under Q4 would be necessary before on-board deployment becomes practical.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q6.}~\emph{How should one weigh the high latency of VLMs against their generalisation benefits?}

\noindent Community-wide investment in LLM serving has yielded rapid speedups through techniques such as post-training quantisation~\cite{xiao2023smoothquant}, and dedicated token-pruning methods already accelerate BLIP-2 inference by 40\% without accuracy loss~\cite{shi2023crossget}. Historically, early prototypes in autonomous driving often prioritise capability over frame rate before engineering teams streamline them for deployment. While latency remains a clear limitation of our current pipeline, orthogonal advances in efficient foundation models should steadily narrow this gap.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q7.}~\emph{What makes VQA preferable to alternative pre-training paradigms (such as world-model video generation) when transferring web-scale models to driving?}

\noindent Safe driving demands both perceptual grounding and explicit causal reasoning under distribution shift. Because VLMs inherit chain-of-thought and compositional reasoning capabilities from their language backbones, formulating driving tasks as VQA directly taps into those strengths.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q8.}~\emph{Do current VLMs grasp physical traffic scenes as reliably as LLMs process purely textual domains?}

\noindent That question remains open and merits systematic investigation. What VLMs offer is a scalable, data-driven mechanism for out-of-distribution generalisation that has already proven effective across numerous vision--language benchmarks.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q9.}~\emph{Why does conditioning on the QA graph yield only modest gains on standard VQA metrics?}

\noindent Several factors likely limit the margin of improvement: our straightforward text-concatenation prompt, the moderate capacity of the base VLM, and imperfect causal coupling between parent and child questions in the annotated data. The synthetic DriveLM-CARLA benchmark offers a controlled testbed for isolating each factor and guiding future GVQA annotation protocols.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q10.}~\emph{How should one design the question inventory when building a driving QA dataset?}

\noindent Selecting informative questions is central to GVQA design. As an initial study, our work relies on domain heuristics rather than a formal selection theory; systematic question design remains an open problem. Ablations across individual question families are reported in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:question_wise}.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q11.}~\emph{Is out-of-distribution generalisation overstated as a problem for self-driving cars?}

\noindent Everyday traffic consists mostly of cars, pedestrians, and cyclists. Yet commercial deployment is governed by safety in the long tail: failing to react appropriately to rare hazards can cause severe collisions~\cite{2022incidentai}. Consequently, open-vocabulary detection and corner-case benchmarks have attracted sustained research effort~\cite{zareian2021openvocabulary,li2022coda}. Even though unusual objects account for a small fraction of driving mileage, resolving them remains one of the primary hurdles to wide-scale autonomy.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q12.}~\emph{Why rely on single-frame camera inputs rather than multi-frame video?}

\noindent Feeding temporal sequences to imitation-learned planners can induce causal confusion~\cite{wen2021keyframefocused}, a pitfall that led several strong baselines to condition on single frames~\cite{wu2022tcp,jaeger2023hidden}. Incorporating temporal context is nonetheless valuable over the longer term. As an initial test, Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:llama} evaluates a multi-frame variant built on LLaMA-Adapter V2~\cite{gao2023llamaadapterv2}, where temporal inputs yield a modest gain.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Q13.}~\emph{Where does the primary technical contribution of this study lie?}

\noindent Research at the intersection of language and driving previously lacked a common benchmark, task definition, and evaluation suite. Addressing that gap is the central contribution of our work: rather than proposing a complex network architecture, we deliberately pair our graph-structured task formulation and curated datasets with straightforward baseline models, demonstrating that even simple VLM adaptations benefit from structured driving QAs.

\section{DriveLM-nuScenes}\label{drivelm:app:nuscenes}

Below we describe the data schema, annotation pipeline, and statistical properties of DriveLM-nuScenes.

\subsection{Dataset Composition}\label{drivelm:app:nus_composition}

DriveLM-nuScenes contains 4,072 training key frames and 799 validation key frames. Every clip carries a scene-level summary of the ego vehicle's manoeuvre across the video, paired with frame-level QAs and 2D bounding boxes projected onto the six nuScenes camera views. Frame-level QAs are organised into three stages:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Perception} probes the visible state of the surrounding environment. Alongside a subset of human-written questions, rule-based templates query object attributes derived from nuScenes~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes} and OpenLane-V2~\cite{wang2023openlanev2} ground truth.
  \item \textbf{Prediction} asks how salient road users and the ego vehicle will evolve in the near future and why. Because forecasting interactions requires nuanced judgement, all prediction answers are \emph{written manually by human annotators}.
  \item \textbf{Planning} queries the ego vehicle's immediate decision and its underlying rationale. Given the complexity of interactive decision-making, we provide structured reasoning prompts and collect all planning answers \emph{through manual annotation}.
\end{itemize}
Salient traffic participants referenced in the QAs are denoted by \textbf{c tags} formatted as $\langle c, \mathit{CAM}, x, y\rangle$, where $c$ is the instance token, $\mathit{CAM}$ identifies the camera view containing the box centre, and $(x, y)$ gives the 2D centre coordinates within that image plane. Every key frame is accompanied by an object dictionary storing the 2D box dimensions, semantic class, motion status, and appearance description of each referenced instance. The overall schema is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:composition}.

\subsection{Collection Methodology}\label{drivelm:app:nus_collection}

Experienced drivers serve as annotators, inspecting panoramic strips stitched from the six surround-view nuScenes cameras. As depicted on the left of Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:nus_anno_pipeline}, labelling proceeds in three stages: sampling informative key frames from each clip, marking task-relevant road users on those frames, and authoring the frame-level QAs. Annotated batches then pass through multi-stage verification and linguistic post-processing, shown on the right of Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:nus_anno_pipeline}.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ch-drivelm/images/nus_pipeline_supp_v2.pdf}
  \caption[DriveLM-nuScenes annotation and auditing workflow]{\textbf{Left: three-stage human annotation workflow.} Annotators sequentially identify key frames, mark salient road users, and answer frame-level QAs for each clip. \textbf{Right: verification and translation pipeline.} Clips are grouped into 8-video batches for auditing and post-processing, yielding two release variants of DriveLM-nuScenes.}
  \label{drivelm:fig:nus_anno_pipeline}

\paragraph{Selecting key frames.}
Annotators scan each video clip to pick frames that capture dense traffic context or precede a potential manoeuvre change. Because selection relies on human judgement rather than an automated trigger for steering or speed changes, straight-ahead driving still accounts for a large share of the chosen frames (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:community}). During the same pass, annotators summarise the ego vehicle's overall action across the clip to form the clip-level description.

\paragraph{Selecting key objects.}
Within every selected key frame, annotators flag the road users and traffic control elements that influence the ego vehicle's decision, referred to as key objects. Pre-computed 2D projections of nuScenes ground-truth boxes~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes} are overlaid on the views to speed up selection, and annotators can draw new boxes for salient objects omitted from the official annotations.

\paragraph{Authoring QAs.}
The question bank combines rule-generated factual queries with manually answered open-ended queries. Factual answers are populated directly from ground-truth metadata, whereas open-ended questions are crafted beforehand and answered by human labellers. Most manual questions offer candidate choices alongside an ``Other (blank entry)'' field for custom responses, and an additional free-form slot lets annotators contribute bespoke question--answer pairs for the frame.

\paragraph{Auditing quality.}
Automated format checks run at every annotation stage, supplemented by human audits (exact rejection thresholds and pass rates are omitted here; see Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:data_nus}). Annotations are audited in work packets of 8 video clips containing the clip descriptions, chosen key frames, marked objects, and frame QAs. Auditors grade each packet against written guidelines: whenever manual labels fall short of the target quality, the entire 8-clip packet is returned with written feedback for re-annotation, while any inconsistent rule-generated QAs are corrected directly by the inspectors.

\paragraph{Translation and polishing.}
Because annotation was conducted in Chinese, all text is rendered into English in two steps: fixed terminology is first mapped via a bilingual glossary, and free-text segments are converted using GPT-3.5 before undergoing manual proofreading. A second release variant applies GPT-3.5 stylistic polishing to the verified English text using the prompt documented in~\cite{sima2024drivelm}.

\section{DriveLM-CARLA}\label{drivelm:app:carla}

We next describe the graph schema of DriveLM-CARLA, the privileged PDM-Lite expert used to drive the ego vehicle, and the simulator recording protocol.

\subsection{Dataset Composition}\label{drivelm:app:carla_composition}

DriveLM-CARLA pairs simulator recordings with rule-generated frame-level QAs connected as a directed graph (see Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:carla_graph}). The present release covers road topology, vehicles, traffic lights, and stop signs, while the generator can be extended to additional classes such as static obstacles, weather conditions, and regulatory signs.

Synthesising annotations inside CARLA removes human labelling cost and exposes multi-modal ground truth, including LiDAR point clouds, depth maps, and semantic segmentation masks, alongside multi-view RGB images. Within the QA graph, upstream perception queries establish situational facts that inform downstream prediction and planning nodes. Every object-centric QA stores a persistent simulator actor ID, enabling cross-frame tracking and temporal extensions, together with explicit parent and child edge pointers for graph traversal.

\subsection{Expert Algorithm: PDM-Lite}\label{drivelm:app:pdm}

Earlier privileged agents for CARLA, such as the rule-based planner in TransFuser++~\cite{jaeger2023hidden}, were engineered for Leaderboard 1.0 and cannot handle the expanded scenario suite of Leaderboard 2.0. To collect expert demonstrations across all 38 Leaderboard 2.0 scenarios, we rely on PDM-Lite, a privileged six-stage rule-based planner designed by Bei{\ss}wenger~\cite{beisswenger2024pdmlite} that couples path planning and actor forecasting with intelligent-driver-model speed selection and low-level control; complete algorithmic details appear in~\cite{beisswenger2024pdmlite}.

\paragraph{Results.}
Co-authors benchmarked PDM-Lite across the 20 official Leaderboard 2.0 validation routes under three random seeds (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:pdm_results}). Routes 10--19 repeat the geometry of routes 0--9 under altered weather presets, offering a second check on run-to-run variability. Because PDM-Lite reads privileged simulator state that is invariant to lighting and precipitation, score fluctuations stem from stochastic background traffic rather than weather changes. Per-route cells list the three-seed mean and standard deviation. Recomputing the bottom Avg.\ row from the 20 route rows confirms that its standard deviations are pooled across all 60 route--seed trials in every column, blending inter-route difficulty with inter-seed noise; across the 20 route means alone, the DS standard deviation is 23.9, whereas the seed-to-seed standard deviation of the 20-route mean cannot be recovered from the summary table (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:data_expert}).

\subsection{Collection Methodology}\label{drivelm:app:carla_collection}

\paragraph{Simulator settings.}
Recordings are collected in CARLA 0.9.14 under Leaderboard 2.0~\cite{dosovitskiy2017carla}, which expands environmental variety through two large maps and an updated scenario bank. We allocate Town 12 to training and hold out Town 13 for evaluation; each map spans $10\times10$ square kilometres across highway, urban, residential, and rural road networks. From the 90 official training routes (780.6 kilometres) and 20 evaluation routes (247.6 kilometres), each containing multiple scripted scenarios, we slice trajectories into chunks of roughly 150 metres and discard degenerate segments whose start and end points coincide. Because the default Leaderboard 2.0 traffic manager spawns only `car' actors, we augment background traffic with `trucks', `vans', `bicycles', and `motorcycles', and randomise weather presets per route while omitting night-time presets whose poor lighting prevents reliable visibility checks during automated labelling.

\paragraph{Data collection.}
Running the privileged expert across the segmented routes logs five sensor streams: (1) multi-view RGB images, (2) LiDAR point clouds, (3) perspective semantic segmentation masks, (4) depth maps, and (5) bird's-eye-view (BEV) semantic masks. DriveLM-Agent consumes only the RGB stream, whereas the remaining modalities supervise auxiliary heads when retraining TransFuser++. Alongside sensor frames, we query the simulator for privileged actor states:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Ego vehicle}: actor ID, 3D oriented box, forward speed, and braking flag.
  \item \emph{Other vehicles}: actor ID, 3D box, enclosed LiDAR point count, range to ego, speed, steer/throttle/brake commands, body colour, class label, wheel count, relevant traffic-light state, road and lane identifiers, junction-occupancy flag, distance to upcoming junction, and next route command.
  \item \emph{Pedestrians}: actor ID, 3D box, enclosed LiDAR point count, demographic attributes (age group and gender), range to ego, walking speed, and lane occupancy.
  \item \emph{Traffic lights}: 3D box, range to ego, signal phase, and whether the light governs the ego lane.
  \item \emph{Stop signs}: 3D box, range to ego, and ego-relevance flag.
  \item \emph{Static cars (parked cars)}: 3D box and occupied lane identifier.
  \item \emph{Landmarks (\eg, speed signs)}: actor ID, 3D box, range to ego, sign text, and numeric limit.
  \item \emph{Weather}: active simulator weather parameters.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Language labels.}
Simulator state dictionaries are converted into question--answer pairs through deterministic text templates. Although paraphrasing those templates with an LLM such as GPT-4 could broaden lexical variety and reduce template overfitting, all experiments reported here use the unaugmented template outputs directly.

\section{DriveLM-Metrics}\label{drivelm:app:metrics}

DriveLM-Metrics comprises three evaluation suites covering $P_{1-3}$ VQA responses, discrete behaviour classification, and continuous motion trajectories.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$P_{1-3}$}{P1-3} VQA Metrics}\label{drivelm:app:metrics_vqa}

Stage $P_{1-3}$ answers are scored with standard language-generation metrics alongside an LLM-based judge (GPT Score) that relaxes strict lexical matching, plus a frame-level Completeness score measuring the fraction of questions answered above a quality threshold. Every metric grades each QA pair in isolation, without conditioning on whether parent nodes in the graph were answered accurately.

\paragraph{BLEU.}
BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu} computes n-gram precision between a candidate response and reference texts; because it relies on exact surface n-gram overlap, it penalises valid paraphrases and word-order variations.

\paragraph{ROUGE-L.}
ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge} measures recall over the longest common word subsequence shared by the predicted answer and the ground-truth reference.

\paragraph{METEOR.}
METEOR~\cite{lavie-agarwal-2007-meteor} aligns candidate and reference unigrams using exact, stem, and synonym matches, combining unigram precision and recall with a fragmentation penalty that accounts for word order.

\paragraph{CIDEr.}
CIDEr~\cite{vedantam2015cider} represents each sentence via TF-IDF-weighted n-gram vectors across multiple n-gram lengths and scores candidate--reference agreement by average cosine similarity.

\paragraph{SPICE.}
SPICE~\cite{anderson2016spice} runs a probabilistic context-free grammar parser~\cite{jelinek1992basic} to build a syntactic dependency tree, converts that tree into a semantic scene graph of objects, attributes, and relations, and reports the F-score between predicted and reference scene graphs.

\paragraph{GPT Score.}
Because surface-overlap metrics often misjudge semantically equivalent answers, we also query GPT-3.5~\cite{ouyang2022training,chatgpt} to grade semantic agreement between the predicted response \texttt{\{Pred\}} and reference answer \texttt{\{GT\}} on a 100-point scale~\cite{sima2024drivelm}:
\begin{quote}\small\raggedright
\textit{System:} \texttt{An evaluator who rates my answer based on the correct answer.}\\[2pt]
\textit{User:} \texttt{Rate my answer based on the correct answer out of 100, with higher scores indicating that the answer is closer to the correct answer, and you should be accurate to single digits like 62, 78, 41, etc. This is the correct answer: \{GT\}. This is my answer: \{Pred\}.}
\end{quote}
We extract the integer grade from the model's response, where larger values denote closer agreement. Evaluation in Tables~\ref{drivelm:tab:vqa} and~\ref{drivelm:tab:vqa_more_metrics} used the \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo} API with the prompt above, which supplies the ground-truth and predicted answers without the question text or visual context, and without an explicit grading rubric; subsequent analysis in DriveBench noted that this setup produces tightly clustered scores (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:community}), and run-to-run scoring variance was not tracked.

\paragraph{Completeness.}
Completeness measures the proportion of questions within a frame whose predicted answers clear a quality bar: a response is marked correct whenever its SPICE score exceeds 0.5, and Completeness equals the resulting binary accuracy across all evaluated QAs.

\subsection{Behaviour Metrics}\label{drivelm:app:metrics_behavior}

Discrete behaviour outputs are scored by classification accuracy on the joint category $(B_{sp},B_{st})$ as well as on the marginal speed ($B_{sp}$) and steering ($B_{st}$) components. Ground-truth labels are quantised from the ego vehicle's future $(x,y)$ step displacements (Eq.~\eqref{drivelm:eq:xy_dist}, Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:task}) and compared directly against the discrete tokens predicted by DriveLM-Agent.

\subsection{Motion Metrics}\label{drivelm:app:metrics_motion}

Planned trajectories in stage $M$ are evaluated using the standard nuScenes and Waymo open-loop criteria: Average Displacement Error (ADE), Final Displacement Error (FDE), and trajectory collision rate.

\paragraph{ADE.}
ADE averages the Euclidean point-wise error between predicted and ground-truth ego positions across the 1\,s, 2\,s, and 3\,s horizons.

\paragraph{FDE.}
FDE measures the Euclidean distance between the predicted and ground-truth ego positions at the final 3\,s horizon.

\paragraph{Collision rate.}
The collision rate is the percentage of evaluation frames in which the planned ego footprint intersects any ground-truth obstacle box; Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch} reports the mean across the 1\,s, 2\,s, and 3\,s horizons (whether the rightmost Waymo column lists collision rate or FDE remains under verification, as noted below that table).

All three motion criteria follow the UniAD evaluation protocol~\cite{hu2023uniad} rather than the cumulative averaging convention of ST-P3~\cite{hu2022stp3}: specifically, the 3\,s FDE and collision figures reflect the instantaneous value at $t=3\text{\,s}$ rather than an average across $\{0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3\}\text{\,s}$ (see the UniAD codebase commit\footnote{\raggedright\url{https://github.com/OpenDriveLab/UniAD/commit/ffac1a69a6d01c9dca5cd2ed751007896df351f8}}). Furthermore, metrics published on the full 6,019-frame nuScenes validation split cannot be compared directly against figures on the 799-frame DriveLM-nuScenes validation split, which samples only human-selected key frames (Section~\ref{drivelm:app:nus_collection}).

\section{DriveLM-Agent}\label{drivelm:app:agent}

We next describe how DriveLM-Agent formats graph context during training and inference, and how continuous ego trajectories are mapped to discrete language tokens.

\subsection{Prompting with Context}\label{drivelm:app:agent_context}

Context conditioning follows a teacher-forcing regime~\cite{lamb2016professor,toomarian1992learning} during training and switches to autoregressive rollout at test time. At training time, every directed edge $e\in E$ within a frame prepends the ground-truth parent QA pair to the child question, while root nodes without incoming edges are trained without prefix context; all QA pairs are optimised under standard next-token cross-entropy. At inference time, the agent steps through the five graph stages sequentially ($P_1 \rightarrow P_2 \rightarrow P_3 \rightarrow B \rightarrow M$), feeding its own predicted answers from earlier stages as context prefixes for downstream child queries.

\subsection{Trajectory Tokenisation}\label{drivelm:app:agent_tokenisation}

Following RT-2~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2}, we cast continuous trajectory generation as discrete token prediction so that the same language head handles both VQA and motion planning.

Based on the empirical range of future ego displacements in nuScenes, we quantise each spatial axis $(x,y)$ into 256 uniform bins, balancing spatial resolution against vocabulary size.

Each spatial bin is assigned a dedicated token ID drawn from the existing numeric entries of the language model vocabulary, excluding single-digit numerals so that ordinary numbers in text responses remain unaffected. Wrapping the resulting 256 bin tokens between a start-of-trajectory (SOT) marker and an end-of-trajectory (EOT) marker allows the VLM to emit future waypoints as a standard token sequence, which is then de-quantised back into metric $(x,y)$ coordinates (Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:simple_pipeline}).

\section{Additional Experimental Details and Results}\label{drivelm:app:exp}

Below we document the hyperparameter settings for Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:experiments}, full VQA metric breakdowns, a stage-wise context ablation on Waymo, experiments with a second VLM backbone, and runtime notes.

\subsection{Implementation Details}\label{drivelm:app:impl}

\paragraph{LoRA fine-tuning setup.}
LoRA adaptation~\cite{hu2021lora} is run with a constant learning rate of 0.0001 (no scheduler), random seed 1234, and default LoRA rank and scaling settings. For BLIP-2, the maximum token sequence length is capped at 400 while retaining all other hyper-parameters from the official BLIP-2 codebase.

\paragraph{DriveLM-nuScenes protocol.}
The protocol below governs Sections~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_arch}, \ref{drivelm:sec:exp_question_wise}, and~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_vqa}. Training consumes every QA pair in each frame, prepending ground-truth parent QAs via teacher forcing~\cite{lamb2016professor,toomarian1992learning} for all non-root stages ($P_2$, $P_3$, $B$, and $M$). During validation, because the total count of $P_{1-3}$ queries per frame fluctuates widely across traffic scenes (with a cross-frame variance exceeding 260 in DriveLM-nuScenes), scoring every query would overweight crowded frames. We therefore evaluate GVQA metrics on a balanced subset sampled via stage-specific question templates, guaranteeing at least one matched query per stage in every validation frame. Root queries in $P_1$ run without context, whereas stages $P_2$ through $M$ condition on the model's own predicted answers from the preceding stage.

\paragraph{Waymo transfer protocol.}
For the cross-sensor transfer study in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_waymo}, we take the checkpoint trained on the DriveLM-nuScenes \texttt{train} split and synthesise test-time questions on Waymo via rule-based chaining. Specifically, inference starts by posing the generic perception queries from DriveLM-nuScenes; whenever a predicted perception response mentions a road-user category shared with DriveLM-nuScenes (for instance ``cars'', ``trucks'', or ``pedestrians''), rule templates instantiate follow-up prediction and planning queries for that category. Consequently, the downstream question set varies with each model's own stage-$P_1$ predictions (see Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_waymo}).

\paragraph{DriveLM-CARLA-ped protocol.}
In the unseen-category study of Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_carla}, we insert two pedestrian-focused nodes into the QA graph: first, \emph{Is there a person in the scene?}; second, conditioned on an affirmative answer, \emph{What should the vehicle do based on the pedestrian that is crossing the road?} (falling back to \emph{What should the ego vehicle do?} otherwise). The predicted answer to this second query is appended to the context prefix of the final behaviour node.

\section{Broader Impact}\label{drivelm:app:impact}

Connecting autonomous driving pipelines to general-purpose VLMs allows self-driving research to benefit directly from ongoing advances in multimodal foundation models. At the same time, deploying VLMs in safety-critical mobility inherits known risks, including language hallucinations and substantial compute and energy demands. Conversely, exposing intermediate reasoning in natural language makes driving decisions easier for human passengers and regulators to inspect, which may foster warranted trust and safer deployment over the long run.
