Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} equipped the autonomous agent with a geometry-first representation of surrounding occupancy. Knowing which voxels are occupied, however, does not specify how the vehicle should act. An effective planner must still determine which surrounding traffic participants are relevant, anticipate their future trajectories and select a safe response. Whenever an encounter departs from the training distribution, whether through an altered camera rig or an unfamiliar category of road user, lookup from memorised patterns breaks down and explicit multi-step reasoning becomes necessary. Structured reasoning therefore forms the second pillar of our response to the curse of reality.

Web-pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) offer a natural foundation for such reasoning. They bring broad commonsense priors from internet corpora, express intermediate judgements in human-readable text, and decompose complex decisions into sequential steps. Yet off-the-shelf VLMs lack physical grounding in vehicle control: standard pre-training never asks a model to map multi-view camera streams into continuous waypoints, while driving question-answering benchmarks available when this project started restricted themselves either to isolated scene-level explanations or to linear question chains targeting a single obstacle. Neither setup captures the relational structure of human driving cognition, which weighs multiple traffic participants in parallel and progresses from perception through prediction to planning. Addressing this limitation motivates our second research question:
\begin{quote}
    \textbf{RQ2 (Reason).} \emph{How can the general knowledge of vision-language models be grounded in driving in a structured and verifiable way that mirrors how humans reason from perception through prediction to planning, and does such reasoning help a driving agent generalise to unseen situations?}
\end{quote}

We investigate RQ2 by introducing DriveLM, a framework comprising a structured task, two datasets, an evaluation suite and a baseline model for language-guided driving. Our task formulation, Graph Visual Question Answering (GVQA), organises question-answer pairs across perception, prediction, planning, behaviour and motion into a directed acyclic graph whose edges represent both inter-object interactions and stage-to-stage dependencies. Two datasets, collectively termed DriveLM-Data, instantiate GVQA under complementary regimes: DriveLM-nuScenes provides 4,871 keyframes averaging 91.4 largely human-annotated QA pairs per frame, whereas DriveLM-CARLA supplies roughly 1.6 million rule-generated QA pairs harvested via PDM-Lite, a CARLA Leaderboard 2.0 expert planner created by Bei{\ss}wenger~\cite{beisswenger2024pdmlite}. Our baseline model, DriveLM-Agent, intentionally avoids complex architectural engineering, adapting BLIP-2 for trajectory generation by conditioning child questions on parent answers and discretising waypoints into language tokens. Empirical results clarify both the strengths and the failure modes of graph-structured prompting. Within the training domain on nuScenes, DriveLM-Agent matches or surpasses a single-frame adaptation of UniAD in open-loop trajectory error, yet feeding $P_{1-3}$ graph context hurts in-domain performance relative to predicting behaviour without context: average displacement error (ADE) worsens from 1.39 to 1.74, collision rate rises from 1.67 to 1.89, and behaviour classification accuracy drops from 61.45 to 57.49. Under cross-dataset transfer from nuScenes to Waymo, however, conditioning on the graph lifts speed classification accuracy from 43.90 to 54.29. Tested zero-shot on Waymo after nuScenes-only training, DriveLM-Agent achieves an ADE of 2.63 compared with 4.16 for single-frame UniAD; nevertheless, training the identical BLIP-2 backbone solely on waypoint tokens without VQA supervision already yields 2.78, and the baseline comparison carries structural asymmetries because UniAD-Single loses its rear camera view on Waymo whereas DriveLM-Agent consumes only the front view and contains roughly 30 times more parameters (3.955B versus 131.9M). In CARLA, when pedestrians are withheld from training, inserting a pedestrian-targeted prompt into the inference graph boosts behaviour accuracy on pedestrian encounters from 4.59 to 27.04 while reducing accuracy on standard traffic scenes from 59.63 to 52.17. Because crafting that prompt required prior awareness of the omitted category, this improvement reflects an oracle-injected human prior in experiments conducted by co-authors. Every reported figure stems from a single training seed. In the taxonomy of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, the nuScenes-to-Waymo evaluation probes a perceptual sensing shift, whereas the CARLA pedestrian evaluation probes a semantic shift.

\section{Motivation}\label{drivelm:sec:motivation}

Modern autonomous driving pipelines remain limited along two critical dimensions~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey,chib2023recent}: out-of-distribution generalisation to unfamiliar camera setups or rare road hazards, and transparent communication with human passengers or regulators~\cite{atakishiyev2023explaining}. Contemporary end-to-end planners predominantly operate over dense bird's-eye-view (BEV) feature grids~\cite{hu2023uniad,chitta2023transfuser,li2023bevdevils}. We posit that human drivers instead rely on an object-centric cognitive progression, denoted $P_{1-3}$: coarsely spotting salient traffic participants, forecasting their plausible manoeuvres, and synthesising those forecasts into an ego-vehicle action. While this perspective draws conceptual inspiration from object-centred theories of visual processing~\cite{Marr82} and core object cognition~\cite{spelke2007core}, neither cognitive theory addresses vehicle control, and the present chapter treats $P_{1-3}$ strictly as a task-design hypothesis rather than testing human psychology directly.

Pre-trained VLMs~\cite{li2023blip2,zhang2023llamaadapter,liu2023llava,wang2023visionllm} offer three properties suited to this object-centric paradigm. First, internet-scale pre-training endows them with broad world priors that have already aided zero-shot transfer in robotic manipulation~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2,driess2023palme}. Second, natural-language interfaces produce human-legible rationales, in contrast to opaque bounding-box or waypoint outputs~\cite{li2022bevformer,hu2022stp3,Renz2022CORL,dauner2023parting}. Third, autoregressive prompting supports multi-hop reasoning across chained sub-problems~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2,wei2022cot,wang2023selfconsistency,yao2023tree,besta2023got,ding2023everything}. Although a single VLM backbone can be fine-tuned across every stage of driving reasoning, passing discrete text strings between stages blocks end-to-end gradient backpropagation~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey}; furthermore, DriveLM-Agent trains intermediate stages under teacher forcing and isolates motion generation in separate adapter parameters (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:agent}).

Prior attempts to pair VLMs with driving relied on two simplified visual question answering (VQA) formats. Scene-level VQA pairs an overall ego manoeuvre with a brief justification, \eg, ``The car is moving into the right lane because it is safe to do so''~\cite{kim2018bddx,kim2019had}. Single-object VQA traces how the ego vehicle reacts to one selected hazard along a linear ``what--which--where--how--why'' sequence, \eg, ``The ego vehicle stops because there is a pedestrian in a white shirt crossing the intersection in front of the ego vehicle and it does not want to crash into the pedestrian''~\cite{sachdeva2023rank2tell,malla2023drama,qian2024nuscenesqa}. Neither formulation captures multi-object relational dependencies across sequential $P_{1-3}$ stages.

DriveLM therefore reformulates the task representation rather than inventing a new network backbone (Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:teaser}). Under GVQA, frame-level questions are connected in a directed acyclic graph whose terminal nodes specify ego behaviour and trajectory waypoints, turning graph traversal into trajectory planning. Two benchmark design principles follow from our focus on generalisation: pairing training splits with zero-shot test protocols across sensor layouts (nuScenes~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes} to Waymo~\cite{sun2020waymo}) and withheld object categories (unseen pedestrians in DriveLM-CARLA), and prioritising open datasets, metrics and evaluation servers over architectural complexity in accordance with the benchmark-driven methodology of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\section{Related Work}\label{drivelm:sec:related}

We review relevant literature published prior to or concurrently with DriveLM in 2024; broader background appears in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}, and post-2024 developments are examined in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:subsequent}.

\paragraph{Generalisation in autonomous driving.}
Rare long-tail traffic events remain a primary bottleneck for safe autonomous driving~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey,teng2023motion,tampuu2020survey}. Classical remedies expand training coverage via data augmentation or realistic simulation~\cite{suo2021trafficsim,chen2022learning,akhauri2020enhanced,wang2021advsim,hanselmann2022king}, as exemplified by TrafficSim~\cite{suo2021trafficsim} generating safety-critical traffic scenarios. More recent work incorporates semantic embeddings to spot out-of-distribution obstacles~\cite{elhafsi2023semantic,wang2023driveanywhere,chen2023categorical,pan2024vlp}. Because zero-shot planning under distribution shift remains fragile, this chapter investigates whether structured multi-stage VQA reasoning improves robustness.

\paragraph{Language-grounded driving.}
Numerous concurrent studies integrate large language models (LLMs) into driving pipelines~\cite{seff2023motionlm,mao2023gptdriver,chen2024drivingwithllms,jin2023adapt,xu2024drivegpt4,wayve2023lingo1,wang2023driveanywhere,marcu2024lingoqa,chen2023categorical,wang2023drivemlm,mao2023language,tian2024drivevlm,shao2024lmdrive,yuan2024ragdriver,pan2024vlp,han2024dmedriver}. Systems such as GPT-Driver~\cite{mao2023gptdriver} and Driving-with-LLMs~\cite{chen2024drivingwithllms} serialise detected scene attributes into text prompts so an LLM can output trajectory plans, whereas DriveGPT4~\cite{xu2024drivegpt4} maps visual inputs into token embeddings to predict vehicle control signals alongside textual rationales. Those concurrent works did not test zero-shot transfer across camera rigs or withheld object categories; DriveLM explicitly tests both regimes, revealing that pre-trained VLM weights account for most of the cross-sensor gain (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:experiments}).

\paragraph{Vision-language benchmarks for driving.}
Existing language-annotated driving corpora~\cite{zhou2023vision,Dewangan2023talk2bev,wu2023referkitti,wu2025nuprompt,qian2024nuscenesqa,kim2019had,kim2018bddx,sachdeva2023rank2tell,malla2023drama,marcu2024lingoqa,ding2024holistic} typically target isolated aspects of scene understanding. NuScenes-QA~\cite{qian2024nuscenesqa} and NuPrompt~\cite{wu2025nuprompt} query object attributes and spatial locations; BDD-X~\cite{kim2018bddx} supplies post-hoc action justifications; and DRAMA~\cite{malla2023drama} and Rank2Tell~\cite{sachdeva2023rank2tell} highlight single critical hazards paired with recommended responses. Because these datasets lack multi-object, multi-stage relational structure, DriveLM introduces dense graph-connected annotations spanning perception, prediction and planning (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:dataset}).

\paragraph{Embodied planning with LLMs.}
Foundation language models~\cite{touvron2023llama,floridi2020gpt,kojima2022large} have been widely adopted for high-level task decomposition in robotics~\cite{brohan2023can,huang2022inner,zitkovich2023rt2,driess2023palme,liang2023code,karamcheti2023voltron,rana2023sayplan}. PaLM-E~\cite{driess2023palme} fuses continuous sensor embeddings into an LLM for sequential manipulation; Code as Policies~\cite{liang2023code} synthesises executable control programs from language prompts; and RT-2~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2} discretises continuous end-effector actions into vocabulary tokens. We adapt RT-2's action tokenisation to vehicle waypoint prediction and test its transferability in autonomous driving.

\paragraph{Reasoning over graph structures.}
Inferring new conclusions from established premises~\cite{chen2020review} frequently benefits from explicit graph topologies~\cite{shi2019scenegraph,besta2023got,chen2019graph}. Explainable Neural Modules (XNMs)~\cite{shi2019scenegraph} perform visual reasoning over scene graphs, while Graph of Thoughts~\cite{besta2023got} organises intermediate LLM prompts into directed graphs. Drawing on these ideas, DriveLM structures driving QA pairs as a directed acyclic graph linking perception, prediction and planning, and Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:experiments} evaluates how conditioning on this graph affects in-domain and out-of-distribution driving.

\section{Graph Visual Question Answering}\label{drivelm:sec:gvqa}

Cognitive models of human driving partition vehicle control into sequential information-processing stages: spotting relevant road users, projecting their likely trajectories and interactions, and selecting a safe ego manoeuvre~\cite{macadam2003understanding,groeger2013understanding}. GVQA translates this progression into a structured visual question-answering proxy task. We first formalise the graph structure (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:task}) and then specify our stage-wise evaluation metrics (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:metrics}); dataset construction follows in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:data}.

\subsection{Task Formulation}\label{drivelm:sec:task}

For each camera frame, GVQA organises all question-answer (QA) pairs into a directed acyclic graph (DAG) $G=(V,E)$, allowing any question node to condition on multiple parent and ancestor answers. Each vertex $v=(q,a)\in V$ represents a question-answer pair grounded in one or more salient traffic objects, while each directed edge $e=(v_p,v_c)\in E\subseteq V\times V$ routes the answer of parent node $v_p$ as context to child node $v_c$. Dependencies arise along two axes: object-level edges capture cross-agent interactions (such as the pedestrian perception node conditioning the sedan planning node in Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:teaser}, centre), whereas task-level edges follow five sequential functional stages:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Perception} ($P_1$): spotting, describing and localising salient traffic participants in the current frame.
  \item \textbf{Prediction} ($P_2$): forecasting plausible future motions and inter-object interactions conditioned on $P_1$.
  \item \textbf{Planning} ($P_3$): enumerating safe candidate manoeuvres for the ego vehicle.
  \item \textbf{Behaviour} ($B$): classifying the high-level ego driving decision in natural language.
  \item \textbf{Motion} ($M$): generating continuous bird's-eye-view future trajectory waypoints.
\end{itemize}

While $P_{1-3}$ mirror standard modular autonomous driving stages~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey}, both behaviour $B$ and motion $M$ are derived directly from the ground-truth future ego trajectory. Specifically, $M=\{(x_0,y_0),(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_N,y_N)\}$ comprises $N$ bird's-eye-view waypoint offsets sampled at uniform time steps, with step-wise displacements
\begin{equation}
  \{x,y\}_{\text{dist}} = \{(\delta_{x,1},\delta_{y,1}),\ldots,(\delta_{x,N},\delta_{y,N})\},
  \label{drivelm:eq:xy_dist}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{x,i}=x_i-x_{i-1}$ and $\delta_{y,i}=y_i-y_{i-1}$ for $i=1,2,\ldots,N$. The discrete behaviour representation $B=(B_{sp},B_{st})$ bridges $P_{1-3}$ and continuous waypoints $M$ by quantising the mean longitudinal and lateral step displacements into five speed bins and five steering bins:
\begin{align*}
  B_{sp} &\in \{\texttt{fast}_2, \texttt{fast}_1, \texttt{moderate}, \texttt{slow}_1, \texttt{slow}_2\},\\
  B_{st} &\in \{\texttt{left}_2, \texttt{left}_1, \texttt{straight}, \texttt{right}_1, \texttt{right}_2\},
\end{align*}
where subscripts denote manoeuvre magnitude. Although this discretisation simply bins kinematic averages, the GVQA formalism accommodates richer semantic manoeuvre classes such as lane changes or overtaking.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}\label{drivelm:sec:metrics}

DriveLM-Metrics score motion $M$, behaviour $B$ and intermediate $P_{1-3}$ question answering independently. Motion accuracy is quantified using standard nuScenes and Waymo open-loop planning criteria following UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad}: average displacement error (\textbf{ADE}), final displacement error (\textbf{FDE}) and trajectory \textbf{collision rate}. Behaviour predictions are scored via joint \textbf{classification accuracy} alongside separate speed and steering accuracies. Intermediate $P_{1-3}$ answers are evaluated with two complementary metrics. First, \textbf{SPICE}~\cite{anderson2016spice} parses candidate and reference answers into scene-graph tuples of objects, attributes and relations to compute an F-score over propositional overlap rather than surface n-grams, subject to the coverage limits of its semantic parser. Second, the \textbf{GPT Score} prompts GPT-3.5~\cite{ouyang2022training,chatgpt} (using the template in Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:metrics_vqa}) with the reference and predicted answers, omitting the question text, to assign a numerical rating between 0 and 100. We elevated the GPT Score to primary $P_{1-3}$ metric status post hoc after observing that standard n-gram metrics responded inconsistently across context ablations (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:vqa_metrics}); moreover, the evaluation used an unversioned GPT-3.5 endpoint without multi-run variance tracking. Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:metrics} provides full metric equations, prompt templates, standard language metrics (BLEU, ROUGE-L, CIDEr) and our Completeness metric. Crucially, because all metrics evaluate nodes in isolation, none verifies whether child answers remain logically consistent with parent answers along graph edges, a limitation examined in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:discussion}.

\subsection{DriveLM-nuScenes}\label{drivelm:sec:data_nus}

Constructing DriveLM-nuScenes proceeds in three sequential phases: sampling informative keyframes from nuScenes video clips, tagging salient traffic objects within each keyframe, and authoring frame-level $P_{1-3}$ QA graphs anchored on those objects. While a subset of perception QAs is derived automatically from nuScenes~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes} and OpenLane-V2~\cite{wang2023openlanev2} annotations, the remaining perception items and all 299k prediction and planning QAs (out of 443k total QAs in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:dataset}) are written by human annotators. Five autonomous-driving specialists designed the question templates to mirror human decision stages; annotators review the full template bank per frame and may skip inapplicable prompts.

Because manual annotation dominates DriveLM-nuScenes, we enforce batch-level quality control throughout labelling (Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:data_collection}): annotators submit work in batches, inspectors audit a 10\% random sample per batch, and any batch falling below the target pass rate is returned for complete relabelling before undergoing manual grammatical cleanup and GPT-3.5 linguistic refinement (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:nus_collection}). Exact batch rejection thresholds, relabelled batch fractions, and formal inter-annotator agreement metrics were not logged separately during production. The completed dataset comprises 4,072 training keyframes and 799 validation keyframes, averaging 91.4 graph-linked QAs per frame compared with at most 15.3 in prior benchmarks (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:dataset}). Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:nuscenes} documents the full annotation protocol and statistical breakdown.

\subsection{PDM-Lite: An Expert Driver for CARLA Leaderboard 2.0}\label{drivelm:sec:data_expert}

We generate DriveLM-CARLA inside CARLA 0.9.14 under the Leaderboard 2.0 evaluation protocol~\cite{dosovitskiy2017carla}. Leaderboard 2.0 introduces substantially harder long-horizon traffic scenarios than Leaderboard 1.0, causing legacy privileged experts such as the TransFuser++ rule-based planner~\cite{jaeger2023hidden} to drop to a 2\% Driving Score (DS) on the official 8+ km validation routes. To enable scalable data collection, co-author Jens Bei{\ss}wenger developed PDM-Lite~\cite{beisswenger2024pdmlite}, the first working rule-based expert planner for CARLA Leaderboard 2.0. Adapting ideas from the nuPlan expert PDM-Closed~\cite{dauner2023parting,caesar2021nuplan}, PDM-Lite computes target longitudinal speeds via the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM)~\cite{treiber2000idm} conditioned on leading vehicles, pedestrians, stop signs and traffic lights, while replacing PDM-Closed's 16-trajectory rollouts with two lateral path candidates scored by a lightweight cost formulation adapted from TransFuser++~\cite{jaeger2023hidden}. Across the 20 official validation routes evaluated over three random seeds each (60 route-seed runs conducted by co-authors), PDM-Lite achieves a route-pooled mean DS of 36.3\,$\pm$\,28.1 (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:pdm_results}), whereas the main text of~\cite{sima2024drivelm} cited an approximate figure of 44\% from an initial subset evaluation. The $\pm$\,28.1 dispersion reflects all 60 runs combined; across the 20 route means the standard deviation is 23.9, with within-route three-seed standard deviations spanning 0.0 to 37.4 (individual seed-level 20-route averages were not archived separately). Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:pdm} details PDM-Lite's architecture and per-route scores.

\subsection{DriveLM-CARLA}\label{drivelm:sec:data_carla}

To assemble DriveLM-CARLA, PDM-Lite drives predefined routes spanning urban, residential and rural maps at 4 FPS while logging sensor streams, simulator state and active expert rules (Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:data_collection}). Keyframes are triggered whenever the expert's discrete longitudinal or lateral decision changes, such as transitioning from cruising to braking. Privileged simulator states for static and dynamic actors, together with the expert's internal rule activations across 35 of the 38 Leaderboard 2.0 scenario types (omitting only \mbox{\emph{InterurbanAdvancedActorFlow}}, \mbox{\emph{MergerIntoSlowTraffic}} and \mbox{\emph{VehicleTurningRoute}}), populate compositional sentence templates that automatically wire perception, prediction, planning, behaviour and motion QAs into a DAG (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:carla}). Because data generation requires specifying only route coordinates and scenario configurations before running unattended, this pipeline yields 1.6M QA pairs, making DriveLM-CARLA the largest driving-language corpus in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:dataset} by total question-answer volume.

\section{DriveLM-Agent: A GVQA Baseline}\label{drivelm:sec:agent}

DriveLM-Agent serves as a reference GVQA baseline that leverages internet-scale vision-language pre-training to map a camera image through intermediate stages $P_1$, $P_2$, $P_3$ and $B$ into future ego waypoints $M$. We instantiate the model on BLIP-2~\cite{li2023blip2} for its modular Q-Former interface and parameter-efficient fine-tuning, though any vision-language backbone can substitute. Architecturally, DriveLM-Agent combines standard BLIP-2 visual-textual prompting with RT-2 waypoint tokenisation~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2} and text-based parent-to-child context propagation.

As depicted in Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:model_pipeline}, inference proceeds through three functional tiers: (1) the $P_{1-3}$ stages parse salient scene objects, forecast their trajectories and identify safe ego responses; (2) the behaviour stage $B$ condenses the $P_{1-3}$ subgraph into a discrete natural-language manoeuvre command; and (3) the motion stage $M$ converts the image and predicted behaviour command into continuous trajectory coordinates. Directed edges in the GVQA graph are realised via textual context injection, detailed below.

\subsection{Prompting with Context}\label{drivelm:sec:context}

Mapping raw camera pixels directly to waypoint coordinates~\cite{Prakash2021transfuser,chitta2021neat} provides neither intermediate interpretability nor explicit subtask decomposition. To expose intermediate reasoning steps in human-readable form, DriveLM-Agent conditions each question node $v_c$ on the answers of its parent nodes: for every incoming edge $e=(v_p,v_c)\in E$, the parent question-answer pair $v_p$ is prepended to the prompt of $v_c$ under a ``\emph{Context:}'' header, concatenating multiple parents sequentially when $|\{v_p:(v_p,v_c)\in E\}|>1$. Training uses ground-truth parent answers (teacher forcing), whereas test-time inference executes stages autoregressively in topological order ($P_1 \rightarrow P_2 \rightarrow P_3 \rightarrow B \rightarrow M$) using the model's own predicted parent answers (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:agent}). While full training uses all annotated QAs per frame, test-time inference can evaluate compact heuristically sampled subgraphs to match computational budgets (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:impl}).

\paragraph{Context aggregation through behaviour.}
Although traffic scenes vary widely in visual appearance, diverse hazards such as red traffic lights, stop signs or decelerating lead vehicles frequently map to the same discrete manoeuvre class. The behaviour node $B$ acts as an information bottleneck that summarises upstream $P_{1-3}$ reasoning into a compact natural-language manoeuvre description, receiving all sampled $P_{1-3}$ QAs as its parent context. Introducing this intermediate behaviour bottleneck proves crucial on nuScenes: predicting $B$ before $M$ without $P_{1-3}$ context cuts open-loop ADE from 2.63 (direct waypoint prediction via BLIP-RT-2) to 1.39 (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch}), whereas conditioning $B$ on $P_{1-3}$ graph context improves trajectory metrics only under cross-sensor transfer to Waymo (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:experiments}).

\subsection{Trajectory Tokenisation for Motion}\label{drivelm:sec:traj_token}

Because autoregressive language models struggle to regress continuous floating-point coordinates directly, we follow RT-2~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2} in discretising longitudinal and lateral waypoint offsets into 256 uniform bins calibrated to the empirical training-set range. We repurpose 256 tokens from the BLIP-2 vocabulary to represent these spatial bins and fine-tune the model to output waypoint token sequences conditioned on the camera image and the predicted behaviour string $B$ (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:agent}). To keep the baseline modular, the motion stage shares the BLIP-2 backbone architecture but trains an independent set of LoRA adapter weights exclusively on motion-stage QAs; in principle, any lightweight language model~\cite{radford2019language} or command-conditioned trajectory head~\cite{hu2022stp3,wu2022tcp} could fulfil this role.

\section{Experiments}\label{drivelm:sec:experiments}

Our empirical study investigates five questions: (1) how effectively a general VLM can be adapted for open-loop end-to-end trajectory planning; (2) whether VLM-based planners transfer zero-shot across distinct camera configurations; (3) how models respond to an object category withheld during training; (4) how individual perception, prediction and planning question types affect behaviour classification; and (5) how context conditioning affects $P_{1-3}$ question-answering quality. Supplementary ablations across alternative VLM backbones appear in Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:exp}.

\paragraph{Setup.}
Every model variant is fine-tuned via LoRA~\cite{hu2021lora}. On DriveLM-nuScenes, BLIP-2 is trained on the \texttt{train} split for 10 epochs at batch size 2 per GPU, requiring roughly 7 hours across 8 V100 GPUs. On DriveLM-CARLA, BLIP-2 is trained on the keyframe \texttt{train} split for 6 epochs in approximately 6 hours across 4 A100 GPUs (further hyperparameters in Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:impl}).

\paragraph{Evaluation sets and uncertainty.}
In-domain evaluations use the 799 keyframes of DriveLM-nuScenes \texttt{val}; cross-sensor transfer uses 1,000 frames sampled from Waymo \texttt{val}; and CARLA evaluations use the DriveLM-CARLA \texttt{val} split alongside the pedestrian-only DriveLM-CARLA-ped split (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_carla}; in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:carla}, the regular-scene percentages match a denominator of 161 validation frames to within 0.01, while exact post-filtering QA denominators and per-split class histograms were not archived separately in the public release). Because every model configuration was trained once with random seed 1234 (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:impl}), multi-seed training variance and paired frame-level significance tests are unavailable. To indicate binomial sampling precision alone, a 60\% classification accuracy over 799 independent frames carries a 95\% Wilson confidence interval of roughly $\pm$3.4 percentage points, while a 50\% accuracy over 1,000 Waymo frames carries roughly $\pm$3.1 percentage points.

\subsection{VLMs for End-to-End Driving}\label{drivelm:sec:exp_arch}

We first test open-loop trajectory planning on DriveLM-nuScenes, isolating how intermediate behaviour prediction and $P_{1-3}$ context conditioning affect waypoint accuracy. Prior work has shown that open-loop nuScenes metrics can be dominated by extrapolating ego-vehicle kinematic history from the CAN bus~\cite{li2024ego}. To mitigate this shortcut, DriveLM-Agent omits CAN-bus ego status inputs and evaluates exclusively on the 799 manually selected DriveLM-nuScenes keyframes, which target complex scenes and potential manoeuvre transitions (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:nus_collection}), though we did not run an ego-status-only baseline on these keyframes to quantify how much of the shortcut remains.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
To anchor task difficulty, \textbf{Command Mean} averages all training-split trajectories sharing the test frame's high-level navigation command (`turn left', `turn right' or `go straight'). We also compare against UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad}, the leading modular end-to-end planner on nuScenes at the time of publication. Because standard UniAD ingests multi-frame surround-view video and CAN-bus ego status~\cite{li2024ego}, we train a single-frame adaptation (\textbf{UniAD-Single}) using the official UniAD codebase to match DriveLM-Agent's single-timestep visual regime, while reporting the full multi-frame UniAD model on the same validation split in italics for reference. Finally, \textbf{BLIP-RT-2} fine-tunes BLIP-2~\cite{li2023blip2} directly on waypoint tokens (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:traj_token}) without intermediate behaviour prediction or $P_{1-3}$ VQA supervision.

\paragraph{DriveLM-Agent variants.}
We compare three context-conditioning configurations that progressively incorporate the GVQA graph: (1) `\textbf{None}', a two-stage model that predicts $B$ directly from the image without $P_{1-3}$ context before predicting $M$ conditioned on $B$; (2) `\textbf{Chain}', which executes the $P_{1-3}$ graph but forwards only the final planning answer ($P_3$) as context to $B$; and (3) `\textbf{Graph}', the full configuration that concatenates all sampled $P_{1-3}$ QA pairs as context for $B$.

\subsection{Generalisation Across Sensor Configurations}\label{drivelm:sec:exp_waymo}

To probe robustness under a perceptual sensor shift, we evaluate the nuScenes-trained models of Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_arch} zero-shot on 1,000 validation frames from the Waymo Open Dataset~\cite{sun2020waymo}. Because Waymo omits a rear-facing camera, UniAD-Single must zero out its rear-view input channel, whereas the front-camera-only VLM variants transfer without input modification. Lacking human GVQA annotations on Waymo, test-time prompts are generated via rules (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:impl}).

This cross-dataset test involves clear structural asymmetries. Whereas the front-camera-only VLMs keep their exact training sensor layout, the surround-view UniAD-Single model must operate without its nuScenes rear camera. Parameter counts also differ by roughly a factor of 30 (3.955B versus 131.9M in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:latency}), and no baseline disentangles vision-language pre-training through either a front-view-only driving network or a randomly initialised BLIP-2 model. Moreover, because each model's own perception outputs determine which Waymo prediction and planning prompts are instantiated, the resulting prompt distributions differ from DriveLM-nuScenes and can shift across context settings, confounding the context ablation. All methods in Tables~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch} and~\ref{drivelm:tab:waymo_supp} are evaluated on the same 1,000 sampled Waymo \texttt{val} frames, although the random sampling seed and the exact counts of frames receiving rule-generated prediction and planning prompts were not archived.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch}(b) shows that altering the camera rig degrades UniAD-Single below BLIP-RT-2. Consequently, the bulk of the improvement over UniAD-Single accompanies the pre-trained vision-language backbone rather than graph reasoning: UniAD-Single records an ADE of 4.16, whereas BLIP-RT-2 (trained without VQA supervision or context) reaches 2.78 and context-free DriveLM-Agent reaches 2.76. Given the sensor mismatch and parameter gap noted above, even this backbone gap cannot be ascribed purely to vision-language pre-training. Multi-stage prompting in DriveLM-Agent yields a smaller additional reduction: full-graph context brings ADE down to 2.63 and prediction-only context reaches 2.62 (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:waymo_supp}), indicating that the trajectory improvement does not depend on the complete graph. What requires the full graph is speed classification accuracy, which climbs from 43.90 without context to 54.29 under full-graph prompting, exceeding three times the half-width of the sampling interval reported in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:experiments}. By contrast, the chain variant fails to propagate adequate context and attains only 41.28 in speed accuracy. Stage-wise context ablations in Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:waymo_ablation} likewise indicate that improved behaviour accuracy generally tracks lower ADE. Every result in this section reflects a single training run.

Representative DriveLM-Agent predictions on nuScenes and Waymo appear in Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:qualitative}. Apart from occasional errors (\eg, nuScenes planning or Waymo perception), the responses align with human intuition and allow direct inspection of intermediate GVQA reasoning steps. On Waymo, downstream prediction and planning outputs remain sensible even when upstream perception is imperfect; additional qualitative examples appear in the published paper~\cite{sima2024drivelm}.

\subsection{Generalisation to Unseen Objects}\label{drivelm:sec:exp_carla}

We next examine zero-shot transfer to an object category withheld during training, in experiments led by co-authors (see the statement of contribution). When constructing the training and validation splits of DriveLM-CARLA from the 35 Leaderboard 2.0 scenarios, all frames containing pedestrians were filtered out so that pedestrians never appear during training. A separate evaluation split, DriveLM-CARLA-ped, was then assembled exclusively from frames in which a pedestrian crosses ahead of the ego vehicle, where the required action is always to brake to a stop. Because every frame in DriveLM-CARLA-ped shares this identical target action (stopping with straight steering, as discussed below), a trivial classifier that unconditionally outputs a stop command would achieve a score of 100 on DriveLM-CARLA-ped. Performance on this split therefore reflects only stopping recall and must be interpreted alongside accuracy on standard pedestrian-free scenes, where a constant stopping policy would fail; such a constant predictor was not separately evaluated.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
DriveLM-Agent is benchmarked against TransFuser++~\cite{jaeger2023hidden}, a leading CARLA planner at the time of the study. Relative to DriveLM-Agent, TransFuser++ ingests higher-resolution camera images alongside LiDAR point clouds and requires dense driving supervision (depth maps, semantic segmentation, 3D bounding boxes and HD map labels). Those specialised sensor inputs and supervision targets tie TransFuser++ to the base DriveLM-CARLA split and prevent joint training with general-purpose vision datasets, limiting its exposure to unseen object categories.

\paragraph{DriveLM-Agent variants.}
Because a VLM uses a task-agnostic text interface, DriveLM-Agent can be co-trained on DriveLM-CARLA alongside general vision-language samples from COCO~\cite{lin2014microsoft} and GQA~\cite{hudson2019gqa}. Two inference-time modifications are evaluated: (1) inserting a pedestrian-focused $P_1$ prompt into the graph, whose predicted response selects a conditional follow-up question regarding the ego vehicle's response and appends that answer to the behaviour prompt context (`+ Pedestrian QA'); and (2) an oracle-context setting that substitutes ground-truth $P_{1-3}$ answers for model predictions during inference (`GT'). Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:impl} lists the exact prompt wording. Under `+ Pedestrian QA', the oracle context supplies the ground-truth answer to ``Is there a person in the scene?'' (which is affirmative on every DriveLM-CARLA-ped frame) and triggers the pedestrian follow-up prompt along with its ground-truth answer. Because this oracle setting directly exposes the target condition to the behaviour stage, the `GT' rows do not represent a realistic performance ceiling.

\paragraph{Results.}
As reported in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:carla}, both baselines fail on most pedestrian encounters: TransFuser++ falls from 70.19 on standard DriveLM-CARLA scenes to 8.72 on DriveLM-CARLA-ped, while base DriveLM-Agent drops from 59.63 to 4.59, trailing TransFuser++. Appending the pedestrian prompt lifts DriveLM-Agent accuracy on DriveLM-CARLA-ped to 27.04 at the cost of lowering standard-scene accuracy from 59.63 to 52.17, and the agent still fails to brake on roughly 73\% of pedestrian frames. Moreover, this improvement depends on prior human knowledge: because the added prompt was designed specifically for the withheld pedestrian category, the experiment demonstrates responsiveness to an oracle-informed prompt rather than autonomous discovery of an unseen class. The model also benefits from unablated co-training on COCO and GQA, which include human instances, and all numbers reflect a single training run. \subsection{Question-wise Analysis on DriveLM-nuScenes}\label{drivelm:sec:exp_question_wise}

To examine how individual question types affect behaviour prediction when supplied as context, we select three representative prompts from each reasoning stage and train BLIP-2 under cumulative subsets of these prompts as behaviour context (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:question_wise}).

\paragraph{Representative questions.}
Across the three stages, the selected prompts are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=4.4em, labelwidth=3.8em, labelsep=0.6em, align=left, itemsep=0pt]
  \item[$P_{1-1}$:] What are the important objects in the current scene?
  \item[$P_{1-2}$:] What is the moving status of object \emph{X}?
  \item[$P_{1-3}$:] What is the visual description of object \emph{X}?
  \item[$P_{2-1}$:] What is the future state of object \emph{X}?
  \item[$P_{2-2}$:] Would object \emph{X} be in the moving direction of the ego vehicle?
  \item[$P_{2-3}$:] What object should the ego vehicle notice first / second / third when the ego vehicle is getting to the next possible location?
  \item[$P_{3-1}$:] What actions could the ego vehicle take based on the observation of object \emph{X}?
  \item[$P_{3-2}$:] What actions taken by the ego vehicle can lead to a collision with object \emph{X}?
  \item[$P_{3-3}$:] In this scenario, what are safe actions to take for the ego vehicle?
\end{itemize}
These nine prompts were selected because they account for roughly 60\% of all QAs across the three stages and, in our assessment, capture the core cues required for driving decisions. Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:waymo_ablation} reports a coarser stage-level ablation.

One untested hypothesis is that predicted future states of surrounding traffic already encode the information needed to select the ego behaviour.

\subsection{Performance for \texorpdfstring{$P_{1-3}$}{P1-3} via GVQA}\label{drivelm:sec:exp_vqa}

Lastly, we benchmark question answering across the $P_{1-3}$ stages of GVQA and evaluate how context affects response quality. Two models are compared: off-the-shelf BLIP-2~\cite{li2023blip2} evaluated zero-shot without DriveLM fine-tuning, and DriveLM-Agent.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Evaluating without context (`None') reduces the task to conventional single-turn VQA, mapping an image and question directly to an answer. Conversely, the oracle-context configuration (`GT') supplies ground-truth parent answers rather than model-generated predictions at test time. Neither configuration acts as a strict empirical bound: as Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:vqa} demonstrates, oracle context occasionally scores below the context-free baseline.

\paragraph{Results.}
Across every context mode in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:vqa}, both models score lower on DriveLM-nuScenes than on DriveLM-CARLA, reflecting the higher lexical and phrasing diversity of human annotations in nuScenes compared with rule-generated CARLA templates. Fine-tuning gives DriveLM-Agent a wide margin over zero-shot BLIP-2 on both benchmarks (\eg, a context-free GPT Score of 71.39 versus 42.97 on DriveLM-nuScenes). Conditioning on graph context yields mixed outcomes: it increases the GPT Score across all four model--dataset pairs by 1.12 to 8.66 points and improves SPICE in three pairs, but slightly reduces SPICE for zero-shot BLIP-2 on DriveLM-CARLA (10.46 to 10.30). Supplying ground-truth context can also hurt performance, lowering the BLIP-2 GPT Score on DriveLM-nuScenes from 42.97 without context to 41.10. For fine-tuned DriveLM-Agent on DriveLM-nuScenes, the graph-context increase in GPT Score is modest (71.39 to 72.51), and the run-to-run variance of the GPT-3.5 judge was not quantified to verify whether a roughly one-point gap exceeds evaluation noise. \section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{drivelm:sec:discussion}

Our empirical findings qualify where graph-structured prompting is beneficial. Within the training domain on DriveLM-nuScenes, conditioning on the graph worsened open-loop trajectory errors and behaviour accuracy compared with predicting behaviour without upstream context (ADE rising from 1.39 to 1.74, collision rate from 1.67 to 1.89, and behaviour accuracy falling from 61.45 to 57.49 in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch}). Positive effects emerged only under distribution shift: across the nuScenes-to-Waymo camera change, graph context improved speed classification (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_waymo}), and on withheld pedestrians in CARLA it helped once a class-specific prompt was manually inserted into the graph (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_carla}). Even in those settings, the gains attributable to the graph are narrower or more reliant on human intervention than raw comparisons against task-specific driving baselines imply, and several broader limitations apply.

\paragraph{Backbone versus graph.}
Cross-domain experiments entangle the pre-trained representations of the VLM backbone with the explicit structure of the GVQA graph, and our baselines only partially isolate the two factors. On Waymo, fine-tuning the BLIP-2 architecture directly on trajectories without VQA data or context (BLIP-RT-2) already yields an ADE of 2.78, and context-free DriveLM-Agent achieves 2.76, compared with 4.16 for UniAD-Single. Adding full-graph context reduces ADE to 2.63, while prediction-only context reaches 2.62 (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:waymo_supp}). Thus, the primary reduction relative to UniAD-Single accompanies the backbone rather than the graph, yet even that reduction is confounded by camera asymmetry (UniAD-Single loses its rear view whereas DriveLM-Agent always uses only the front view) and model capacity (3.955B versus 131.9M parameters in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:latency}), with no control that isolates vision-language pre-training. Graph conditioning contributes a smaller incremental gain centred on speed accuracy (43.90 $\rightarrow$ 54.29), while prediction prompts alone match its ADE improvement. In CARLA, improving performance on unseen pedestrians requires injecting a hand-authored pedestrian prompt informed by prior knowledge of the missing class, alongside unablated co-training on COCO and GQA. Under both interventions, DriveLM-CARLA-ped accuracy rises to 27.04 while standard-scene accuracy declines from 59.63 to 52.17, and the 92.35 oracle-context figure leaks pedestrian presence rather than establishing an upper bound. Every experiment was executed once, leaving cross-seed variance unmeasured.

\paragraph{Baselines and metrics that were not evaluated.}
Several diagnostic baselines and evaluation checks were omitted from this study and represent methodological limitations. First, behaviour accuracies were not compared against a majority-class predictor. Subsequent analysis in DriveBench showed that roughly 78.6\% of DriveLM-nuScenes behaviour labels correspond to ``Going Straight'' (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:community}). Assuming a comparable validation distribution, unconditionally predicting \texttt{straight} would achieve around 78.6\% steering accuracy, outperforming the Chain variant (75.34 in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch}) as well as the perception-only entries of Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:question_wise} (74.34--75.72). Reporting majority-class baselines for speed, steering, and combined behaviour across splits would clarify the margin gained over the label prior, just as a constant stopping rule would score 100 on DriveLM-CARLA-ped (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_carla}). Second, no image-blind baseline was run to measure how much performance comes from question text alone; DriveBench later performed this check on a challenge-trained model (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:community}), but not on the checkpoints evaluated here. Third, evaluating an ego-status-only model on the selected key frames would verify whether key-frame sampling mitigates the open-loop ego-status shortcut (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_arch}). Fourth, and central to GVQA, node predictions were never scored for cross-edge logical consistency. Checking conditional child accuracy given correct versus erroneous parent predictions, perturbing parent answers to observe downstream behaviour changes, or measuring agreement between stage-$P_3$ safe actions and stage-$B$ behaviour choices would directly test whether downstream decisions rely on upstream reasoning; node-independent DriveLM-Metrics leave this unverified. Fifth, scoring predictions across multiple runs of a version-locked LLM judge would establish whether one- to two-point differences in GPT Score (such as 71.39 versus 72.51 in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:vqa}) exceed judge noise.

\paragraph{Data quality and answer priors.}
Quality control for DriveLM-nuScenes is reported only at the process level (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:data_nus}), without quantitative inter-annotator agreement or post-audit error rates. Because annotators chose key frames judged to be visually informative and indicative of upcoming state transitions (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:nus_collection}) rather than filtering algorithmically for manoeuvres, ``Going Straight'' dominates the resulting behaviour labels. Evaluating behaviour accuracy on such a skewed distribution rewards predictions aligned with the dominant prior.

\paragraph{Efficiency constraints.}
Because DriveLM-Agent queries an autoregressive LLM sequentially across multiple graph stages, inference is approximately $10\times$ slower than UniAD (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:latency}), bottlenecked by a generation throughput of 8.5 tokens/s. Concurrent advances in LLM quantisation and token pruning~\cite{xiao2023smoothquant,shi2023crossget} can reduce this latency in orthogonal ways, and Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:questions} outlines scheduling strategies such as running the final trajectory head at CARLA's 20\,Hz control rate while updating upstream reasoning nodes at a lower frequency.

\paragraph{Driving-specific inputs.}
DriveLM-Agent feeds a single low-resolution front-camera frame into the standard VLM vision encoder, omitting LiDAR, multi-frame temporal history, and 360-degree surround coverage. This single-camera input also creates a mismatch with DriveLM-nuScenes annotations: key objects are annotated across all six surround cameras (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:nus_composition}), and because prompts referencing non-front cameras were retained without view filtering during DriveLM-Agent training and evaluation, the model can only answer those prompts from textual coordinate clues or label priors. Adapting the graph to ingest multi-camera views or multi-frame sensory streams is left to subsequent work.

\paragraph{Closed-loop planning.}
All evaluations in this chapter are open-loop. Because supplying ego-vehicle velocity and acceleration in open-loop benchmarks can inflate trajectory metrics without improving closed-loop driving~\cite{li2024ego}, ego status is withheld from DriveLM-Agent, and the full UniAD model (which encodes ego status in BEV) is listed strictly as a privileged reference. Running DriveLM-Agent in closed loop was precluded by computational cost rather than simulator constraints: although CARLA's synchronous mode permits non-real-time stepping, evaluating a 0.16 FPS model (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:latency}) at every 20\,Hz simulation tick requires roughly 125\,s of wall-clock compute per simulated second. Lower-cost closed-loop evaluation of VLM planners on benchmarks such as DriveLM-CARLA was subsequently pursued by later studies (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:subsequent}).

\paragraph{Question design.}
Selecting an effective set of graph prompts remains heuristic in DriveLM and relies on domain intuition, or on advance knowledge of the target class in the CARLA pedestrian study. Under our current prompt templates, raising $P_{1-3}$ answer quality proved difficult and translated weakly into downstream behaviour and trajectory gains, while graph conditioning brought only modest VQA improvements. Factors contributing to this ceiling may include simple prompt formatting, moderate VLM capacity, or weak causal coupling between annotated stages; Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:questions} discusses how DriveLM-CARLA can be used to study these factors systematically.

\section{From Benchmark to Community}\label{drivelm:sec:community}

To support follow-up research, we publicly released the DriveLM datasets, baseline checkpoints, and evaluation server\footnote{\url{https://github.com/OpenDriveLab/DriveLM}}. A central community application was the \emph{Driving with Language} track of the 2024 Autonomous Grand Challenge\footnote{\url{https://opendrivelab.com/challenge2024/}}, co-located with the CVPR 2024 Workshop on Foundation Models for Autonomous Systems and organised with the thesis author serving as lead organiser. Competition submissions were evaluated on multiple-choice, conversation, and yes/no prompts drawn from the DriveLM-nuScenes v1.1 (version 1.1) validation split; this split matches the validation scenes used in this chapter, with ground-truth answers withheld from the public release to prevent test-set leakage. Entrants were ranked by a composite metric assigning weight 0.4 to a ChatGPT score and weight 0.2 each to standard language scores (BLEU, ROUGE-L, and CIDEr), key-object coordinate matching, and classification accuracy. Participants received a fine-tuned LLaMA-Adapter V2~\cite{gao2023llamaadapterv2} baseline as starter code. Across the seven challenge tracks, this track drew the largest participation, with 978 submissions from 152 teams across 14 countries and regions. The track's Innovation Award recognised team ADLM for aligning unified BEV spatial features with an LLM in an end-to-end architecture to strengthen spatial reasoning. Separately, the CARLA expert PDM-Lite~\cite{beisswenger2024pdmlite} introduced alongside DriveLM has become one of two standard data-collection experts adopted on CARLA Leaderboard 2.0~\cite{hamdan2025eta}.

Public competition also prompted closer auditing of the benchmark itself. In a subsequent study co-authored by the thesis author, DriveBench~\cite{xie2025vlms} examined whether VLM predictions on DriveLM-nuScenes genuinely rely on visual inputs. Evaluating 12 VLMs (including a challenge-recipe LLaMA-Adapter V2 DriveLM-Agent) across 200 DriveLM-nuScenes key frames under clean images, 15 synthetic visual corruptions, and black-image text-only inputs revealed that most models suffer minimal score degradation even when visual input is removed entirely: given text alone, GPT-4o preserved roughly 95\% of its clean GPT score on open-ended prediction and planning prompts, whereas human accuracy on perception multiple-choice questions fell from 93.33\% on clean frames to 33.33\% under fog. DriveBench traced this invariance to skewed label distributions and prompt leakage: ``Going Straight'' accounts for roughly 78.6\% of behaviour labels in DriveLM-nuScenes, and prompts explicitly state the source camera and 2D pixel coordinates of queried objects. The audit also exposed weaknesses in evaluation metrics. Standard n-gram scores heavily favour memorised phrasing: the fine-tuned DriveLM baseline surpassed GPT-4o on planning ROUGE-L (53.12 \vs 6.54) yet lagged behind both Qwen2-VL-72B and GPT-4o on prediction GPT score (44.33 \vs 49.35 and 51.30). Moreover, LLM judges supplied only with reference and predicted answers produced compressed, homogeneous ratings, whereas incorporating the prompt, scene context, and task-specific grading rubrics sharpened discrimination.

These findings carry two direct implications for interpreting this chapter. First, the VQA numbers in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:vqa} rely on n-gram metrics and a reference-only GPT Score that DriveBench showed can reward surface templates, while the behaviour evaluation is skewed toward ``Going Straight'' and lacks blind or majority-class baselines (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:discussion}). Second, at a methodological level, benchmarks for multimodal reasoning require adversarial checks against visual corruption and text-only shortcuts, since a model that disregards camera images will appear invariant to sensor degradation. DriveBench therefore recommends class-balanced, context-grounded splits paired with rubric-guided LLM evaluation. Releasing a benchmark, stress-testing it through an open competition, and auditing its failure modes completes one full iteration of the benchmark-driven methodology outlined in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\subsection{Subsequent Developments (2023--2026)}\label{drivelm:sec:subsequent}

Research published concurrently with or subsequent to DriveLM has reshaped three aspects of language-based driving: how decision steps are structured, how reasoning interfaces with control, and how evaluation protocols are designed.

\paragraph{Decomposing the decision.}
Whereas GVQA relies on a manually specified question topology, general visual reasoning frameworks such as VisProg and ViperGPT prompt an LLM to synthesise executable programs that invoke perception modules and expose intermediate outputs~\cite{gupta2023visprog,suris2023vipergpt}, while Visual CoT grounds multi-step chains of thought in specific image crops~\cite{shao2024visualcot}. Within autonomous driving, several benchmarks now supervise the reasoning chain directly: concurrent work on Reason2Drive pairs perception--prediction--reasoning chains with consistency scoring against the final action~\cite{nie2024reason2drive}; OmniDrive synthesises counterfactual 3D reasoning prompts~\cite{wang2025omnidrive}; and DriveLMM-o1 provides step-level nuScenes reasoning annotations alongside logical-coherence metrics~\cite{ishaq2025drivelmmo1}. Such protocols directly target the limitation identified in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:discussion}, namely that DriveLM evaluates graph nodes in isolation without verifying edge-wise consistency.

\paragraph{From questions to actions.}
Recent architectures typically unify language reasoning and trajectory generation within a single policy. EMMA predicts trajectories, 3D objects, and road topology directly from camera views and reports planning gains from chain-of-thought supervision~\cite{hwang2025emma}. AutoVLA discretises trajectories into language tokens in the manner of DriveLM-Agent while applying reinforcement fine-tuning to skip redundant reasoning in routine scenes~\cite{zhou2025autovla}, and Alpamayo-R1 applies reinforcement learning to align its intermediate reasoning traces with its output trajectories~\cite{nvidia2025alpamayo}. Multiple recent VLM planners have also moved to closed-loop evaluation: ORION~\cite{fu2025orion} and AutoVLA benchmark on Bench2Drive~\cite{jia2024bench2drive}, while SimLingo won the 2024 CARLA Challenge~\cite{renz2025simlingo} after training on DriveLM-style CARLA VQA prompts, evaluating on an answer-balanced validation split, and testing instruction following in closed loop. To overcome the inference latency of sequential VLM prompting, dual-system architectures pair a low-frequency VLM reasoner with a high-frequency motion planner~\cite{tian2024drivevlm,jiang2026senna,hamdan2025eta}, whereas FLARE (co-authored by the thesis author) eliminates test-time text decoding entirely by aligning a pre-trained VLM via self-supervised future DINOv2 feature prediction and group relative policy optimisation on NAVSIM~\cite{xie2026flare}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
Evaluation methodology has evolved in parallel. LingoQA introduced Lingo-Judge, a trained answer-verification classifier that aligns closely with human judgements in place of generic LLM prompting~\cite{marcu2024lingoqa}, complementing DriveBench's finding that rubric-free GPT scoring lacks discriminative power. To test out-of-distribution planning, the 2025 Waymo Vision-Based End-to-End Driving Challenge (WOD-E2E) curates long-tail driving segments and evaluates candidate trajectories against human rater preferences rather than open-loop $L_2$ displacement from logged trajectories~\cite{xu2026wode2e}, contrasting with the random validation sampling and ADE metrics used in our Waymo experiment. Viewed in retrospect, DriveLM served as an early formulation and community testbed for structured language reasoning in driving, whose public challenge catalysed subsequent audits. While its own empirical evidence remained open-loop, node-independent, and partially confounded, subsequent work has progressed toward closed-loop testing, balanced label distributions, and explicit verification of reasoning--action alignment.

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{drivelm:sec:summary}

This chapter investigated RQ2 by examining how pre-trained vision-language models can be linked to autonomous driving through a human-inspectable reasoning structure and whether that structure aids generalisation under distribution shift. We introduced DriveLM and the Graph VQA (GVQA) formulation, which organises driving queries into a directed graph spanning perception, prediction, planning, behaviour, and motion so that intermediate answers remain readable by human operators. Two datasets instantiate GVQA: DriveLM-nuScenes (comprising 4,871 frames with an average of 91.4 predominantly human-annotated QAs per frame) and DriveLM-CARLA (containing roughly 1.6M QAs generated via PDM-Lite, a rule-based expert created by Bei{\ss}wenger~\cite{beisswenger2024pdmlite} that achieves a 36.3 average driving score across 20 official Leaderboard 2.0 validation routes over three seeds, compared with 2\% for the prior expert), accompanied by stage-wise DriveLM-Metrics and a token-based BLIP-2 baseline, DriveLM-Agent.

Across our experiments, graph conditioning provided gains only under distribution shift rather than in-domain, and its isolated contribution was smaller than direct comparisons against task-specific driving models suggest. In-domain on DriveLM-nuScenes, DriveLM-Agent matches or exceeds single-frame UniAD in open-loop ADE (1.39 without context and 1.74 with graph context, versus 1.80), yet adding graph context worsens in-domain ADE (1.39 $\rightarrow$ 1.74), collision rate (1.67 $\rightarrow$ 1.89), and behaviour accuracy (61.45 $\rightarrow$ 57.49) relative to no context. Under zero-shot transfer to Waymo, DriveLM-Agent achieves an ADE of 2.63 compared with 4.16 for UniAD-Single, but most of this margin is already present without VQA supervision (BLIP-RT-2 at 2.78) or without context (2.76), and the comparison is confounded by rear-camera removal in UniAD-Single and a 3.955B versus 131.9M parameter disparity. Within that Waymo transfer setting, full-graph context specifically improves speed accuracy (43.90 $\rightarrow$ 54.29), while prediction-only context matches the full-graph ADE (2.62). On DriveLM-CARLA, where training scenes exclude pedestrians, TransFuser++ behaviour accuracy drops from 70.19 to 8.72 on unseen pedestrians; DriveLM-Agent (co-trained on COCO and GQA) rises from 4.59 to 27.04 on pedestrian frames only when supplied with a hand-crafted pedestrian prompt, which simultaneously reduces standard-scene accuracy from 59.63 to 52.17. Because that prompt relies on advance knowledge of the withheld class, the gain reflects an injected human prior, and the 92.35 oracle-context score leaks pedestrian presence rather than bounding achievable performance. Co-authors led the CARLA experiments, and every table entry in this chapter comes from a single run. Question-level ablations on DriveLM-nuScenes indicate that prediction and planning prompts aid behaviour classification slightly more than perception prompts alone, by margins comparable to sampling uncertainty, while on $P_{1-3}$ VQA, graph context improved GPT Scores across all four settings without uniformly improving SPICE, and the GPT Score was designated as primary post hoc. Key limitations include slow open-loop inference at 0.16 FPS (8.5 tokens/s, roughly tenfold slower than UniAD), single front-camera input, the absence of majority-class, image-blind, ego-status-only, and graph-consistency evaluations, and vulnerability to text priors later demonstrated by DriveBench.

Even when structured VLM prompting aids transfer under sensor or object shifts, it neither guarantees reliability nor removes the need for human priors when unmodelled categories appear. Once deployed, any end-to-end driving policy will inevitably encounter out-of-distribution traffic scenarios where its predictive confidence is miscalibrated and static weights offer no mechanism for online self-correction. Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} shifts focus from language reasoning to online adaptation through test-time training, enabling a motion planner to update its representations during deployment by minimising predictive uncertainty computed over the five steering behaviour clusters introduced in this chapter.
