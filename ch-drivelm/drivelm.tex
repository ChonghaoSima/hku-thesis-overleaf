Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} gave the agent a universal description of what is where. Such a description is necessary, but it does not tell the agent what to do. A driver who knows where every object is must still decide which objects matter, how they are likely to move and how to respond. When the situation is one that training never covered, such as an unfamiliar sensor configuration or a road user of a kind never seen before, these decisions cannot be read off from past examples; they have to be reasoned out. Reasoning is therefore the second front on which a physical agent meets the curse of reality.

Vision-language models (VLMs) trained on web-scale data are an obvious source of such reasoning. They carry broad knowledge of the world, they communicate in language that people can read and question, and they can break a decision into several logically connected steps. They are not, however, grounded in driving. A general VLM has never been asked to turn a camera image into a trajectory, and the driving question-answering datasets available when this work began asked either one scene-level question or a short chain of questions about a single object. Neither format reflects what we take to be the structure of a driver's reasoning: considering several objects, and reasoning about each of them in several steps, from perception through prediction to planning. This gap leads to the second research question of the thesis:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ2 (Reason).} \emph{How can the general knowledge of vision-language models be grounded in driving in a structured and verifiable way that mirrors how humans reason from perception through prediction to planning, and does such reasoning help a driving agent generalise to unseen situations?}
\end{quote}

This chapter answers RQ2 with DriveLM, which provides a task, datasets, metrics and a baseline for driving with language. The task, Graph Visual Question Answering (GVQA), arranges perception, prediction, planning, behaviour and motion question-answer pairs as the nodes of a directed acyclic graph, whose edges encode logical dependencies between objects and between reasoning stages. The datasets, collectively called DriveLM-Data, instantiate this task twice. DriveLM-nuScenes contains 4,871 frames with 91.4 question-answer pairs per frame on average, most of them written by human annotators. DriveLM-CARLA contains about 1.6 million rule-generated question-answer pairs, collected with PDM-Lite, a rule-based expert for CARLA Leaderboard 2.0 developed for this purpose by Bei{\ss}wenger~\cite{beisswenger2024pdmlite}. The baseline, DriveLM-Agent, is deliberately simple: it repurposes a general VLM (BLIP-2) for end-to-end driving by passing parent answers as context to child questions and by expressing trajectories as tokens. The experiments show where structured reasoning helps, where it hurts, and how much. On nuScenes, DriveLM-Agent is competitive with a single-frame version of the driving-specific UniAD in open-loop planning, but in-domain, graph context degraded open-loop planning and behaviour accuracy: compared with predicting the behaviour without context, the average displacement error (ADE) rose from 1.39 to 1.74, the collision rate from 1.67 to 1.89, and the behaviour accuracy fell from 61.45 to 57.49. Under the sensor shift from nuScenes to Waymo, graph context improved speed decisions, raising the speed accuracy of the predicted behaviour from 43.90 to 54.29. Evaluated zero-shot on Waymo after training only on nuScenes, DriveLM-Agent reaches an ADE of 2.63 against 4.16 for the single-frame UniAD, but the same BLIP-2 network trained on the motion task alone, without VQA data or context, already reaches 2.78, and the comparison is not like-for-like: UniAD-Single loses its rear camera on Waymo, whereas DriveLM-Agent uses only the front camera, and DriveLM-Agent has about 30 times as many parameters (3.955B against 131.9M). In CARLA, where pedestrians never appear in training, adding a pedestrian-specific question to the graph raises behaviour accuracy on pedestrian scenes from 4.59 to 27.04, while accuracy on regular scenes falls from 59.63 to 52.17. That question was written with knowledge of which class was missing, so this gain relies on an injected human prior: it is an oracle-guided result, from experiments led by co-authors. All of these results come from single training runs. In the vocabulary of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, the Waymo transfer tests a perceptual (sensing) shift and the CARLA pedestrian test a semantic shift.

\section{Motivation}\label{drivelm:sec:motivation}

Current autonomous driving stacks still lack crucial capabilities~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey,chib2023recent}. The first is generalisation: the ability to handle unseen scenarios or unfamiliar sensor configurations. The second is interaction with humans, including the ability to explain driving decisions, for which there are growing calls~\cite{atakishiyev2023explaining}. Moreover, today's driving models plan on geometrically precise bird's-eye-view (BEV) representations~\cite{hu2023uniad,chitta2023transfuser,li2023bevdevils}. We hypothesise that human drivers instead reason in an object-centric way, which we refer to as $P_{1-3}$: a rough identification and localisation of the key objects, followed by reasoning about their possible movement and the aggregation of this information into a driving action. The hypothesis is inspired by accounts of object-centred representation in vision~\cite{Marr82} and of a core system for objects in human cognition~\cite{spelke2007core}; neither studies driving, and this chapter does not test the hypothesis.

VLMs~\cite{li2023blip2,zhang2023llamaadapter,liu2023llava,wang2023visionllm} have three strengths that match these needs. First, they hold a base understanding of the world, learned from internet-scale data, that could facilitate generalisation in planning; VLMs have already achieved this kind of generalisation in simpler robotics tasks~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2,driess2023palme}. Second, language as input and output offers a platform for human-friendly interaction, unlike the bounding boxes or trajectories that are more common in current methods~\cite{li2022bevformer,hu2022stp3,Renz2022CORL,dauner2023parting}. Third, VLMs can make decisions in multiple steps linked by logical reasoning~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2,wei2022cot,wang2023selfconsistency,yao2023tree,besta2023got,ding2023everything}. A single VLM can also be fine-tuned on all of these steps with driving data. This is not end-to-end differentiability in the sense valued for autonomous driving~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey}, however: the answers passed between steps are discrete text, so gradients do not flow through them, and in DriveLM-Agent the steps are trained with ground-truth context and the motion stage has separate weights (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:agent}).

At the time of this work, efforts to apply VLMs to driving relied on two kinds of visual question answering (VQA). Scene-level VQA describes the driving behaviour with one or two supporting reasons, \eg, ``The car is moving into the right lane because it is safe to do so''~\cite{kim2018bddx,kim2019had}. Single object-level VQA explains the response of the ego vehicle to one object through a chain of questions of the form ``what--which--where--how--why'', \eg, ``The ego vehicle stops because there is a pedestrian in a white shirt crossing the intersection in front of the ego vehicle and it does not want to crash into the pedestrian''~\cite{sachdeva2023rank2tell,malla2023drama,qian2024nuscenesqa}. Neither paradigm is a suitable proxy for the hypothesised $P_{1-3}$ reasoning, in which several objects are considered and each of them is reasoned about in several steps.

DriveLM therefore changes the proxy task rather than the model (Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:teaser}). In GVQA, the questions of a frame are linked by logical dependencies that can guide the answering process, and the chain of questions ends with the behaviour and motion of the ego vehicle, so that answering the graph amounts to driving. Two design choices follow from the aim of generalisation. First, the training data are paired with test settings that demand zero-shot generalisation: a new sensor configuration (training on nuScenes~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes} and testing on Waymo~\cite{sun2020waymo}) and a new object class (pedestrians, which never appear in the training data of DriveLM-CARLA). Second, the contribution lies in the task, the data, the metrics and an official evaluation server rather than in a new algorithm. The young field of driving with language lacked a standardised dataset, task and evaluation framework, so we make no claim of algorithmic novelty and keep the baseline intentionally simple. This follows the benchmark-driven research cycle described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\section{Related Work}\label{drivelm:sec:related}

This section covers the work that preceded or was concurrent with DriveLM, up to its publication in 2024. Chapter~\ref{ch:background} reviews the field more broadly, and Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:subsequent} positions DriveLM against later work.

\paragraph{Generalisation in autonomous driving.}
The inadequate generalisation of driving systems to the ``long tail'' of corner cases poses serious safety concerns~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey,teng2023motion,tampuu2020survey}. Prior research tackles this issue primarily with data-driven methods~\cite{suo2021trafficsim,chen2022learning,akhauri2020enhanced,wang2021advsim,hanselmann2022king}; for example, TrafficSim~\cite{suo2021trafficsim} collects more data for safety-critical cases through simulation. An emerging direction leverages semantic information to supervise the detection of unseen or anomalous objects~\cite{elhafsi2023semantic,wang2023driveanywhere,chen2023categorical,pan2024vlp}. Even so, the zero-shot performance of driving systems is not yet satisfactory. This chapter explores a different route towards generalisation: learning logical reasoning through Graph VQA.

\paragraph{Language-grounded driving.}
Several concurrent methods feed multi-modal inputs into large language models (LLMs) for driving tasks~\cite{seff2023motionlm,mao2023gptdriver,chen2024drivingwithllms,jin2023adapt,xu2024drivegpt4,wayve2023lingo1,wang2023driveanywhere,marcu2024lingoqa,chen2023categorical,wang2023drivemlm,mao2023language,tian2024drivevlm,shao2024lmdrive,yuan2024ragdriver,pan2024vlp,han2024dmedriver}. GPT-Driver~\cite{mao2023gptdriver} and Driving-with-LLMs~\cite{chen2024drivingwithllms} encode the perceived scene state into prompts and rely on LLMs to formulate reasonable plans. DriveGPT4~\cite{xu2024drivegpt4} projects raw sensor data into tokens and uses an LLM to predict control signals and explanations end to end. At the time, whether LLMs help driving systems generalise had not been tested. DriveLM combines VLMs with training on graph-structured QAs and evaluates zero-shot end-to-end planning under a sensor shift and with an unseen object class, which these concurrent studies did not evaluate; as Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:experiments} shows, most of the resulting gain under the sensor shift comes from the pre-trained VLM rather than from the graph.

\paragraph{Vision-language benchmarks for driving.}
Many driving datasets with language annotations have been proposed~\cite{zhou2023vision,Dewangan2023talk2bev,wu2023referkitti,wu2025nuprompt,qian2024nuscenesqa,kim2019had,kim2018bddx,sachdeva2023rank2tell,malla2023drama,marcu2024lingoqa,ding2024holistic}. Some of them express perceptual information as text: NuScenes-QA~\cite{qian2024nuscenesqa} and NuPrompt~\cite{wu2025nuprompt} describe the positions and states of surrounding objects. BDD-X~\cite{kim2018bddx} gives reasons for the actions of the ego vehicle in natural language, while DRAMA~\cite{malla2023drama} and Rank2Tell~\cite{sachdeva2023rank2tell} identify crucial objects and provide corresponding driving suggestions. These datasets, however, focus on scene-level context or on individual objects. DriveLM fills this gap by organising language annotations at the object level and the task level in a graph structure (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:dataset}).

\paragraph{Embodied planning with LLMs.}
Recent work leverages the reasoning and generalisation capacity of LLMs~\cite{touvron2023llama,floridi2020gpt,kojima2022large} for embodied AI systems~\cite{brohan2023can,huang2022inner,zitkovich2023rt2,driess2023palme,liang2023code,karamcheti2023voltron,rana2023sayplan}. PaLM-E~\cite{driess2023palme} trains an LLM for various embodied tasks, including sequential robotic manipulation planning. Code as Policies~\cite{liang2023code} provides a robot-centric formulation of programs generated by language models and executed on real systems. RT-2~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2} represents robot actions as language tokens and trains VLMs to output robot policies. These methods show the capabilities of LLMs in embodied planning and inspire us to apply them to the shortcomings of generalisation in driving, which are far less explored.

\paragraph{Reasoning over graph structures.}
Reasoning derives new judgements from one or several existing ones~\cite{chen2020review}, and many reasoning methods are built on graphs~\cite{shi2019scenegraph,besta2023got,chen2019graph}. XNMs~\cite{shi2019scenegraph} use scene graphs for explainable and explicit visual reasoning with structured knowledge, and Graph of Thoughts~\cite{besta2023got} models the information generated by an LLM as an arbitrary graph. Inspired by these works, DriveLM links the perception, prediction and planning stages of driving through a graph, with the aim that a model learns the reasoning process and applies it to unseen scenarios. Whether it does so is tested in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:experiments}.

\section{Graph Visual Question Answering}\label{drivelm:sec:gvqa}

Models of human driving decompose decision-making into distinct stages that follow a logical progression: identifying and localising the key objects, anticipating their possible future actions and interactions, and planning the ego motion based on all of this information~\cite{macadam2003understanding,groeger2013understanding}. GVQA casts this progression as a proxy task that is intended to mimic this reasoning process. This section formulates the task (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:task}) and the metrics used to evaluate it (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:metrics}); the datasets that instantiate it are described in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:data}.

\subsection{Task Formulation}\label{drivelm:sec:task}

We organise all question-answer pairs (QAs) of an image frame into a graph. By ``graph-structured'' we mean a directed acyclic graph (DAG): a question can take context from several parent and grandparent nodes. The graph $G=(V,E)$ contains a set of vertices $V$, where each vertex is a QA pair $v=(q,a)$ associated with one or more key objects in the scene. What distinguishes GVQA from ordinary VQA is that its QAs have logical dependencies, which form the edges of the graph: $E\subseteq V\times V$ is a set of directed edges, and each edge $e=(v_p,v_c)$ connects a parent QA $v_p$ to a child QA $v_c$. The edge set is built along two dimensions. Object-level edges represent the impact of interactions between different objects; for example, in Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:teaser} (centre) the planning node for the sedan is influenced by the perception node of the pedestrian. Task-level edges capture the logical chain of reasoning stages:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Perception} ($P_1$): identification, description and localisation of the key objects in the current scene.
  \item \textbf{Prediction} ($P_2$): estimation of the possible actions and interactions of the key objects, based on the perception results.
  \item \textbf{Planning} ($P_3$): possible safe actions of the ego vehicle.
  \item \textbf{Behaviour} ($B$): classification of the driving decision.
  \item \textbf{Motion} ($M$): waypoints of the future trajectory of the ego vehicle.
\end{itemize}

The concepts of perception, prediction and planning ($P_{1-3}$) are similar to those of end-to-end autonomous driving~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey}, while motion and behaviour are defined from the future trajectory of the ego vehicle. The motion $M$ is a set of $N$ points with coordinates $(x,y)$ in bird's-eye view, $M=\{(x_0,y_0),(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_N,y_N)\}$, where each point is the offset of the future position from the current position after a fixed time interval. The distance travelled in $x$ and $y$ over each time interval is
\begin{equation}
  \{x,y\}_{\text{dist}} = \{(\delta_{x,1},\delta_{y,1}),\ldots,(\delta_{x,N},\delta_{y,N})\},
  \label{drivelm:eq:xy_dist}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{x,i}=x_i-x_{i-1}$ and $\delta_{y,i}=y_i-y_{i-1}$ for $i=1,2,\ldots,N$. The behaviour representation serves as an interface from $P_{1-3}$ to $M$. To obtain it, we map the mean of $x_{\text{dist}}$ and $y_{\text{dist}}$ to one of the predefined bins, where each bin corresponds to a category of either speed or steering, denoted $B_{sp}$ and $B_{st}$ respectively. We use five bins for each:
\begin{align*}
  B_{sp} &\in \{\texttt{fast}_2, \texttt{fast}_1, \texttt{moderate}, \texttt{slow}_1, \texttt{slow}_2\},\\
  B_{st} &\in \{\texttt{left}_2, \texttt{left}_1, \texttt{straight}, \texttt{right}_1, \texttt{right}_2\},
\end{align*}
where the subscript indicates the intensity. The speed and steering categories of a trajectory together form its behaviour category $B=(B_{sp},B_{st})$. This simple definition of $B$ is a starting point for research on driving with VLMs; the formulation also supports more abstract behaviours such as lane changes or overtaking.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}\label{drivelm:sec:metrics}

DriveLM-Metrics evaluate the three components of GVQA: motion $M$, behaviour $B$ and $P_{1-3}$. For motion, we use the standard metrics of the nuScenes and Waymo benchmarks, the average and final displacement errors (\textbf{ADE} and \textbf{FDE}) and the \textbf{collision rate} of the predicted trajectory, following UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad}. For behaviour, we report the \textbf{classification accuracy} together with its breakdown into speed and steering accuracy. For $P_{1-3}$, we use two metrics. \textbf{SPICE}~\cite{anderson2016spice}, a prevailing metric in image captioning, parses the predicted and the ground-truth text into scene-graph tuples of objects, attributes and relations and reports the F-score of their overlap. It therefore compares propositional content rather than n-grams, but only the content that its parser can express as such tuples. We also use the \textbf{GPT Score}: with the prompt reproduced in Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:metrics_vqa}, GPT-3.5~\cite{ouyang2022training,chatgpt} is given the ground-truth answer and the predicted answer, but not the question, and asked to rate the prediction on a scale from 0 to 100; the score is parsed from the returned text. We made the GPT Score the primary $P_{1-3}$ metric after the conventional language metrics had shown no consistent trend across context settings (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:vqa_metrics}), so the choice was made after seeing results rather than specified in advance. The judge is specified only as GPT-3.5, without a model version; its scores are not deterministic, and their variance over repeated scoring was not measured. Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:metrics} defines all metrics in detail, reproduces the GPT prompt, and introduces conventional language metrics and a Completeness score used for further analysis. None of these metrics measures whether the answers are consistent along the edges of the graph, which is the defining property of GVQA; Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:discussion} returns to this gap.

\subsection{DriveLM-nuScenes}\label{drivelm:sec:data_nus}

We divide the annotation process into three steps: selecting key frames from video clips, choosing key objects within these key frames, and annotating the frame-level $P_{1-3}$ QAs for the key objects. Part of the perception QAs are generated from the ground truth of nuScenes~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes} and OpenLane-V2~\cite{wang2023openlanev2}; the remaining QAs are annotated manually. The question templates for manual annotation were designed by five domain experts to reflect how humans make driving decisions. Annotators are shown all question templates for each frame and encouraged to answer all of them, with a skip option for questions that do not apply.

Because most of DriveLM-nuScenes is annotated manually (the 299k prediction and planning QAs, out of 443k in total, are all answered by annotators; computed from Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:dataset}), quality is particularly important. We run multiple rounds of quality checks during annotation. In each round, the data are divided into batches and ten percent of the data in each batch are inspected; if the qualification rate of the manually annotated data in this sample falls below expectations, the annotators re-label all data in the batch. Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:data_collection} shows an example of this pipeline, in which all questions undergo quality checks according to our standards. We do not report inter-annotator agreement or error rates for the final data. \authorcheck{give the qualification-rate threshold used in the quality check, the fraction of batches that were re-labelled, the numbers of rule-generated and manually written perception QAs, and which language version (manually corrected or GPT-refined, Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:nus_collection}) the experiments of this chapter use}. The resulting dataset has 4,072 training frames and 799 validation frames. It has far more text annotations per frame than previously proposed datasets (91.4 QAs, against at most 15.3 for the other datasets in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:dataset}), and its QAs, linked in a graph, cover the driving process from perception and prediction to planning. Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:nuscenes} details its composition, annotation protocol and statistics.

\subsection{PDM-Lite: An Expert Driver for CARLA Leaderboard 2.0}\label{drivelm:sec:data_expert}

We collect DriveLM-CARLA with CARLA 0.9.14 in the Leaderboard 2.0 framework~\cite{dosovitskiy2017carla}. Leaderboard 2.0 contains a large set of new driving scenarios compared with its predecessor, Leaderboard 1.0, but at the time of this work no method existed to collect training data at scale in it. For example, the privileged rule-based expert used by TransFuser++~\cite{jaeger2023hidden}, a state-of-the-art method on Leaderboard 1.0, obtains a Driving Score (DS) of only 2\% on the 8+ kilometre-long official validation routes of Leaderboard 2.0. DriveLM-CARLA is therefore collected with PDM-Lite, a rule-based expert for CARLA Leaderboard 2.0 developed for this purpose by Bei{\ss}wenger~\cite{beisswenger2024pdmlite}. At the time of publication, it was the first working rule-based expert for Leaderboard 2.0, and it handles the new challenges of this leaderboard. It is similar to PDM-Closed~\cite{dauner2023parting}, a rule-based planner for nuPlan~\cite{caesar2021nuplan}. PDM-Lite uses the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM)~\cite{treiber2000idm} to obtain a target speed based on the leading vehicle, pedestrian, stop sign or traffic light. Unlike PDM-Closed, which selects one of 16 proposals with a complex cost function, PDM-Lite uses only two proposals and a simpler cost function based on the TransFuser++ expert~\cite{jaeger2023hidden}. The result is a light-weight planner suitable for scalable QA generation. PDM-Lite was developed, and evaluated in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:pdm_results}, by co-authors. On the 20 official validation routes, each driven with three seeds, PDM-Lite reaches an average DS of 36.3 (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:pdm_results}). The accompanying $\pm$\,28.1 is the standard deviation over all 60 route--seed runs, so it mixes the variation between routes with the variation between seeds. The standard deviation of the 20 route means is 23.9 (computed from Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:pdm_results}), and the per-route standard deviations over the three seeds range from 0.0 to 37.4. How much the 20-route average itself varies across seeds, which is what the uncertainty of 36.3 depends on, cannot be computed from the table. \authorcheck{from the evaluation logs, reconcile the DS of 44\% quoted in the main text of the published paper~\cite{sima2024drivelm} with the 36.3 of Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:pdm_results} and correct whichever is wrong, and add the standard deviation of the 20-route average DS across the three seeds}. \subsection{DriveLM-CARLA}\label{drivelm:sec:data_carla}

To collect DriveLM-CARLA, we set up routes in urban, residential and rural areas and execute PDM-Lite on them. During this process, we record the necessary sensor data, sample keyframes, generate QAs from privileged information about the objects and the scene, and connect the QAs into a graph according to their logical relationships. Data and labels are generated at 4 FPS, and keyframes are extracted when the decision of the expert changes, \eg, from accelerating to braking. The rule-based annotation pipeline is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:data_collection}. During collection, we extract privileged information from the simulator about the state of static and dynamic objects in the scene and about the rules triggered in the expert. We use all 38 scenarios of Leaderboard 2.0 except \mbox{\emph{InterurbanAdvancedActorFlow}}, \mbox{\emph{MergerIntoSlowTraffic}} and \mbox{\emph{VehicleTurningRoute}}, and create questions and answers from the extracted information with hand-crafted sentence templates. The questions and their graph structure are given in Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:carla}. This annotation process scales easily, since only the route and scenario settings need to be defined in CARLA and all subsequent steps run automatically. With 1.6M QAs obtained through this straightforward scaling recipe, DriveLM-CARLA is the largest driving-language benchmark in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:dataset} in terms of total textual content.

\section{DriveLM-Agent: A GVQA Baseline}\label{drivelm:sec:agent}

DriveLM-Agent is a baseline for GVQA built on a general VLM, so that it can exploit the knowledge acquired during pre-training. Its goal is to translate an image into the desired motion of the ego vehicle ($M$) through the VQA stages $P_1$, $P_2$, $P_3$ and $B$. We choose BLIP-2~\cite{li2023blip2} as the base VLM because of its simple architecture and flexible fine-tuning, but the approach can be applied to other VLMs as well. Its components are not new: DriveLM-Agent combines BLIP-2 with the trajectory tokenisation of RT-2~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2} and passes answers between questions as textual context.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{drivelm:fig:model_pipeline}, DriveLM-Agent works in stages. (1) The $P_{1-3}$ stages, \ie, perception, prediction and planning, form the foundation: they interpret the scene and reason about its structure. (2) The behaviour stage aggregates the crucial information from $P_{1-3}$ into a description of the desired driving action in language. (3) The motion stage translates this behaviour into an executable driving trajectory. The logical dependency between linked QAs is implemented through context passed between connected nodes of the graph, as described next.

\subsection{Prompting with Context}\label{drivelm:sec:context}

Directly translating images into motion, as in~\cite{Prakash2021transfuser,chitta2021neat}, is extremely challenging. Motivated by the multi-step reasoning of humans, we adopt a similar strategy for VLM-based driving, with the aim of making the knowledge stored in LLMs easier to retrieve and the decision easier to inspect. The model uses the answers of earlier steps of the reasoning process as \emph{context} for later questions. For each edge $e=(v_p,v_c)\in E$, we append the QA of the parent node $v_p$ to the question of the current node $v_c$, with the prefix ``\emph{Context:}''. If a node has several parents, their QAs are concatenated into one context sequence. Context is only one possible way to implement the logical dependencies of GVQA; we choose it for its simplicity. With this scheme, relevant information is passed forward along the logical dependencies established by the graph. During training, the ground-truth parent QAs serve as context (teacher forcing); at inference, the model is applied in multiple rounds in the order $P_1$, $P_2$, $P_3$, $B$, $M$, so that each stage receives the predicted answers of the preceding stages (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:agent}).

The size and structure of the graph at inference time are a design choice that can be adapted to the task or to the available compute budget. We use this property to train on all available QAs but to perform inference on specific subgraphs whose questions are sampled with heuristics (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:impl}).

\paragraph{Context aggregation through behaviour.}
Driving encompasses a wide array of situations that require an appropriate response. Despite this diversity, almost all of them involve decisions that can be discretised into a set of behaviours. For example, braking appropriately addresses situations as different as a red light, a stop sign or an object ahead of the vehicle. The behaviour stage generates such a behaviour: a statement in natural language that articulates the intended movement of the vehicle. As described in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:task}, this description serves as a reflective step in which the model summarises all crucial information from the graph. We therefore use all possible sources of context for predicting the behaviour, \ie, all QAs in $P_{1-3}$. Predicting the behaviour as an intermediate step proved important: on nuScenes it lowers the ADE from 2.63, for the same network predicting motion directly, to 1.39 (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch}). Passing all $P_{1-3}$ QAs to the behaviour stage as context, in contrast, helped only under the sensor shift (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:experiments}).

\subsection{Trajectory Tokenisation for Motion}\label{drivelm:sec:traj_token}

General VLMs cannot easily output fine-grained numerical values. RT-2~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2} handles robotic actions with a specialised trajectory tokenisation module, and we adopt this approach so that DriveLM-Agent can take the image and the behaviour description as input and output a trajectory. Specifically, we divide the waypoint coordinates into 256 bins, determined empirically from the statistics of the training trajectories. We re-define tokens of the BLIP-2 language tokeniser so that each bin has its own token, and fine-tune the VLM on the re-defined vocabulary (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:agent}). For simplicity, the motion stage uses the same VLM architecture (BLIP-2), but with independent LoRA weights, trained on a dataset that contains only the QAs of the motion stage. This functionality could therefore equally be provided by a lightweight LLM~\cite{radford2019language} or by a driving-specific architecture that accepts a command as input~\cite{hu2022stp3,wu2022tcp}.

\section{Experiments}\label{drivelm:sec:experiments}

The experiments address five questions. (1) How can VLMs be effectively repurposed for end-to-end autonomous driving? (2) Can VLMs for driving generalise when evaluated with unseen sensor setups? (3) Can they generalise to an object class never seen during training? (4) What is the effect of each question in perception, prediction and planning on the final behaviour decision? (5) How well do VLMs perform perception, prediction and planning via GVQA? Further results, including other base VLMs, are given in Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:exp}.

\paragraph{Setup.}
All fine-tuning is implemented with LoRA~\cite{hu2021lora}. On DriveLM-nuScenes, we fine-tune BLIP-2 on the \texttt{train} split for 10 epochs with a batch size of 2 per GPU; the entire training takes approximately 7 hours on 8 V100 GPUs. On DriveLM-CARLA, we train BLIP-2 on the keyframes of the \texttt{train} split for 6 epochs, which takes 6 hours on 4 A100 GPUs. Further implementation details are given in Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:impl}.

\paragraph{Evaluation sets and uncertainty.}
In-domain results use the 799 frames of the DriveLM-nuScenes \texttt{val} split, sensor-shift results use 1,000 frames sampled from the Waymo \texttt{val} set, and the CARLA results use the DriveLM-CARLA \texttt{val} split and the DriveLM-CARLA-ped test set (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_carla}). \authorcheck{give the number of frames in the DriveLM-CARLA \texttt{val} split and in DriveLM-CARLA-ped (the regular-scene accuracies in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:carla} are consistent, to within 0.01, with 161 frames); the number of QA pairs scored for Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:vqa} on each dataset; the number of behaviour decisions behind the accuracies in Tables~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch}, \ref{drivelm:tab:question_wise} and~\ref{drivelm:tab:waymo_supp}, several of which are not whole-number fractions of 799 or 1,000; and the speed and steering class distributions of each evaluation set}. \subsection{VLMs for End-to-End Driving}\label{drivelm:sec:exp_arch}

The first experiment assesses the ability of VLMs to perform open-loop planning on DriveLM-nuScenes, and in particular the impact of the context provided to the behaviour and motion stages. Given the sensor data (and, for VLM methods, a text input), the model predicts the future trajectory of the ego vehicle as waypoints. Open-loop planning on nuScenes can be solved to a large extent from the status of the ego vehicle alone~\cite{li2024ego}. We intended to reduce this effect by not feeding ego status to DriveLM-Agent and by evaluating only on the key frames of DriveLM-nuScenes, which annotators selected as rich in scene information and potentially indicative of future state changes (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:nus_collection}). Whether key-frame evaluation actually removes the advantage of ego status was not tested, for example with an ego-status-only baseline on the same frames.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
As a reference for the difficulty of the task, we provide a simple \textbf{Command Mean} baseline. Each frame in nuScenes is associated with one of three commands, `turn left', `turn right' or `go straight', and the baseline outputs the mean of all training trajectories whose command matches that of the test frame. We also compare with UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad}, the state of the art on nuScenes at the time. Its original setting uses multi-frame (video) input, and its BEV encoder also receives the ego status from the CAN bus~\cite{li2024ego}; we therefore train a single-frame version (\textbf{UniAD-Single}), which is closer to the single-image input of DriveLM-Agent. \authorcheck{state whether UniAD-Single keeps the CAN-bus ego-status input of the BEV encoder; whether it was trained on all nuScenes training frames or only on the DriveLM-nuScenes training key frames; whether DriveLM-Agent receives the navigation command, as Command Mean does; and on which frames, and with which averaging, the original UniAD row of Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch} was obtained, since it does not match the averages published for UniAD under the same convention}. Finally, \textbf{BLIP-RT-2} denotes BLIP-2~\cite{li2023blip2} fine-tuned on DriveLM-Data with the trajectory tokenisation of Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:traj_token} for the motion task only. It indicates the performance of the same network architecture as DriveLM-Agent without context inputs or VQA training data.

\paragraph{DriveLM-Agent variants.}
We consider three variants that add the proposed components step by step: (1) a two-stage version that predicts the behaviour and then the motion (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:task}) but uses no $P_{1-3}$ context for behaviour prediction (`None'); (2) a `Chain' version that builds the $P_{1-3}$ graph but passes only its final node ($P_3$) to the behaviour stage; and (3) the full model (`Graph'), which uses all QAs of $P_{1-3}$ as context for $B$.

Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch}(a) shows the in-domain results. Among the baselines, BLIP-RT-2 is unable to match UniAD-Single, although both perform well relative to Command Mean. \subsection{Generalisation Across Sensor Configurations}\label{drivelm:sec:exp_waymo}

As a more challenging setting, we apply the models of Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_arch} without further training to a new domain, the Waymo Open Dataset~\cite{sun2020waymo}. Waymo does not include images from rear cameras, so we drop this input for UniAD-Single. The VLM methods use only the front view and do not require any adaptation. Since Waymo has no GVQA annotations, the questions are generated with rules (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:impl}).

This comparison is not like-for-like. UniAD-Single, a surround-view model trained on the nuScenes cameras, loses its rear camera, a structural change of its input that the front-camera-only VLMs never face. The models also differ about 30-fold in size (3.955B against 131.9M parameters, Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:latency}). No baseline isolates vision-language pre-training, such as a driving model that uses only the front camera, or the BLIP-2 architecture without vision-language initialisation. Finally, the prediction and planning questions on Waymo are generated from each model's own perception answers, so the question distribution differs from that of DriveLM-nuScenes and may differ between context settings, which confounds the context ablation. \authorcheck{state how the 1,000 Waymo \texttt{val} frames were sampled (sampling seed), confirm that every method in Tables~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch} and~\ref{drivelm:tab:waymo_supp} was evaluated on the same frames, and give the number of frames that received prediction and planning questions}

\paragraph{Results.}
As shown in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch}(b), UniAD-Single does not cope well with the new sensor configuration and drops below BLIP-RT-2. Most of the advantage over UniAD-Single therefore comes with the pre-trained vision-language model rather than from the graph: BLIP-RT-2, which has neither VQA training data nor context, reaches an ADE of 2.78 against 4.16 for UniAD-Single, and DriveLM-Agent without context reaches 2.76. Because of the camera asymmetry and the difference in scale described above, this advantage cannot be attributed to vision-language pre-training alone. The multi-stage approach of DriveLM-Agent provides a further, smaller improvement. Graph context lowers the ADE to 2.63, and prediction-only context to 2.62 (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:waymo_supp}), so the ADE gain does not require the full graph. The gain that is specific to the full graph lies in the accuracy of the speed predictions, which rises from 43.90 without context to 54.29 with the full graph, a difference of more than three times the half-width of the sampling interval given in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:experiments}. The chain approach, on the other hand, does not pass on enough useful information, with a speed accuracy of only 41.28. The stage-wise ablation of the context in Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:waymo_ablation} also shows that higher behaviour accuracy goes together, roughly, with lower ADE. All of these results come from a single training run.

\subsection{Generalisation to Unseen Objects}\label{drivelm:sec:exp_carla}

Next, we evaluate zero-shot generalisation to a novel object class. The experiments in this section were led by co-authors (see the statement of contribution). DriveLM-CARLA is collected without any pedestrians in its training and validation splits. \authorcheck{state how pedestrians were excluded from the training and validation splits, given that the 35 Leaderboard 2.0 scenarios used include pedestrian scenarios such as PedestrianCrossing and ParkingCrossingPedestrian: were their pedestrians disabled, or were frames containing pedestrians removed?} We therefore generate a new test set, DriveLM-CARLA-ped, which consists only of frames in which a pedestrian is present in the scene; the correct behaviour is to stop for the pedestrian. Because this correct behaviour is the same on every frame (stopping, with straight steering, as noted below), a predictor that always outputs it would score 100 on DriveLM-CARLA-ped. The test set therefore measures only whether a model stops, and its accuracy must be read together with the accuracy on regular scenes, where such a constant predictor would be penalised. We did not evaluate a constant baseline of this kind.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare DriveLM-Agent with TransFuser++~\cite{jaeger2023hidden}, a state-of-the-art method on CARLA at the time. Compared with DriveLM-Agent, TransFuser++ uses a larger input image, an additional LiDAR sensor and several driving-specific annotations (depth, semantics, 3D bounding boxes and HD map). Because of these task-specific inputs and outputs, however, TransFuser++ can only be trained on the base DriveLM-CARLA dataset and cannot incorporate general computer vision data during training, which makes generalisation more challenging.

\paragraph{DriveLM-Agent variants.}
Taking advantage of the more general architecture of a VLM, we train DriveLM-Agent on samples from COCO~\cite{lin2014microsoft} and GQA~\cite{hudson2019gqa} together with DriveLM-CARLA. We compare several versions: (1) adding a pedestrian-specific $P_1$ question to the graph at inference time, whose answer determines a follow-up question on how the ego vehicle should act, with the answer to the latter appended to the context of the behaviour question (`+ Pedestrian QA'); and (2) as an oracle-context reference, feeding the ground-truth $P_{1-3}$ graph to the model at inference time instead of its own predictions (`GT'). The exact questions are given in Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:impl}. With the pedestrian QA, the ground-truth context contains the true answer to ``Is there a person in the scene?'', which is yes on every frame of DriveLM-CARLA-ped, and selects the pedestrian-specific follow-up question, whose ground-truth answer is also passed to the behaviour question. This context thus leaks the information that the behaviour decision depends on, so the GT rows are not an attainable upper bound.

Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:carla} presents the results. On the pedestrian test set, TransFuser++ struggles: its accuracy drops from 70.19 on DriveLM-CARLA to just 8.72 on DriveLM-CARLA-ped. DriveLM-Agent experiences a similar drop, from 59.63 to 4.59, which leaves it below TransFuser++. Adding the pedestrian QA raises its accuracy in the generalisation setting to 27.04, but lowers its accuracy on regular scenes from 59.63 to 52.17; even with the added question, the agent fails to stop on about 73\% of the pedestrian frames. This gain relies on an injected human prior. The question was written by someone who knew which class was missing from training, so the result is oracle-guided: it shows that the agent can use a relevant question when one is supplied, not that it discovers an unseen class by itself. The gain is also obtained with a model co-trained on COCO and GQA, which contain people, and the contribution of this co-training is not ablated. The result comes from a single run. We attribute most of the remaining errors to pedestrians that the VLM fails to detect; whether stronger VLMs~\cite{gao2023llamaadapterv2,peng2023kosmos} would close the gap was not tested. With oracle context, the pedestrian QA raises the accuracy on the frames with pedestrians from 20.92 to 92.35; because this context reveals that a person is present, the row measures how well the model uses a correct answer rather than an achievable level of performance. DriveLM-CARLA-ped only contains pedestrians crossing a straight road, so all models obtain 100\% accuracy on the steering class, which is always \texttt{straight}.

\paragraph{Representative questions.}
The representative questions of each stage are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=4.4em, labelwidth=3.8em, labelsep=0.6em, align=left, itemsep=0pt]

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{drivelm:sec:discussion}

The experiments give a nuanced answer to the question of where structured reasoning helps. In-domain on DriveLM-nuScenes, graph context degraded open-loop planning and behaviour accuracy relative to predicting the behaviour without context (ADE 1.39 $\rightarrow$ 1.74, collision rate 1.67 $\rightarrow$ 1.89, behaviour accuracy 61.45 $\rightarrow$ 57.49; Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch}). Its benefit appears when the test distribution departs from training: under the sensor shift from nuScenes to Waymo it improved speed decisions (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_waymo}), and on a new object class it helped once the graph contained a question written for that class (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_carla}). Both benefits are smaller, or depend more on human input, than the comparison with driving-specific baselines suggests, and the work has further important limitations.

\paragraph{Backbone versus graph.}
The generalisation results mix two effects: the knowledge of the pre-trained vision-language backbone and the structure of the graph. The baselines of this chapter separate them only in part. On Waymo, BLIP-RT-2, the same BLIP-2 network fine-tuned on the motion task without VQA data or context, reaches an ADE of 2.78, and DriveLM-Agent without context reaches 2.76, against 4.16 for UniAD-Single. Graph context lowers the ADE to 2.63, and prediction-only context to 2.62 (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:waymo_supp}). Most of the gap to UniAD-Single therefore comes with the pre-trained model, but it cannot be attributed to vision-language pre-training as such. The comparison is confounded by input asymmetry, since UniAD-Single loses its rear camera whereas DriveLM-Agent is trained and tested on the front camera only, and by model scale (3.955B against 131.9M parameters, Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:latency}), and no baseline isolates pre-training. The graph adds a smaller gain, concentrated in speed accuracy (43.90 $\rightarrow$ 54.29), and its effect on ADE is matched by the prediction QAs alone. In CARLA, the gain on unseen pedestrians requires a pedestrian-specific question, written with knowledge of which class was missing from training; it is an oracle-guided result that relies on an injected human prior. It is also obtained with a model co-trained on COCO and GQA, whose contribution is not ablated. With both, accuracy on pedestrian scenes reaches 27.04, while accuracy on regular scenes drops from 59.63 to 52.17; the oracle-context score of 92.35 is not an upper bound, because its context reveals that a person is present. All of these results come from single training runs, so their variance across seeds is unknown.

\paragraph{Baselines and metrics that were not evaluated.}
Several simple references that would sharpen these conclusions were not run; we list them as limitations, together with what each would test. First, no majority-class baseline was evaluated for the behaviour accuracies. DriveBench found that about 78.6\% of the behaviour answers in DriveLM-nuScenes are ``Going Straight'' (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:community}). If the steering classes of the validation split have a similar share, a constant \texttt{straight} predictor would reach about 78.6\% steering accuracy, above the Chain variant (75.34, Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:arch}) and the perception-only rows of Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:question_wise} (74.34--75.72). Majority-class accuracies for speed, steering and the joint behaviour on each evaluation set would show how far each accuracy exceeds the class prior; on DriveLM-CARLA-ped, a constant stop predictor would score 100 (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_carla}). Second, no blind baseline, which answers from the question alone, was evaluated; it would test whether the answers and behaviour decisions use the image at all. DriveBench later ran such a test on a DriveLM-Agent trained with the challenge recipe (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:community}), but not on the models of this chapter. Third, an ego-status-only baseline on the same key frames would test whether key-frame evaluation removes the open-loop shortcut (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:exp_arch}). Fourth, and most important for GVQA, no graph-aware consistency metric was evaluated. Such a metric would test the defining property of the task, that answers depend on their parents: for instance, the accuracy of child answers given correct and incorrect parent answers, the change in the behaviour decision when a parent answer is corrupted or removed, or the agreement between the safe actions named at the planning stage and the predicted behaviour. Because DriveLM-Metrics score every node separately, this chapter does not show that the behaviour answers of DriveLM-Agent are supported by its own perception and prediction answers. Fifth, repeated GPT scoring, or a fixed and versioned judge, would show whether GPT-score differences of one or two points, such as 71.39 against 72.51 in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:vqa}, exceed the variability of the judge.

\paragraph{Data quality and answer priors.}
The quality checks of DriveLM-nuScenes are described procedurally (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:data_nus}), and inter-annotator agreement and error rates are not reported. Key frames were selected by annotators as rich in scene information and potentially indicative of future state changes (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:nus_collection}), not by a rule that detects changes of intention, and the dominance of ``Going Straight'' among the behaviour answers shows that most of them do not involve a turn. The behaviour metrics are therefore computed on an unbalanced set of decisions, which favours models that follow the prior.

\paragraph{Efficiency constraints.}
Inheriting the drawbacks of LLMs, DriveLM-Agent suffers from long inference times, especially since it requires multiple rounds of prediction over the graph; it is roughly $10\times$ slower than UniAD (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:latency}). The core problem lies in the slow throughput of the current model, 8.5 tokens/s, which may hinder practical use. At the time of this work, however, LLM inference was becoming orders of magnitude faster~\cite{xiao2023smoothquant,shi2023crossget}, as it is a topic of broad interest, and we expect this rapid progress in orthogonal research to alleviate the issue. Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:questions} discusses possible remedies, such as executing only the final motion stage at every 20\,Hz simulation step of CARLA while running the other stages less often.

\paragraph{Driving-specific inputs.}
DriveLM-Agent directly applies the vision module of the VLM and takes a low-resolution front-view image as input. Driving-specific sensors such as LiDAR cannot be processed, which results in a lack of temporal information and of 360-degree scene understanding. The single front view also mismatches the annotation: the key objects of DriveLM-nuScenes are tagged in all six cameras (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:nus_composition}), so questions about objects outside the front view, if they are kept, can only be answered from the question text or from priors. \authorcheck{state whether questions about key objects outside the front camera were kept in the training and evaluation of DriveLM-Agent, and if so how many} Extending DriveLM-Agent to images from multiple views is straightforward, as the graph formulation allows different input frames for different nodes. Options for multi-modal and multi-frame inputs are left for future work.

\paragraph{Closed-loop planning.}
DriveLM-Agent is currently evaluated open-loop. In this setting, feeding the status of the ego vehicle as input can substantially improve the metrics without improving driving~\cite{li2024ego}, so we do not feed it to DriveLM-Agent; the original UniAD, which receives it through its BEV encoder, is shown only as a reference. The obstacle to closed-loop evaluation is cost rather than feasibility: CARLA's synchronous mode does not require real-time inference, but at 0.16 FPS (Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:latency}) querying the model at every 20\,Hz simulation step takes about 125\,s of computation per simulated second. Extending the work to closed-loop planning with an affordable budget of training time and computation is a promising direction; DriveLM-CARLA provides a foundation for research on closed-loop planning with VLMs, and later work has evaluated vision-language driving models in closed loop (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:subsequent}).

\paragraph{Question design.}
Determining which questions to ask is a critical aspect of DriveLM, yet this work, as a pioneering study of driving with VLMs, cannot recommend a detailed protocol; the choice relies on domain expertise, and in the pedestrian experiment it relied on knowing the shift in advance. Improving the $P_{1-3}$ QA performance under the current question setting is non-trivial, and we found that it had little effect on the final behaviour and motion performance. Likewise, graph reasoning does not bring very strong improvements in VQA. The simple prompting scheme, the relatively small base VLMs, insufficiently strong logical dependencies in the data, or a combination of these factors may be responsible; DriveLM-CARLA provides a platform to study them carefully (Appendix~\ref{drivelm:app:questions}).

\section{From Benchmark to Community}\label{drivelm:sec:community}

DriveLM was released with its data, models and an official evaluation server\footnote{\url{https://github.com/OpenDriveLab/DriveLM}} so that other researchers could build on it. Its most visible use was as a competition. DriveLM-nuScenes (version 1.1) formed the basis of the \emph{Driving with Language} track of the Autonomous Grand Challenge 2024\footnote{\url{https://opendrivelab.com/challenge2024/}}, which was held in conjunction with the CVPR 2024 Workshop on Foundation Models for Autonomous Systems; the author of this thesis was its lead organiser. The test questions were those of the DriveLM-nuScenes v1.1 validation split, with the ground-truth answers withheld, and were limited to multiple-choice, conversation and yes/no questions. \authorcheck{state whether the answers to these validation questions were ever public, for example in v1.0, and whether the experiments of this chapter used the same validation split} Submissions were ranked by a final score that weights a ChatGPT score by 0.4 and three further metrics by 0.2 each: language metrics (BLEU, ROUGE-L and CIDEr), a match score based on the predicted key-object coordinates, and accuracy. A LLaMA-Adapter V2~\cite{gao2023llamaadapterv2} baseline fine-tuned on DriveLM-nuScenes was provided as a starting point. The track attracted 152 teams from 14 countries and regions, who made 978 submissions, more teams and more submissions than any other of the seven tracks of the challenge. Its Innovation Award went to a team (ADLM) that aligned a spatially unified BEV feature with an LLM, in an end-to-end pipeline, to help the LLM comprehend spatial relationships. Beyond the challenge, PDM-Lite~\cite{beisswenger2024pdmlite} has been cited as one of the two experts commonly used to collect training data for CARLA Leaderboard 2.0~\cite{hamdan2025eta}.

The challenge also exposed the benchmark to scrutiny. DriveBench~\cite{xie2025vlms}, a follow-up study co-authored by the author of this thesis, asked whether the answers of VLMs on DriveLM-nuScenes are actually grounded in the images. It evaluated 12 VLMs, among them a DriveLM-Agent trained with the challenge recipe (LLaMA-Adapter V2), on 200 keyframes of DriveLM-nuScenes under clean inputs, 15 synthetic corruptions and a text-only setting with black images. Most models kept near-clean scores under corruption and even without any image: answering from text alone, GPT-4o retained about 95\% of its clean GPT score on open-ended prediction and planning questions, whereas human accuracy dropped sharply under corruption, for example from 93.33\% to 33.33\% on perception multiple-choice questions under fog. DriveBench attributes this apparent robustness to priors and textual cues rather than to visual evidence: about 78.6\% of the behaviour answers in DriveLM-nuScenes are ``Going Straight'', and the questions themselves reveal the camera and the pixel coordinates of the object they ask about. It also found that the metrics can mislead. Language metrics largely reward answer templates: the fine-tuned DriveLM model led GPT-4o by a wide margin in ROUGE-L on planning (53.12 \vs 6.54) but trailed Qwen2-VL-72B and GPT-4o in GPT score on prediction (44.33 \vs 49.35 and 51.30). GPT scores computed from only the ground truth and the prediction were highly homogeneous, and became more discriminative once task-specific rubrics, the question and the driving context were added.

For this chapter, the lesson is twofold. First, the language scores in Table~\ref{drivelm:tab:vqa} should be read with these caveats in mind: they rely on the kind of metrics that DriveBench shows can reward templates, including a GPT Score computed from only the ground truth and the prediction, and the behaviour answers of DriveLM-nuScenes are dominated by going straight, while the experiments of this chapter include no blind or majority-answer baseline (Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:discussion}). Second, and more generally, a reasoning benchmark must itself be stress-tested against degraded sensing and shortcut answers, because a model that ignores the image can look robust precisely because it ignores reality. DriveBench accordingly calls for balanced, context-aware datasets and for GPT-based evaluation with task-specific rubrics and driving context. Building a benchmark, opening it to the community through a challenge, and then auditing it is one turn of the benchmark-driven research cycle described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\subsection{Subsequent Developments (2023--2026)}\label{drivelm:sec:subsequent}

Work that appeared concurrently with DriveLM or after it changed three things: who decides how a driving decision is decomposed, how language is connected to action, and how such models are evaluated.

\paragraph{Decomposing the decision.}
GVQA fixes by hand how a driving decision is decomposed into questions. In general visual reasoning, VisProg and ViperGPT instead let a language model write a program that calls vision modules and returns their intermediate outputs as a rationale~\cite{gupta2023visprog,suris2023vipergpt}, and Visual CoT trains multimodal models to reason in steps grounded in image regions~\cite{shao2024visualcot}. In driving, several datasets annotate the reasoning itself. Reason2Drive, concurrent with DriveLM, builds chains of perception, prediction and reasoning steps and scores whether the steps support the final decision~\cite{nie2024reason2drive}; OmniDrive generates counterfactual reasoning data~\cite{wang2025omnidrive}; and DriveLMM-o1 annotates step-by-step reasoning for questions on nuScenes and scores the logical coherence of the steps as well as the final answer~\cite{ishaq2025drivelmmo1}. Evaluations of this kind address the gap noted in Section~\ref{drivelm:sec:discussion}: DriveLM scores each node of its graph separately and does not check that the answers are consistent along the edges.

\paragraph{From questions to actions.}
Most later systems produce actions with the same model that reasons. EMMA maps camera images directly to trajectories, objects and road-graph elements, and reports that chain-of-thought rationales improve its planning~\cite{hwang2025emma}. AutoVLA, like DriveLM-Agent, represents trajectories as discrete tokens of the language model, and adds reinforcement fine-tuning that reduces unnecessary reasoning in simple scenes~\cite{zhou2025autovla}. Alpamayo-R1 uses reinforcement learning to make its reasoning traces and its trajectories consistent~\cite{nvidia2025alpamayo}, the property that a graph-aware metric would check. Several of these systems were evaluated in closed loop, which this chapter did not do: ORION~\cite{fu2025orion} and AutoVLA on Bench2Drive~\cite{jia2024bench2drive}, and SimLingo, which won the 2024 CARLA Challenge~\cite{renz2025simlingo}. SimLingo also generates its VQA data in CARLA with DriveLM, evaluates on a harder validation set sampled uniformly over answer types rather than from the whole set, and tests whether its actions follow language instructions. Dual systems address the latency of models such as DriveLM-Agent by pairing a slow reasoning model with a fast planner~\cite{tian2024drivevlm,jiang2026senna,hamdan2025eta}, while FLARE (co-authored by the author) avoids text generation at test time altogether by activating a pre-trained VLM through self-supervised future DINOv2 feature prediction and group relative policy optimisation on NAVSIM~\cite{xie2026flare}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
Later benchmarks also changed how such models are scored. LingoQA replaced generic LLM scoring with Lingo-Judge, a learned classifier that estimates the correctness of an answer and correlates closely with human ratings~\cite{marcu2024lingoqa}, and DriveBench showed that GPT scores without rubrics are homogeneous (see above). For generalisation, WOD-E2E, the dataset of the 2025 Waymo vision-based end-to-end driving challenge, curates segments of rare long-tail scenarios and scores a trajectory against rater preferences rather than against the logged one~\cite{xu2026wode2e}; the Waymo test of this chapter, by contrast, uses randomly sampled validation frames and displacement errors. In this landscape, we read DriveLM as an early benchmark and data format for structured driving reasoning, whose challenge helped expose its own weaknesses. Its evidence on whether structured reasoning helps driving is open-loop, scored node by node and confounded; the field has since moved towards closed-loop evaluation, balanced answers and checks of the consistency between reasoning and action.

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{drivelm:sec:summary}

This chapter addressed RQ2: how the general knowledge of vision-language models can be grounded in driving in a structured and verifiable way, and whether such reasoning helps a driving agent generalise to unseen situations. We proposed DriveLM. Its task, Graph VQA, turns a driving decision into a graph of connected questions from perception through prediction and planning to behaviour and motion, so that every intermediate step of the decision is expressed in language that a person can read and check. DriveLM-nuScenes (4,871 frames with 91.4 QAs per frame on average, most of them written by human annotators) and DriveLM-CARLA (about 1.6M QAs generated with PDM-Lite, a rule-based expert developed for this purpose by Bei{\ss}wenger~\cite{beisswenger2024pdmlite}, which reaches an average driving score of 36.3 on the 20 official Leaderboard 2.0 validation routes, driven with three seeds each, where the previous expert reaches 2\%) instantiate the task, and DriveLM-Metrics evaluate each stage separately. DriveLM-Agent, a BLIP-2 model that passes answers along the graph as context and outputs trajectories as tokens, provides a deliberately simple baseline.

The central finding is that structured reasoning helped under distribution shift rather than in-domain, and by less than the comparison with driving-specific models suggests. On DriveLM-nuScenes, DriveLM-Agent is competitive with a single-frame UniAD (ADE 1.39 without context and 1.74 with graph context, against 1.80), but in-domain, graph context degraded open-loop planning and behaviour accuracy (ADE 1.39 $\rightarrow$ 1.74, collision rate 1.67 $\rightarrow$ 1.89, behaviour accuracy 61.45 $\rightarrow$ 57.49). Zero-shot on Waymo, DriveLM-Agent reaches an ADE of 2.63 against 4.16 for UniAD-Single, but most of this gap is already present without VQA training (BLIP-RT-2, 2.78) or context (2.76), and the comparison is confounded by input asymmetry (UniAD-Single loses its rear camera, whereas DriveLM-Agent uses only the front camera) and by model scale (3.955B against 131.9M parameters). Under this sensor shift, graph context improved speed decisions (speed accuracy 43.90 $\rightarrow$ 54.29), and prediction-only context matches its ADE (2.62). In CARLA, where pedestrians never appear in training, the behaviour accuracy of TransFuser++ falls from 70.19 to 8.72. DriveLM-Agent, co-trained on COCO and GQA, improves on pedestrian scenes only once a pedestrian-specific question is added to its graph, from 4.59 to 27.04, while its accuracy on regular scenes drops from 59.63 to 52.17. That question was written with knowledge of the missing class, so this oracle-guided result relies on an injected human prior; the 92.35 obtained with ground-truth context leaks the pedestrian answer and is not an upper bound. These CARLA experiments were led by co-authors, and all of the results in this chapter come from single runs. In-domain, the question-wise analysis suggests that prediction and planning QAs help the behaviour decision more than perception QAs alone, by margins of the order of the sampling uncertainty. For $P_{1-3}$ question answering, graph context raised the GPT Score in all four settings but did not raise every other metric, and the GPT Score was made the primary metric after the results were seen. The limitations are equally clear. DriveLM-Agent runs at 0.16 FPS (8.5 tokens/s, roughly ten times slower than UniAD), sees only a single low-resolution front view and is evaluated open-loop; no majority-class, blind or ego-status-only baseline and no graph-aware consistency metric were evaluated; and the later DriveBench audit showed that benchmarks of this kind must guard against answers driven by textual priors rather than by the scene.

Structured reasoning on top of a pre-trained vision-language model can help an agent under some shifts, but it does not make it infallible, and in this chapter its benefit on a new object class depended on a person who knew the shift. A deployed end-to-end planner, with or without language, will still meet out-of-distribution scenes in which its own confidence is misplaced, and nothing in its design lets it notice and correct this while driving. Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} turns from reasoning to acting and addresses this problem with test-time training, which lets a planner correct itself on the very scenes it encounters by reducing its own uncertainty. The five steering categories of the behaviour stage defined in this chapter reappear there as the clusters over which that uncertainty is measured.
