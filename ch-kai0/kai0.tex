In Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}, we examined non-reactive simulation over logged driving states and demonstrated that an end-to-end planner can adjust its frame-by-frame outputs at test time by reducing predictive uncertainty without ground-truth supervision~\cite{sima2024centaur}. Here we extend the Act pillar to a second physical domain. Research question RQ3(b) investigates how a learned policy can preserve reliability once deployment conditions diverge from training in \emph{real-world robot manipulation}. Physical realities pose distinct obstacles in this setting. Handling deformable cloth involves contact-rich dynamics over a high-dimensional configuration space starting from arbitrary crumpled states. Human teleoperation demonstrations are mutually inconsistent and sample only a small fraction of valid solution trajectories. Both data acquisition and computation are constrained: recording expert teleoperation consumes robot and operator hours, while post-training a large vision-language-action (VLA) backbone requires substantial GPU time. Moreover, policies trained offline on recorded trajectories must act on physical hardware, where control delays and mechanical bounds cause executed motions to diverge from model predictions.

We posit that under such practical resource constraints, data and model scale is not the sole bottleneck to reliable execution; a central barrier lies in the discrepancy among three probability distributions across the robot learning loop: the distribution of human expert demonstrations $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, the conditional action distribution learned by the policy $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ at each state, and the trajectory distribution physically realised on the robot at test time, $P_{\mathrm{test}}$. Within the taxonomy of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, this mismatch represents a deployment shift between training conditions and test-time execution. Expressed in the notation of Section~\ref{sec:method:framework}, $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ maps to $p_{\mathrm{train}}$ while $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ corresponds to the physically executed component of $p_{\mathrm{deploy}}$, and $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ reflects both the demonstration data and the architectural bias of the policy family. Throughout this chapter, these three distributions act as an analytical framework for structuring our method rather than directly estimated densities (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:distributions}). Instead of brute-force expansion of unfiltered demonstrations or network parameters, we pair each mismatch between the three distributions with a dedicated alignment mechanism, including targeted DAgger-style recovery collection.

Our resulting framework, \chiz,\footnote{Project page: \url{https://mmlab.hk/research/kai0}. Code: \url{https://github.com/OpenDriveLab/KAI0}.} comprises three modules. First, \emph{Model Arithmetic} (MA) trains separate policies on disjoint partitions of the demonstration corpus and combines their parameters via convex interpolation, tuning the mixing weights against validation loss on out-of-distribution DAgger recovery rollouts; this procedure seeks to reconcile $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ with $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ without expanding the demonstration pool, though acquiring the validation rollouts itself entails DAgger collection. Second, \emph{Stage Advantage} (SA), developed by co-author Lirui Zhao (see the contribution statement above), estimates advantage directly from observation pairs conditioned on the semantic stage of a multi-step task, converting the estimate into a binary optimality indicator for advantage-weighted behaviour cloning; this direct formulation yields a less noisy signal than subtracting independent value predictions and guides $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ towards productive actions during execution. Third, \emph{Train--Deploy Alignment} (TDA) shifts $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ closer to $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ via heuristic DAgger collection and spatio-temporal augmentation, while mitigating inference-to-actuation delay at test time through temporal chunk-wise smoothing.

We test the approach on bimanual collaborative garment manipulation across two dual-arm platforms, covering cloth flattening, folding, conditional sorting and rack hanging. End-to-end evaluation against the base policy is performed on Task~A (T-shirt flattening and folding), whereas Tasks~B and~C serve for module-level ablations. Across 30 real-robot rollouts per configuration on Task~A, task completion increases from roughly 27\% (about 8 of 30 trials) for the $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline to roughly 97\% (about 29 of 30) for \chiz, corresponding to a relative gain of about 250\% (Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA}). This gap far exceeds binomial sampling variability for 30 trials, with approximate 95\% Wilson confidence intervals of 14--44\% versus 83--99\%. Numerical metrics reported in the text and tables are taken from the plotted evaluation summaries (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:protocol}). These gains are achieved by post-training a pre-trained $\pi_{0.5}$ backbone using roughly 20 hours of demonstrations per task on 8 A100 GPUs. Because we fine-tune an existing foundation policy rather than training from scratch, we characterise the recipe as resource-aware, while noting that exact GPU, robot and operator hour totals are not logged separately (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:limitations}). Since data scale was not varied under a fixed resource budget and the single largest performance jump stems from targeted DAgger recovery trajectories (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_tda}), the proposition that distributional alignment matters alongside raw scale remains an empirical hypothesis supported by the integrated Task~A benchmark.

\section{Motivation}\label{kai0:sec:intro}

Reliable deployment in open-world environments remains a central goal for robotics. In autonomous driving, wheeled navigation agents now operate routinely across dense urban traffic~\cite{Kusano2025waymocrash,tesla2025fsd,waymo2025blog}. Bringing comparable dependability and user trust to physical manipulation in unstructured settings remains unresolved. A manipulation controller must act over a high-dimensional action space while tolerating wide physical variations in object geometry and lighting. To cope with this complexity, recent industry efforts have prioritised scale, training larger foundation backbones on massive compute clusters~\cite{black2024pi0,black2025pi05,amin2026pistar06,generalist2025gen0,sunday2025act1,zheng2025xvla,beingbeyond2026beingh05} and assembling large-scale teleoperation repositories such as AgiBot World~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros} (co-authored by the author), which contains over one million trajectories gathered across more than 100 physical robots.

While model capacity and dataset volume are indispensable, we argue that raw scale is not the sole determinant of dependable execution: in high-dimensional physical environments, a critical barrier to reliability is \emph{the distributional mismatch across the three stages of the robot learning workflow: data acquisition, policy training and hardware execution}. Such mismatches affect not only binary task completion, but also motion continuity, overall task throughput and the frequency of corrective retries required per episode~\cite{chen2025benchmarking,bu2025agibot_iros,shi2025diversity,amin2026pistar06,jiang2025behavior}. To structure our analysis, we characterise the learning loop through three distributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\bm{P_{\mathrm{train}}}$, representing the empirical distribution of human teleoperation trajectories collected for imitation learning;
  \item $\bm{Q_{\mathrm{model}}}$, denoting the action distribution conditioned on state learned from $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ (Eq.~\eqref{kai0:eq:qmodel}), which encodes both the training demonstrations and the inductive bias of the network architecture;
  \item $\bm{P_{\mathrm{test}}}$, capturing the trajectory distribution physically executed on the robot at test time, which departs from raw policy predictions due to inference latency and low-level controller constraints.
\end{itemize}

During physical rollouts, observed failure modes cluster into three recurring mismatches among these distributions (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:teaser}). First, because deformable manipulation unfolds in a vast state space, $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ covers only a sparse subset of valid solution paths, biasing $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ towards narrow demonstration modes. Second, communication and computation delays between policy evaluation ($Q_{\mathrm{model}}$) and actuator execution ($P_{\mathrm{test}}$) create a temporal offset that degrades otherwise valid action sequences at run time~\cite{black2025rtc,tang2025vlash}. Third, because expert demonstrations rarely contain error states, the learned policy struggles to self-correct: even when starting near the support of $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, small execution disturbances in $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ can initiate compounding errors from which the controller fails to recover~\cite{hu2025rac,pan2026sop,amin2026pistar06}.

In general machine learning, researchers counter distributional shift via dataset expansion~\cite{wiles2022fine,higgins2017beta,radford2021learning}, synthetic or learned data augmentation~\cite{wiles2022fine,cubuk2020randaugment,cubuk2019autoaugment} and test-time or domain adaptation~\cite{wiles2022fine,liu2021just,schneider2020improving}. Directly transferring these recipes to physical manipulation is difficult because teleoperated demonstrations are costly to record, inference latency cannot be ignored during closed-loop control, and fine-tuning large VLA models requires substantial compute.

\chiz addresses these three discrepancies under practical robotics budgets, pairing each distributional gap with a dedicated module (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:teaser}, bottom):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item \textbf{Model Arithmetic (MA)} seeks to better align the trained policy ($Q_{\mathrm{model}}$) with the demonstration distribution ($P_{\mathrm{train}}$). By interpolating parameters across checkpoints trained on disjoint splits of $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, it combines behavioural modes that a single fine-tuning run may overlook.
  \item \textbf{Stage Advantage (SA)}, contributed by co-author Lirui Zhao, guides the policy ($Q_{\mathrm{model}}$) towards actions that advance task completion during deployment ($P_{\mathrm{test}}$). It partitions a multi-step task into semantic stages and supplies stage-conditioned advantage weights for behaviour cloning~\cite{peng2019awr}. Trained on the recorded trajectories, it produces a less noisy supervision signal than a value-difference baseline patterned after $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$~\cite{amin2026pistar06} (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}).
  \item \textbf{Train--Deploy Alignment (TDA)} broadens $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ towards $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ using heuristic DAgger recovery trajectories and spatio-temporal augmentation to cover states frequently encountered at test time. At execution time, it applies temporal chunk-wise smoothing to blend successive action chunks and counteract inference delay, achieving higher throughput than RTC-only control~\cite{black2025rtc} in two out of three augmentation regimes on Task~A (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_tda}).
\end{enumerate}

We benchmark \chiz on multi-stage collaborative cloth manipulation tasks involving flattening, folding and hanging diverse garments. Deformable cloth dynamics and frequent recovery from crumpled states expose all three distributional gaps. Our empirical findings across the three tasks are summarised below; all numbers reflect 30 evaluation trials per setting taken from the plotted summaries, where differences of up to four trials fall within binomial sampling noise (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:protocol}).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Integrated system.} On Task~A, combining all three modules lifts $\pi_{0.5}$ completion from roughly 27\% to roughly 97\%. While this end-to-end gain supports our alignment framework, we did not benchmark against $\pi_{0.5}$ trained on an equal total volume of unaugmented demonstrations, so the experiment does not isolate distributional alignment from total data volume.
  \item \textbf{Train--Deploy Alignment.} Recovery trajectories collected via DAgger provide the largest individual boost: on Task~A, heuristic or standard DAgger elevates $\pi_{0.5}$ completion from roughly 23\% to 87--93\%, and on Task~C from roughly 10\% to 43--53\%. This improvement stems from targeted recovery collection. Heuristic DAgger increases the average number of retries in all tested settings, whereas standard DAgger keeps retry counts nearly unchanged except for $\pi_{0}$ on Task~A. Spatio-temporal augmentation alone shows no consistent advantage on Task~A. Temporal chunk-wise smoothing outperforms RTC in completion rate under two of three augmentation conditions on Task~A while incurring the highest retry counts in two conditions; pairing chunk-wise smoothing with RTC yields the strongest unaugmented control setup.
  \item \textbf{Model Arithmetic.} On Task~C, all weight-merged policies surpass both the best single-split checkpoint and the full-data checkpoint in mean completion rate and throughput, though only the top-performing merge strategy exceeds the full-data baseline beyond sampling variability, and certain merge rules increase retry counts. On Tasks~A and~B, weight merging improves over baselines in the majority of settings. When applied in isolation to $\pi_{0.5}$ on Task~A, MA leaves completion nearly unchanged (roughly 30\% versus 27\%) and reduces the milestone score (roughly 35 versus 41), whereas combining MA with TDA or SA adds roughly 30 percentage points in completion rate. Using DAgger rollouts for validation produces the strongest merged checkpoint on Task~C, though its margin over in-domain validation remains within sampling noise there and varies across Tasks~A and~B.
  \item \textbf{Stage Advantage.} Across all three tasks, Stage Advantage produces smoother temporal advantage estimates than the value-difference baseline. Temporal smoothness does not guarantee accuracy against ground-truth progress, which was not evaluated on held-out annotations. In policy rollouts, completion rises on Task~A (roughly 80\% versus 67\%, within sampling variability); on Task~B, Stage Advantage reduces retry count and raises milestone score but trails in completion rate (roughly 17\% versus 23\%) and throughput; on Task~C, completion rate, throughput and milestone score all decline.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{kai0:sec:related}

\subsection{Imitation Learning and Real-World Policy Deployment}\label{kai0:sec:related_il}
Behaviour cloning from expert demonstrations has become a standard recipe for robotic manipulation, spanning compact transformer controllers~\cite{zhao2023aloha,chi2024umi,chi2023diffusion,zeng2024learning} as well as large VLA backbones pre-trained on broad robot corpora~\cite{brohan2023rt1,zitkovich2023rt2,kim2024openvla,team2024octo,bu2025univla,black2025pi05,amin2026pistar06}. Within this family, the $\pi$ models~\cite{black2024pi0,black2025pi05,amin2026pistar06} achieve strong transfer by leveraging large-scale multi-robot pre-training. Assembling such demonstration repositories~\cite{khazatsky2024droid,oneill2024openx,bu2025agibot_iros,jiang2025galaxea,walke2023bridgedata} nevertheless requires major hardware and operator investment. To improve sample efficiency, prior studies have investigated interactive DAgger aggregation~\cite{ross2011dagger,kelly2019hgdagger,hu2025rac,amin2026pistar06} alongside visual and kinematic augmentation~\cite{laskin2020reinforcement,kostrikov2020image,yu2023scaling,li2025gr}. Standard DAgger, however, depends on continuous human monitoring during live policy execution~\cite{ross2011dagger}, keeping collection labour-intensive. Physical execution introduces an additional hurdle: communication and forward-pass delays create a discrepancy between predicted action chunks and executed joint trajectories. Existing controllers counter this delay via run-time trajectory blending or inpainting~\cite{zhao2023aloha,black2025rtc,tang2025vlash}, often trading off computational overhead. Consequently, prior solutions typically treat data collection, policy fitting or run-time control in isolation rather than jointly aligning distributions across the full robot learning loop~\cite{chen2025retaining,tajwar2024preference}. Here we formulate these challenges through the triplet $(P_{\mathrm{train}}, Q_{\mathrm{model}}, P_{\mathrm{test}})$ and design \chiz to address all three pairwise gaps.

\subsection{Model Merging and Weight Interpolation}\label{kai0:sec:related_merge}
Weight-space interpolation provides a compute-efficient mechanism for combining capabilities across multiple trained networks. Studies in vision and natural language processing established that averaging parameters across checkpoints trained with varied hyper-parameters~\cite{wortsman2022soups} or across task-specific fine-tuning runs~\cite{wortsman2022soups,ilharco2023task,yadav2023ties} can enhance out-of-distribution robustness. Subsequent work has adapted parameter merging to large language models~\cite{Akiba2024EvolutionaryOO,Yang2024ModelMI,Team2025KimiKS}, motion planning~\cite{Lee2025InteractionMergedMP} and embodied control~\cite{wang2024fleet}. Most existing recipes tune interpolation coefficients on in-distribution validation splits, which poorly reflect the state shifts encountered during contact-rich manipulation. Concurrent work on RETAIN~\cite{Yadav2025RobustFO} merges VLA checkpoints to preserve general pre-trained skills during downstream adaptation. In contrast, our Model Arithmetic module targets the coverage bias that arises when a policy is fine-tuned on a small set of demonstrations. We select interpolation weights using an out-of-distribution validation set composed of DAgger recovery trajectories, favouring parameter combinations that handle states absent from normal demonstrations, and we systematically compare uniform averaging, inverse-loss weighting, gradient-based weight optimisation and greedy soup construction.

\subsection{Advantage Estimation for Long-Horizon Tasks}\label{kai0:sec:related_adv}
Conditioning imitation policies on returns, value estimates or advantages is a well-established strategy for steering long-horizon decision-making~\cite{schmidhuber2019reinforcement,emmons2022rvs,zheng2022online,wu2023elastic,kuba2023advantage}. Advantage-weighted regression (AWR), in particular, reweights behaviour cloning updates towards high-advantage transitions~\cite{peng2019awr}. Extending this principle to VLA models, $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$ fits a distributional value network to compute state-action advantages for conditioned policy training~\cite{amin2026pistar06}. In practice, deriving advantages by subtracting two separate value estimates, $V(s') - V(s)$, often suffers from high variance because independent frame-level prediction errors compound over multi-stage rollouts. Stage Advantage, developed by co-author Lirui Zhao for \chiz, mitigates this variance by regressing relative progress directly from paired visual observations while conditioning on discrete semantic task stages. This joint prediction yields a smoother temporal signal (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}) that is then thresholded into a binary optimality label for policy training~\cite{chen2025robot,amin2026pistar06}.

\section{Distributional Inconsistencies in Robot Learning}\label{kai0:sec:problem}

We now set out the formal notation for the three distributions introduced in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:intro} and specify the three mismatches targeted by \chiz. As noted in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:distributions}, these definitions provide a structured vocabulary for the alignment objectives of each module rather than empirically estimated densities.

\subsection{Preliminaries}\label{kai0:sec:prelim}
Let the manipulation task be modelled as a finite-horizon Markov decision process (MDP) with state space $\mathcal{S}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$ and episode horizon $H$. A rollout trajectory $\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots, s_H)$ is governed by physical transition dynamics $s_{t+1} \sim T(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t, \xi)$ under environment parameters $\xi$ and initial state distribution $\mu(s_0)$. Under a stochastic policy $\pi(a \mid s; \phi)$, the resulting distribution over trajectories takes the standard form~\cite{schulman2015trust,levine2018reinforcement}
\begin{equation}
  P_{\pi}(\tau) = \mu(s_0) \prod_{t=0}^{H-1} \pi(a_t \mid s_t; \phi)\, T(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t, \xi),
  \label{kai0:eq:traj_distrib}
\end{equation}
which we denote simply by $P$ whenever the underlying policy $\pi$ is clear from context.

\subsection{Three Distributions of the Robot Learning Cycle}\label{kai0:sec:distributions}
To establish a reference, let $P_{\mathrm{real}}$ denote the target distribution over trajectories that successfully solve the task. Writing $\mathcal{G}$ for the set of successful trajectories,
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{real}}(\tau) \propto \mathbf{1}\{\tau \in \mathcal{G}\}
  \label{kai0:eq:preal}
\end{equation}
defines the support of valid action sequences that achieve task completion under the physical environment dynamics. Relative to $P_{\mathrm{real}}$, we define the three distributions across the learning loop that \chiz seeks to align.

\paragraph{Training distribution.} We denote by $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ the empirical distribution induced by human teleoperation on the physical robot, from which the finite demonstration dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{\tau^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ is sampled.

\paragraph{Model distribution.} In what follows, $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ refers to the learned policy distribution, $Q_{\mathrm{model}} \triangleq \pi(a \mid s; \hat{\phi})$, fitted by maximising log-likelihood $\sum_{t} \log \pi(a_t \mid s_t; \phi)$ across the recorded demonstrations $\mathcal{D}$~\cite{ross2011dagger,osa2018algorithmic}:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\phi} = \argmax_{\phi} \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{D}} \sum_{t} \log \pi(a_t \mid s_t; \phi).
  \label{kai0:eq:qmodel}
\end{equation}
How $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ extrapolates outside $\mathcal{D}$ is governed by the architectural priors and pre-trained initialisation of the policy class, which are intrinsic to the learned policy itself.

\paragraph{Test distribution.} Physical rollout on the robot generates $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ by coupling $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ with an execution operator $I(\tilde{a}_t \mid a_t, s_t)$, where $\tilde{a}_t$ is the command physically applied to the actuators:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{test}}(\tau) = \mu(s_0) \prod_{t=0}^{H-1} \pi(a_t \mid s_t; \hat{\phi})\, \tilde{T}(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t, \xi),
  \label{kai0:eq:ptest}
\end{equation}
with effective transition kernel
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{T}(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t, \xi) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{a}_t \sim I(\cdot \mid a_t, s_t)} \big[ T(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, \tilde{a}_t, \xi) \big].
  \label{kai0:eq:ttilde}
\end{equation}
This operator $I$ captures the discrepancy noted in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:intro} between predicted commands and physically realised motions under inference latency and controller limits.

\paragraph{Status of the formalism.} We do not explicitly fit density estimators for $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ or $P_{\mathrm{test}}$, nor do we compute statistical divergences or support-coverage metrics among them before and after each module. Phrases such as ``aligning $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ with $P_{\mathrm{train}}$'' therefore express the design rationale of each component rather than a directly quantified divergence reduction, and the t-SNE projections in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:consistency} serve as qualitative visualisations. Directly measuring these shifts, for instance via action likelihoods on held-out DAgger states or embedding-space coverage ratios between test rollouts and training demonstrations, remains an open direction for subsequent work.

\subsection{Three Systematic Inconsistencies}\label{kai0:sec:inconsistencies}
Failures observed during physical evaluation fall into three recurring mismatches among the three distributions across different stages of the learning loop:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \textbf{Coverage deficiency.} Because $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ samples the high-dimensional target manifold $P_{\mathrm{real}}$ sparsely, $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ becomes overfitted to the narrow modes present in the collected demonstrations.
  \item \textbf{Temporal mismatch.} Multi-stage tasks often exhibit visually similar cloth configurations at distinct stages of completion, causing $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ to confuse temporal context; concurrently, communication and inference delays introduce an execution offset between predicted and applied commands. Both effects surface in $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ as stalled motion or stage errors.
  \item \textbf{Failure cascade.} Since unperturbed demonstrations in $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ rarely include corrective manoeuvres, the policy lacks self-recovery skills once minor disturbances push trajectories off course in $P_{\mathrm{test}}$.
\end{enumerate}
The three modules of \chiz target these mismatches in turn, as detailed next.

\section{Method}\label{kai0:sec:method}

\subsection{Overview of the Framework}\label{kai0:sec:pipeline}
To counter the three mismatches identified in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:inconsistencies} under practical compute and data budgets, \chiz combines three modules across the learning pipeline (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:pipeline}). Model Arithmetic (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:ma}) broadens policy coverage in parameter space by interpolating checkpoints trained on disjoint data splits. Stage Advantage (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:sa}) mitigates temporal ambiguity during policy fitting through stage-conditioned advantage estimation, yielding less noisy supervision across multi-step episodes. Train--Deploy Alignment (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:tda}) links physical execution back to training by combining run-time chunk smoothing with targeted recovery collection and data augmentation.

\subsection{Model Arithmetic}\label{kai0:sec:ma}
When initial teleoperation budgets are modest, $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ exhibits sparse state coverage, biasing a single fine-tuned policy towards narrow manipulation habits. While collecting orders of magnitude more demonstrations could eventually bring $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ closer to $P_{\mathrm{real}}$, doing so for bimanual garment manipulation demands prohibitive robot and operator time. This motivates a practical question: \emph{how can we reduce coverage bias in the learned policy without collecting additional raw demonstrations?}

Model Arithmetic (MA) addresses this by interpolating the parameters of checkpoints trained on disjoint subsets of the demonstration corpus, using held-out validation loss to choose the mixing weights. Unlike mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures that require learned routing networks and specialised training objectives~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch}, or output-level ensembles that multiply inference cost by running multiple networks in parallel~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017simple}, MA collapses multiple checkpoints into a single parameter vector at zero extra run-time cost. Specifically, we partition the training dataset $\mathcal{D}$ randomly into $n$ disjoint splits $\{\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2, \dots, \mathcal{D}_n\}$ drawn from $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, fine-tune an independent policy checkpoint $\theta_i$ on each split, and form the merged parameter vector via convex combination:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{\mathrm{merged}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i \theta_i, \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad \alpha_i \ge 0, \quad \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i = 1.
  \label{kai0:eq:arith}
\end{equation}
Here the mixing weights $\{\alpha_i\}$ are chosen to minimise validation loss on a held-out split, producing a single deployed policy $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ parameterised by $\theta_{\mathrm{merged}}$. The underlying intuition is that checkpoints fitted to distinct data partitions may capture complementary behavioural modes across the solution manifold; we therefore evaluate several strategies for selecting the interpolation coefficients.

\paragraph{Out-of-distribution validation.} A key factor in weight merging is how the validation set is constructed. Rather than evaluating checkpoints on in-domain subsets drawn from the same demonstration distribution, we assemble an out-of-distribution (OOD) validation set that probes recovery states absent from the initial demonstrations. Concretely, we gather DAgger recovery rollouts~\cite{ross2011dagger,kelly2019hgdagger} by executing the subset-trained policies on the physical robot with human corrective interventions. While MA does not expand the primary training splits, acquiring this OOD validation split does involve physical rollouts and operator supervision, a collection cost noted in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:limitations}.

\paragraph{Merging strategies.} Using this validation split, we benchmark four weight-souping schemes~\cite{wortsman2022soups} for computing $\theta_{\mathrm{merged}}$ (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:arithmetic}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Average weighting}~\cite{wortsman2022soups} sets equal coefficients $\alpha_i = 1/n$ across all $n$ checkpoints.
  \item \emph{Inverse-loss weighting}~\cite{maiti2025souper} scales each weight in inverse proportion to the validation loss $L_i$ of checkpoint $i$ prior to normalisation:
  \begin{equation}
    \alpha_i \propto \frac{1}{(L_i + \delta)^{p}},
    \label{kai0:eq:invloss}
  \end{equation}
  where $\delta$ and $p$ are scalar hyper-parameters.
  \item \emph{Gradient descent}~\cite{ilharco2023task}, along with an adaptive-step variant, parameterises the simplex weights through a softmax transformation and directly minimises the validation loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}$ of the interpolated network $\theta_{\mathrm{merged}}$ via gradient updates:
  \begin{equation}
    \bm{\alpha} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{w}), \qquad \min_{\mathbf{w}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}\Big(\sum_{i} \alpha_i \theta_i\Big).
    \label{kai0:eq:gd}
  \end{equation}
  \item \emph{Greedy search}~\cite{wortsman2022soups} builds the soup incrementally, retaining at each step the candidate checkpoint that yields the largest reduction in validation loss when uniformly averaged with the current active set.
\end{itemize}
By selecting convex combinations in parameter space against held-out recovery data, MA seeks to consolidate behavioural modes from separate data splits into one policy without enlarging the demonstration set. We compare these four schemes empirically in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_ma} and provide further implementation notes in Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:ma}.

\subsection{Stage Advantage}\label{kai0:sec:sa}
Stage Advantage was designed by co-author Lirui Zhao (see the contribution statement) and is included here to give a complete account of the integrated \chiz pipeline. Whereas Model Arithmetic targets coverage bias in $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$, a merged policy can still falter during multi-stage rollouts in $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ due to temporal ambiguity. When visually similar cloth states occur in different phases of a manipulation sequence, the policy may trigger actions appropriate for an earlier or later phase, accumulating errors across long horizons. Resolving this ambiguity requires a progress signal that evaluates action utility relative to the active phase of the task~\cite{chen2025robot}. The central design question is thus \emph{how to construct a low-variance, stage-aware progress signal for long-horizon policy learning}.

\paragraph{Advantage as a difference of values.} Prior work such as $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$~\cite{amin2026pistar06} pairs advantage-weighted regression~\cite{wu2023elastic,kuba2023advantage} with an implicit advantage estimate computed as the difference between two separate state-value predictions:
\begin{equation}
  A(s, a) = V(s') - V(s).
  \label{kai0:eq:adv_vdiff}
\end{equation}
Subtracting two independently regressed values compounds per-frame prediction noise, producing high-variance weights during training. In addition, predicting a single global progress scalar $V(s)$ without stage conditioning becomes ill-posed whenever distinct stages of a task produce visually similar observations.

\paragraph{Direct advantage.} To reduce temporal noise in the supervision signal, Stage Advantage regresses the transition advantage directly from a pair of observations:
\begin{equation}
  A(s, a) = f_{\theta}(s, s'),
  \label{kai0:eq:adv_direct}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\theta}$ outputs the relative progress between $s$ and $s'$. Although the ground-truth training target is still defined as the difference in normalised timestamp progress between $s'$ and $s$ (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:sa}), predicting that difference jointly with a single network alters the error structure. Specifically, if two separate value calls incur errors $e$ and $e'$, the difference $V(s') - V(s)$ has error variance $\operatorname{Var}(e) + \operatorname{Var}(e') - 2\operatorname{Cov}(e, e')$ with $\operatorname{Cov}(e, e') \approx 0$; a joint estimator conditioned on both frames $(s, s')$ can induce positive error correlation across the pair, lowering the resulting variance. We evaluate this smoothness effect empirically in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}. Architecturally, $f_{\theta}$ is implemented as a VLM backbone that ingests paired camera frames (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:pipeline}), with training pairs formed by sampling variable step offsets $\Delta$ and setting $s' = s_{t+\Delta}$ so the estimator does not overfit to a single time stride.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.58\linewidth]{ch-kai0/images/advantage_v4_1_compress.pdf}
  \caption[Cumulative value based on Stage Advantage]{\textbf{Cumulative progress curves from Stage Advantage.} Green and red shading indicate positive and negative predicted advantage, respectively. \textbf{Top}: Task~A trace illustrating a cloth slip followed by recovery. \textbf{Middle}: Task~B trace showing garment retrieval and placement correction. \textbf{Bottom}: Task~C trace under rack pull-over and partial occlusion.}
  \label{kai0:fig:advantage}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Stage conditioning.} To eliminate ambiguity when similar visual states recur across different phases of a task, Stage Advantage partitions each long-horizon task into $S$ sequential semantic sub-goals, referred to as stages. Rather than scoring progress across the entire episode at once, the network conditions its prediction on the active stage indicator $g$:
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{stage}}(s, a, g) = f_{\theta}(s, s' \mid g), \qquad g \in \Big\{0, \frac{1}{S}, \dots, \frac{S-1}{S}\Big\}.
  \label{kai0:eq:adv_stage}
\end{equation}
Here $g$ is a normalised scalar obtained from manual stage boundaries across the $S$ stages (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:pipeline}). Figure~\ref{kai0:fig:advantage} illustrates the resulting cumulative progress traces across the three benchmark tasks of Section~\ref{kai0:sec:tasks}.

\paragraph{Binary optimality indicator.} In line with~\cite{chen2025robot,amin2026pistar06}, the continuous stage-conditioned advantage is converted into a binary optimality label via thresholding:
\begin{equation}
  I = \mathbf{1}[A_{\mathrm{stage}} > \epsilon],
  \label{kai0:eq:indicator}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ partitions transitions into productive and non-productive updates. Rather than fixing a constant cutoff, we set $\epsilon$ by quantile ranking: transitions are ordered by predicted advantage and the upper fraction $q = 0.3$ is assigned $I = 1$, placing $\epsilon$ at the 70th percentile of predicted values (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:sa}). During policy training, this indicator upweights demonstration segments in $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ that advance the active sub-goal, aiming to reduce stalling and repetitive motions during deployment ($P_{\mathrm{test}}$); we assess its impact on task-level metrics in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}. Pair sampling, quantile selection and task stage definitions are detailed in Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:sa}.

\subsection{Train--Deploy Alignment}\label{kai0:sec:tda}
Even when a policy is well regularised over multi-step horizons, deploying it on physical hardware introduces discrepancies between $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{test}}$. Forward-pass computation and communication delays shift the timing of executed commands and allow tracking drift to accumulate. Action-chunking controllers are particularly vulnerable to this delay: the pause between finishing one inference pass and starting the next disrupts velocity and position continuity at chunk boundaries, producing jerky transitions that destabilise cloth contact. Whereas prior controllers smooth or condition chunks at run time~\cite{zhao2023aloha,black2025rtc,tang2025vlash}, \chiz introduces \emph{temporal chunk-wise smoothing} to maintain continuous actuation across chunk updates (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:consistency}, bottom left).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.68\linewidth]{ch-kai0/images/consistency_v4.pdf}
  \caption[Train--Deploy Alignment strategies and t-SNE visualisations]{\textbf{Train--Deploy Alignment mechanisms and qualitative t-SNE projections.} \textbf{Left}: the three alignment mechanisms bridging training and execution. \textbf{Right}: qualitative t-SNE projections illustrating how the trajectory distributions overlap more closely as each mechanism is added.}
  \label{kai0:fig:consistency}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Temporal chunk-wise smoothing.} Denote by $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}$ the active execution buffer holding unexecuted actions from the preceding inference step, and by $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}$ the action chunk just returned by the model. We track the number of steps already consumed from the active buffer via an index $k$, discard actions rendered obsolete during inference up to a ceiling $d_{\max}$, and enforce a minimum blending window $m_{\min}$. Algorithm~\ref{kai0:alg:chunk_smoothing} summarises the update rule. Once the stale prefix of length $d = \min(k, d_{\max})$ is removed from $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}$, the remaining suffix $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{rem}}$ is linearly cross-faded with $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}$ over $L$ overlapping steps via decaying weights $w_i$, ensuring a smooth transition from the ongoing trajectory to the newly planned chunk.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
  \caption{Temporal chunk-wise smoothing}\label{kai0:alg:chunk_smoothing}
  \KwIn{current buffer $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}$, consumption index $k$, new chunk $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}$, drop threshold $d_{\max}$, minimum overlap $m_{\min}$}
  \KwOut{updated buffer $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{buf}}$ and reset index $k \leftarrow 0$}
  $d \leftarrow \min(k, d_{\max})$\tcp*{stale prefix length}
  \If{$d \ge |\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}|$}{
    \Return{$\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}, k$}\tcp*{skip outdated chunk}
  }
  $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{rem}} \leftarrow (a^{\mathrm{new}}_{d}, \dots)$\tcp*{unconsumed new actions}
  \If{$|\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}| < m_{\min}$}{
    pad $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}$ by repeating its final command\;
  }
  $L \leftarrow \min(|\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}|, |\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{rem}}|)$\tcp*{blending window}
  \For{$i \leftarrow 0$ \KwTo $L-1$}{
    $w_i \leftarrow 1 - i/\max(L-1, 1)$\;
    $\tilde{a}_i \leftarrow w_i\, a^{\mathrm{old}}_i + (1 - w_i)\, a^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{rem},i}$\;
  }
  $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{buf}} \leftarrow (\tilde{a}_0, \dots, \tilde{a}_{L-1}) \,\Vert\, (\text{suffix of } \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{rem}})$\;
  $k \leftarrow 0$\tcp*{reset buffer index}
  \Return{$\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{buf}}, k$}\;
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Relation to other execution methods.} Temporal chunk-wise smoothing performs a linear cross-fade between the unexecuted tail of the previous buffer and the incoming chunk. Unlike the temporal ensembling scheme of ACT~\cite{zhao2023aloha}, which runs a forward pass at every control tick and takes an exponentially weighted average over all historical predictions covering the current step, chunk-wise smoothing interpolates only two consecutive buffers without increasing query frequency. It also differs from RTC~\cite{black2025rtc}, which formulates chunk generation as an inpainting problem conditioned on the actions committed during model evaluation: chunk-wise smoothing leaves the network forward pass untouched, operates purely as a post-processing filter on joint commands, and accounts for latency by trimming the stale prefix. Because RTC acts during sampling and chunk-wise smoothing acts on the output buffer, the two techniques are orthogonal and can be used together (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_tda}).

\paragraph{Closing the loop from deployment to training.} Alongside run-time smoothing, we ask: \emph{how can failure states encountered in $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ be folded back into $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ with minimal collection overhead?} Because ordinary demonstrations rarely visit error states, policies trained solely on nominal trajectories are prone to compounding failures. \chiz closes the deployment-to-training loop through two data-side mechanisms:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Standard on-policy DAgger~\cite{ross2011dagger,kelly2019hgdagger} pushes $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ into error-prone regions of the state space, yet waiting for rollouts to fail organically consumes substantial robot time. Under \emph{heuristic DAgger}, operators instead place the garment directly into handcrafted fault configurations (such as slipped grasps or half-dropped cloth) and record human recovery trajectories without running the learned policy (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:consistency}, top left). Fault exposure is thus concentrated at the start of collection. Because both approaches supply corrective trajectories in states where the controller struggles, the resulting improvements reflect targeted data expansion.
  \item To broaden $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ without additional hardware rollouts, \chiz incorporates \emph{spatio-temporal augmentation}: images and actions are mirrored horizontally with left-right arm exchange~\cite{li2025gr}, while variable execution speeds are synthesised through selective frame subsampling (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:consistency}).
\end{enumerate}
Protocols for both standard and heuristic DAgger appear in Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:tda}.

\section{System and Data Setup}\label{kai0:sec:setup}

Our empirical study focuses on multi-stage bimanual garment manipulation encompassing flattening from crumpled states, folding, handover, and rack hanging. Because cloth exhibits high-dimensional contact dynamics and frequent state degradation, these benchmarks expose the distributional mismatches analysed in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:inconsistencies}.

\subsection{Robot Platform}\label{kai0:sec:platform}
The dual-station bimanual setup is illustrated in Figure~\ref{kai0:fig:robot_setup}. Two dual-arm stations are used: one built with AgileX Piper manipulators and the other with ARX X5 manipulators. Every station features two 6-DoF arms fitted with 1-DoF parallel-jaw grippers alongside three Intel RealSense D435i RGB cameras (one overhead static camera plus two wrist-mounted cameras) streaming at $640 \times 480$ resolution. Policy inference executes on a local workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU; hardware specifications and control loop parameters are documented in Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:hardware}.

\subsection{Evaluation Tasks}\label{kai0:sec:tasks}
Three garment manipulation benchmarks ordered by difficulty are used to test \chiz, with key attributes listed in Table~\ref{kai0:tab:tasks}.

\paragraph{Task A: T-shirt flattening and folding (easy).} Adapted as a streamlined version of the $\pi$-series laundry benchmark~\cite{black2024pi0,black2025pi05,amin2026pistar06}, this setting requires unfolding a crumpled T-shirt from an arbitrary starting pose and completing a full fold. Completion is credited when the folded garment rests near the workspace centre within a 180-second horizon.

Success requires the shirt to remain stably hung on the rod without slipping off; no fixed episode time cutoff is recorded in the evaluation summary logs (Table~\ref{kai0:tab:tasks}).

\subsection{Metrics}\label{kai0:sec:metrics}
Every policy variant runs for 30 rollouts (10 rollouts across each of three test garments), and four summary quantities are averaged across those 30 rollouts (uncertainty handling is detailed in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:protocol}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Success rate (SR)}: fraction of rollouts that satisfy the full task completion criteria, expressed as a percentage (higher is better).
  \item \textbf{Throughput (TP)}: projected number of successful task completions per hour of operation (higher is better).
  \item \textbf{Retry cost}: mean count of corrective re-attempts per rollout during closed-loop execution (lower is better).
  \item \textbf{Average score}: partial-credit score normalized to a 100-point scale under a rule-based rubric that awards credit as intermediate sub-goals are reached (higher is better); exact sub-goal weights appear in Table~\ref{kai0:tab:score} of Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:exp}.
\end{itemize}
In the Stage Advantage ablations, temporal regularity of the predicted advantage trace is tracked via the smooth frame ratio (SFR, higher is better) alongside the mean squared temporal difference (MSTD, lower is better). Both metrics capture trajectory smoothness across consecutive frames rather than semantic accuracy, since a trivial constant output achieves zero temporal variation.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Uncertainty}\label{kai0:sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Protocol.} Closed-loop rollouts are conducted on the two physical stations described in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:platform} within the same workspace used for demonstration capture, allowing execution errors to accumulate over time. Every model setting is tested over 30 rollouts partitioned equally across three garment instances (10 trials each), graded against the completion criteria and time limits in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:tasks} and Table~\ref{kai0:tab:tasks}, and assigned partial credit via Table~\ref{kai0:tab:score}. Garments are tossed into arbitrary starting poses prior to each rollout, mirroring the randomized cloth states and lighting variations used during demonstration collection (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:data}). Best-practice reporting checklists for physical robot benchmarks by Kress-Gazit~\etal~\cite{kressgazit2024robot} further recommend logging exact initial-state matching procedures across policies, operator blinding, interleaved versus blocked rollout scheduling, overlap between training and test garments, and exact formulas for throughput projection and retry counting. Because the archived evaluation logs record only aggregate rollout summaries rather than per-episode scheduling and blinding metadata, our experiments strictly measure closed-loop reliability within the training workspace rather than out-of-distribution transfer to unseen hardware, rooms, or cloth instances.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.} Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA} and Figs.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple}--\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control} report metrics from the plotted evaluation summaries, accurate to roughly one axis unit, supplemented with 95\% Wilson score intervals for $n = 30$ trials. Although the plotted bars include whiskers labelled as an ``accumulative standard error'', that quantity is undocumented in the evaluation codebase and is sometimes smaller than binomial sampling variance permits: for instance, the +SA bar in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple} sits near 47\% (14 successes out of 30 trials) with a whisker of roughly $\pm 1$ percentage point, whereas the standard error across the three 10-trial garment strata is at least about 3 points and the pooled binomial standard error is near 9 points. Consequently, whiskers in the plots are ignored when comparing models. Instead, integer success counts are reconstructed from the discrete 3.3-point steps of a 30-trial proportion (where 27\% corresponds to 8 successes out of 30), and two-sided Fisher exact $p$-values alongside 95\% Wilson intervals are derived directly from those counts. At $n = 30$ trials per setting, a 95\% Wilson interval around 50\% spans roughly 34 percentage points, meaning that gaps of four or fewer successes never clear sampling noise ($p > 0.1$ under Fisher's exact test). Because trial-level logs are unavailable for throughput, retry cost, and average score, those three metrics are listed as point means without confidence intervals. Moreover, because the multi-factor grids in Figs.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_souping} and~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control} involve numerous pairwise contrasts, nominal 5\% differences should be interpreted cautiously against multiple-testing inflation.

\subsection{Data Collection and Training}\label{kai0:sec:data}
Roughly 20 hours of human teleoperated demonstrations are collected per benchmark across varied cloth colours, fabrics, starting configurations, and room illumination. Combining the episode totals and mean durations in Table~\ref{kai0:tab:tasks} yields approximately 22.5, 33.6, and 32.4 hours of total recorded trajectories for Tasks~A, B, and~C, respectively, encompassing both initial demonstrations and subsequent DAgger recovery clips. Full-parameter fine-tuning is performed on 8$\times$A100 GPUs under the flow-matching training objective~\cite{black2024pi0}. These compute and dataset totals reflect post-training of an already pre-trained $\pi_{0.5}$ backbone~\cite{black2025pi05} rather than end-to-end training from scratch, and they do not constitute a matched compute or operator-hour budget (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:limitations}). Training hyper-parameters (Table~\ref{kai0:tab:hyperparams}) and collection procedures appear in Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:exp}.

\section{Experiments}\label{kai0:sec:exp}

Physical rollouts on the dual-station platform of Section~\ref{kai0:sec:setup} are organised around four empirical questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{System efficacy.} To what extent do the three modules reinforce one another when combined, and do any pairwise interactions degrade performance? We evaluate this full factorial breakdown on Task~A (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_overall}).
  \item \textbf{Model Arithmetic.} Does weight-space merging of subset-trained policies surpass both the strongest individual subset policy and joint training on the pooled dataset, and does out-of-distribution validation outperform in-domain validation when selecting merge coefficients? (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_ma})
  \item \textbf{Stage Advantage.} Compared with a value-differencing baseline patterned after RECAP in $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$~\cite{amin2026pistar06}, does direct stage-aware advantage estimation yield smoother supervision signals and stronger closed-loop task execution? (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa})
  \item \textbf{Train--Deploy Alignment.} Can heuristic DAgger expand $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ to match the success gains of on-policy DAgger with manageable retry overhead, and how do execution-smoothing controllers interact with spatio-temporal augmentation? (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_tda})
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Baselines and Ablation Design}\label{kai0:sec:baselines}

\paragraph{Base policy.} We adopt $\pi_{0.5}$ as our primary backbone and include $\pi_{0}$ in secondary comparisons, as only these two open-weight models attained non-trivial completion rates on our cloth benchmarks. Alternative architectures that we evaluated, including GO-1 from the AgiBot World suite~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros} (co-authored by the author), X-VLA~\cite{zheng2025xvla}, and DexVLA~\cite{wen2025dexvla}, failed to complete the manipulation sequences reliably even after fine-tuning on the full 20-hour demonstration corpus; preliminary tests with UniVLA and OpenVLA showed similar failures (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:questions}, Q3), and quantitative scores for those unsuccessful models were not archived.

\paragraph{MA ablations.} Two unmerged reference points are evaluated: the \emph{single-best candidate} among policies trained on individual data partitions, and the \emph{full-data candidate} trained jointly on the union of all partitions. Against these references, we test the five coefficient-selection rules from Section~\ref{kai0:sec:ma} (uniform averaging, inverse-loss weighting, gradient descent, adaptive gradient descent, and greedy search), pairing the loss-driven schemes with either an in-domain validation set or an OOD DAgger validation set.

\paragraph{SA ablations.} As a reference method, we implement a value-differencing baseline modelled after the RECAP advantage mechanism in $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$~\cite{amin2026pistar06}. Sharing the PaliGemma vision-language backbone of $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{black2025pi05}, this baseline regresses scalar task progress from a single observation and computes the advantage as the predicted progress gain over a 50-step lookahead within the trajectory. Unlike full RECAP, which learns a distributional value head across demonstrations, on-policy rollouts, and human interventions (Section~\ref{sec:bg:manip}), our $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$-style baseline uses point regression on offline frames. We compare this $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$-style baseline against stage-agnostic direct regression (Direct, Eq.~\eqref{kai0:eq:adv_direct}) and stage-conditioned regression (Direct+Stage, Eq.~\eqref{kai0:eq:adv_stage}).

\paragraph{TDA ablations.} At the control layer, temporal chunk-wise smoothing is benchmarked against synchronous and asynchronous chunk execution~\cite{tang2025vlash}, ACT-style temporal ensembling~\cite{zhao2023aloha}, and RTC~\cite{black2025rtc} across combinations of spatial mirroring and temporal frame skipping. At the data layer, we contrast standard on-policy DAgger~\cite{ross2011dagger} with heuristic DAgger on both $\pi_{0}$~\cite{black2024pi0} and $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{black2025pi05} backbones.

\subsection{System Efficacy Breakdown}\label{kai0:sec:exp_overall}
Component-wise ablation results on Task~A are plotted in Figure~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple} and tabulated in Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA}, where each enabled module uses its best setting from Section~\ref{kai0:sec:ablation}. Several interaction patterns emerge across the eight combinations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Incorporating TDA boosts the completion rate across every pairing by roughly 17 to 53 percentage points. Its influence on retry frequency varies with the partner module: retry counts climb when TDA is paired with +MA (from about 1.8 to 2.6) or +SA (from about 1.1 to 2.3), yet drop when TDA is added to the bare $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline or to +MA+SA. A plausible hypothesis, though not isolated experimentally, is that recovery demonstrations teach the policy to re-grasp after slips, trading extra corrective motions for higher eventual completion.
  \item Enabling SA elevates both completion rate and hourly throughput in every pairing, and settings that include SA achieve the fastest task completion rates overall.
  \item Applying MA in isolation leaves the completion rate nearly unchanged relative to $\pi_{0.5}$ (roughly 30\% versus 27\%, or 9 versus 8 successes out of 30 trials) while slightly depressing the partial-credit score (about 35 versus 41). When combined with either +TDA or +SA, however, MA adds roughly 30 percentage points in completion rate (rising from 16 to 25 and from 14 to 24 successes out of 30; uncorrected Fisher $p \approx 0.025$ and $0.015$).
  \item Combining all three modules yields the top completion rate (about 97\%) and average score (about 97), while matching +SA+TDA in throughput (roughly 66 versus 68 completions per hour). At $n = 30$ trials, the full configuration's 29 successes cannot be statistically separated from the 24 to 26 successes of the two-module pairings (Fisher $p \ge 0.10$), nor does any single module alone clear the sampling threshold over bare $\pi_{0.5}$ (Fisher $p \ge 0.06$); statistically decisive gains require pairing at least two modules.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ablation Studies}\label{kai0:sec:ablation}

We next examine each of the three modules individually to address research questions 2 through 4 from Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp}.

\paragraph{Consistency across figures.} Because individual module ablations were conducted in separate evaluation sessions with different baseline checkpoints and dataset snapshots rather than relative to the single base configuration of Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple}, baseline numbers shift across plots:
\begin{itemize}
  \item On Task~A, the nominal $\pi_{0.5}$ reference attains roughly 27\% completion in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple} versus 23\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_a}, while the synchronous execution baseline in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control} starts at about 37\%.
  \item Adding heuristic DAgger alone brings $\pi_{0.5}$ to roughly 87\% completion in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_a}, whereas the full +TDA module (which incorporates heuristic DAgger) reaches 53\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple}. Similarly, heuristic DAgger elevates retry frequency from roughly 3.0 to 8.6 in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_a}, whereas +TDA reduces retries from 3.0 to 1.7 in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple}.
  \item Temporal chunk-wise smoothing without augmentation incurs about 12 retries per episode in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control}, compared with 1.1 for the integrated system. That same unaugmented smoothing controller appears in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_tda_a_abs} with matching throughput, retry cost, and rubric score, yet its plotted completion rate is roughly 70\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_tda_a_abs} versus 77\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control}.
  \item Within the Stage Advantage study on Task~A (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_advantage}), the value-differencing baseline reaches about 67\% completion and Direct+Stage reaches 80\%, while the full-data baseline in the Model Arithmetic study reaches 83\% (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_soup_a}); by contrast, +SA and bare $\pi_{0.5}$ sit at 47\% and 27\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple}.
  \item On Task~C, unmerged reference policies achieve roughly 10\% completion in Figs.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_c} and~\ref{kai0:fig:app_sa_c}, 20\% for the full-data baseline in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_souping}, and 37\% for synchronous execution in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_tda_c_abs}.
\end{itemize}
These cross-figure offsets arise because the single-module ablation studies (Figs.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_souping}--\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control} and~\ref{kai0:fig:app_soup}--\ref{kai0:fig:app_tda_c}, including the in-chunk smoothing baselines in Figs.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_tda_a}--\ref{kai0:fig:app_tda_c}) reflect separate evaluation sessions and checkpoint stages rather than a single unified experiment run. Accordingly, all quantitative comparisons below are restricted to configurations evaluated within the same figure.

\subsection{Model Arithmetic}\label{kai0:sec:exp_ma}
Figure~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_souping} evaluates weight-space merging strategies against unmerged policies on Task~C across all four evaluation criteria.

\paragraph{Merging.} Every weight-merged policy achieves a higher mean completion rate and hourly throughput than both the single-best subset policy and the jointly trained full-data policy, though certain weighting rules incur extra retries. At 30 rollouts per bar, however, most pairwise gaps lie inside binomial sampling variation. Only the top-performing merge rule, greedy search guided by OOD validation (roughly 15 successes out of 30), clears the nominal 5\% threshold relative to the full-data baseline (roughly 6 out of 30; uncorrected Fisher $p \approx 0.03$), and this gap narrows against the single-best subset policy (roughly 7 out of 30; Fisher $p \approx 0.06$). On the partial-credit rubric, the single-best candidate (score near 70) surpasses most merged models, with only greedy search and OOD inverse-loss weighting scoring higher. Why linear combinations of subset checkpoints match or exceed joint training on the pooled dataset remains an open empirical question; one untested possibility is over-parameterization in fine-tuned VLAs, analogous to weight-redundancy phenomena observed in language models~\cite{yu2024language}.

\paragraph{Validation split.} Replacing in-domain validation loss with OOD DAgger validation loss lifts the completion rate for three of the four loss-guided merging rules (inverse-loss weighting rises from roughly 30\% to 47\%, gradient descent from 27\% to 33\%, and greedy search from 40\% to 50\%) while leaving adaptive gradient descent steady near 40\%. OOD validation also trims retry counts under three strategies, though throughput drops slightly under adaptive gradient descent and greedy search. Within each individual rule, the gap between in-domain and OOD validation remains inside sampling noise (for instance, 12 versus 15 successes out of 30 under greedy search, Fisher $p \approx 0.60$). While directionally compatible with the view that OOD DAgger trajectories capture deployment states better than static demonstrations when balancing $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ modes, these differences are too small at $n = 30$ to be conclusive on their own. Across both splits on Task~C, greedy search delivers the top (or tied-top) completion rate, highest rubric score, and lowest retry count, albeit not the peak throughput. Parallel ablations on Tasks~A and~B appear in Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:ablation_ma}, where merging generally outperforms unmerged baselines but the ranking of validation splits varies; Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:ma} specifies the exact merging rule deployed in Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA}.

\subsection{Stage Advantage}\label{kai0:sec:exp_sa}
Figure~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_advantage} benchmarks Stage Advantage (Direct+Stage, contributed by a co-author) against the $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$-style value-differencing baseline and stage-agnostic direct regression (Direct) on Tasks~A and~B.

\paragraph{Smoothness of the advantage signal.} Across both benchmarks, Direct+Stage produces less erratic advantage trajectories than value differencing: its SFR rises from roughly 46\% to 60\% on Task~A and from 52\% to 77\% on Task~B, while its MSTD drops from 0.82 to 0.58 on Task~B and stays comparable on Task~A (1.0 versus 1.06). Stage-agnostic Direct regression improves SFR relative to value differencing on Task~B but worsens MSTD, and it yields the lowest completion rate and highest retry count among the three methods on that task, supporting the inclusion of explicit stage conditioning. Even so, temporal smoothness measures signal regularity rather than accuracy, as a trivial constant estimator would score perfectly on both smoothness metrics, and no held-out ground-truth advantage labels were used for independent verification.

\paragraph{Task performance.} Downstream policy gains depend on task structure. On Task~A, Direct+Stage outperforms the value-differencing baseline across all four rollout metrics, although the completion-rate increase from roughly 20 to 24 successes out of 30 remains within binomial variation (Fisher $p \approx 0.38$). On the multi-branch sorting benchmark (Task~B), Direct+Stage cuts retry frequency (from about 17 to 10) and lifts the partial-credit score (from about 37 to 48), consistent with suppressing hesitant idling and repeated failed grasps, yet its completion rate (roughly 17\% versus 23\%) and hourly throughput (roughly 4 versus 6) trail the baseline. On Task~C (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:ablation_sa}), Direct+Stage similarly reduces retries while posting lower completion rate, throughput, and rubric score. Although Stage Advantage converges to a lower training objective value than value differencing (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:loss}), lower training loss alone does not guarantee superior policy guidance.

\subsection{Train--Deploy Alignment}\label{kai0:sec:exp_tda}

\paragraph{Heuristic DAgger.} Figures~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_a} and~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_c} compare heuristic DAgger against standard on-policy DAgger across two backbones ($\pi_{0.5}$ and $\pi_0$) and two benchmarks (Tasks~A and~C). Incorporating DAgger recovery demonstrations produces the largest single performance jump in this chapter. On Task~A, heuristic DAgger elevates $\pi_{0.5}$ completion from roughly 23\% to 87\% (7 versus 26 successes out of 30) and lifts $\pi_0$ from near zero to roughly 83\%; on Task~C, $\pi_{0.5}$ rises from about 10\% to 43\% (3 versus 13 out of 30). Because both collection protocols inject human recovery trajectories at failure-prone states, these improvements stem from targeted data addition rather than purely algorithmic alignment. Heuristic DAgger simultaneously increases retry frequency across all settings (for example, from roughly 3.0 to 8.6 retries for $\pi_{0.5}$ on Task~A), reflecting active re-grasping in contrast to the base policy's tendency to stall without retrying. Standard on-policy DAgger attains comparable completion rates (roughly 93\% on Task~A and 53\% on Task~C for $\pi_{0.5}$, corresponding to 28 versus 26 and 16 versus 13 successes out of 30, well within sampling noise), while keeping retry counts lower (roughly 3.4 versus 8.6 on Task~A, and 1.5 versus 2.4 on Task~C) and achieving a higher rubric score on Task~C (about 83 versus 62). On Task~A with $\pi_{0.5}$, standard DAgger also reaches higher hourly throughput (roughly 50 versus 36). While heuristic DAgger avoids policy execution during collection and should therefore reduce wall-clock collection time per episode, exact operator and robot collection hours were not logged for either protocol.

\paragraph{Control strategies and augmentation.} Figure~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control} evaluates temporal chunk-wise smoothing alongside alternative execution controllers across four augmentation regimes on Task~A. \section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{kai0:sec:limitations}

By pairing Model Arithmetic, Stage Advantage, and Train--Deploy Alignment against coverage gaps, temporal discontinuities, and compounding rollout errors, \chiz elevates the Task~A completion rate of $\pi_{0.5}$ from roughly 27\% to 97\%. Below we examine the methodological boundaries of our empirical evidence followed by open technical challenges.

\paragraph{Evidence for the central hypothesis.} Framing distributional inconsistency rather than dataset size as the primary bottleneck is an interpretive hypothesis backed by a full-system factorial study on a single benchmark (Task~A) at 30 rollouts per cell. Because we did not run an iso-resource scaling control that fine-tunes $\pi_{0.5}$ on an equal total number of teleoperator and robot hours consisting purely of standard demonstrations, the experiment does not disentangle consistency interventions from targeted data volume. Indeed, the largest individual jump in completion rate comes from adding DAgger recovery trajectories (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_tda}), which directly expands state coverage with corrective demonstrations. Moreover, individual module gains vary across benchmarks and pairings (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:ablation}), and only the easiest benchmark (Task~A) received the complete eight-way factorial evaluation, whereas Tasks~B and~C were studied through single-module ablations.

\paragraph{Resource budget.} Describing our recipe as resource-aware reflects its reliance on roughly 20 hours of demonstrations per task and 8 A100 GPUs to post-train a pre-trained backbone. However, the archived logs record aggregate dataset durations (Table~\ref{kai0:tab:tasks}) and the 80{,}000-step schedule (Table~\ref{kai0:tab:hyperparams}) without itemising cumulative GPU hours across the four subset checkpoints in Model Arithmetic, the Stage Advantage estimator, and the final policy, or breaking down teleoperator and robot collection hours between base demonstrations and DAgger rollouts relative to the bare $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline. Consequently, direct cost-normalized comparisons against pure data scaling remain future work.

\paragraph{What the evaluation tests.} Although closed-loop physical rollouts expose each policy to its own compounding control errors, all tests take place on the same two robot stations and workspace used during data capture, without separate tracking of unseen test garments (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:protocol}). Our results therefore demonstrate reliability under randomized starting poses and closed-loop perturbations within the training environment, rather than zero-shot transfer to new robot morphologies, rooms, or cloth categories.

\paragraph{Statistics and consistency.} At $n = 30$ rollouts per configuration, differences of four or fewer successes fall inside binomial sampling variation, encompassing several single-module comparisons (Sections~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_overall} and~\ref{kai0:sec:ablation}). Because metrics are taken from the plotted evaluation summaries and individual ablations reflect separate evaluation sessions with differing baseline checkpoints (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:ablation}), only the larger directional effects can be resolved with confidence.

\paragraph{Scalability.} While vision-language-action backbones are motivated by broad cross-task transfer, our experiments did not test whether fine-tuning preserves general pre-trained capabilities or transfers to rigid-body manipulation. Whether weight-space merging via Model Arithmetic can combine specialists trained on distinct manipulation skills, rather than subsets of a single garment benchmark, also remains an open question.

\paragraph{Data valuation.} Informally, we noticed substantial sensitivity to demonstration curation during development: policies trained under identical settings on different data batches varied between roughly 20\% and 60\% in completion rate (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:questions}). Because this observation was not part of a controlled study, assessing dataset utility still relies on expensive end-to-end training runs or non-parallelizable physical replay checks. Developing lightweight offline proxies that predict demonstration utility before training is a priority for future work.

\paragraph{Advantage design and failure modes.} Stage Advantage derives supervision from normalized temporal progress within each stage, implicitly assuming monotonic advancement toward the sub-goal. Learning an unsupervised critic that separates productive manipulation actions from hesitation without assuming linear time progress would relax this assumption, and verifying advantage predictions against held-out human annotations or known failure segments remains necessary. Furthermore, our qualitative failure breakdown identifies imprecise spatial grasping and mid-episode stagnation as the two primary failure modes, reflecting limitations in fine-grained 3D localization and long-horizon reasoning within current pre-trained backbones. Model Arithmetic and Stage Advantage act as post-hoc regularizers that partially alleviate these weaknesses, though their individual reductions of specific failure modes were not separately quantified (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:questions} and Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:failure}).

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{kai0:sec:summary}

This chapter investigated RQ3(b), asking how bimanual manipulation policies can maintain closed-loop reliability under practical data and compute limits when deployment conditions diverge from offline training. We organized our approach around three conceptual distributions: the demonstration corpus $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, the learned generative policy $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$, and the closed-loop rollout distribution $P_{\mathrm{test}}$, whose pairwise divergences appear as coverage gaps, temporal discontinuities, and compounding failure cascades. To counter each divergence, \chiz integrates three modules: Model Arithmetic blends subset-trained checkpoints using merge weights selected on out-of-distribution DAgger validation trajectories; Stage Advantage (contributed by a co-author) supplies stage-conditioned progress labels for advantage-weighted behaviour cloning; and Train--Deploy Alignment couples heuristic DAgger collection with spatio-temporal augmentation and temporal chunk-wise smoothing.

In the full factorial study on Task~A using the dual-station bimanual platform, \chiz lifted the 30-trial completion rate of $\pi_{0.5}$ from roughly 27\% to 97\% (8 versus 29 successes, well beyond binomial sampling noise), corresponding to a 250\% relative gain alongside a roughly tenfold rise in hourly throughput (Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA}). Each benchmark used roughly 20 hours of demonstrations post-trained on 8 A100 GPUs, though total cumulative GPU and operator hours across sub-models were not separately itemized. Single-module ablations across Tasks~A, B, and~C reveal complementary strengths and clear caveats. Targeted recovery trajectories from heuristic and standard DAgger delivered the largest standalone gains (raising Task~A completion from roughly 23\% to 87--93\%). Weight merging in Model Arithmetic surpassed both the best individual subset checkpoint and full-data joint training in most comparisons, yet provided no standalone gain on Task~A and cleared nominal sampling noise over full-data training on Task~C only for the top OOD-validated greedy merge. Stage Advantage produced smoother temporal signals than a $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$-style value-differencing baseline across all three tasks, though its downstream completion-rate improvement was confined to Task~A and remained within sampling error. At the execution layer, combining chunk-wise smoothing with RTC achieved the highest Task~A completion rate, even though standalone chunk-wise smoothing incurred elevated retry counts in two augmentation regimes. Methodologically, because dataset scale was not varied at a matched operator-hour budget, the consistency-versus-scale thesis remains a working hypothesis; evaluations were restricted to 30 rollouts per bar within the training workspace using plotted evaluation summaries across separate ablation sessions; and retention of pre-trained priors, transfer to rigid objects, cross-task model merging, and offline metrics for demonstration quality (which informally shifted completion rates between 20\% and 60\%) remain open directions.

Taken together with the autonomous driving study in Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}, these results point to a shared principle across the Act pillar: robust physical execution requires closing the feedback loop between deployment behaviour and policy learning. Whereas Centaur adapts online at test time via uncertainty-guided updates in non-reactive log replay, \chiz routes physical rollout experience back into the training and selection pipeline, using DAgger recoveries both as targeted training data and as the validation criterion for checkpoint merging while applying chunk-wise smoothing at execution time. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} consolidates these insights into broader design principles for self-correcting embodied systems.
