Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} showed, on logged states in non-reactive simulation, that an end-to-end driving planner can adapt its per-frame decisions at test time by minimising its own uncertainty, without labels~\cite{sima2024centaur}. This chapter carries the question of the Act pillar over to a second kind of physical agent. Research question RQ3(b) asks how a learned policy can stay reliable when the deployment distribution departs from training in \emph{real-world robot manipulation}. Here the curse of reality takes a different shape. Manipulating clothes involves contact-rich interactions with deformable objects, whose state is high-dimensional and may start from an arbitrary configuration. The human demonstrations that the policy imitates are inconsistent with one another and cover only a sparse part of the ways in which a task can be solved. Data and compute are scarce: every hour of expert teleoperation costs robot and operator time, and every fine-tuning run of a large vision-language-action (VLA) model costs GPU time. Finally, a policy is trained on recorded demonstrations but executed on hardware, where inference latency and physical limits make the executed motion differ from the one the model predicted.

This chapter puts forward the hypothesis that, in this regime, the scale of data and compute is not the only obstacle to robustness, and that a further, major obstacle is the inconsistency among three distributions that govern the robot learning cycle: the distribution of human expert demonstrations used for training, $P_{\mathrm{train}}$; the policy learned from them, $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$, that is, the distribution over actions that the trained model assigns to each state; and the distribution of trajectories that the robot actually executes at deployment, $P_{\mathrm{test}}$. In the shift taxonomy of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, this is a deployment shift, which asks whether training matches use. In the notation of Section~\ref{sec:method:framework}, $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ corresponds to $p_{\mathrm{train}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ to the executed part of $p_{\mathrm{deploy}}$; $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ is shaped both by the demonstrations and by the inductive bias of the policy class. The three distributions serve as conceptual vocabulary for organising the method; none of them is measured in this chapter (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:distributions}). Rather than scaling up plain demonstrations or model size, the chapter treats each pairwise mismatch among the three distributions with a targeted remedy. One of these remedies, DAgger-style recovery data, is itself targeted data collection.

The resulting system, \chiz,\footnote{Project page: \url{https://mmlab.hk/research/kai0}. Code: \url{https://github.com/OpenDriveLab/KAI0}.} has three components. \emph{Model Arithmetic} (MA) trains policies on disjoint subsets of the demonstrations and merges their weights by convex interpolation, choosing the mixing coefficients by the validation loss on out-of-distribution recovery data collected with DAgger; it is intended to align $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ with $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ without collecting more demonstrations, although its validation data require DAgger rollouts. \emph{Stage Advantage} (SA), a component developed by a co-author (see the statement of contribution), predicts the advantage directly from pairs of observations, conditioned on the semantic stage of a long-horizon task, and turns it into a binary optimality label for advantage-weighted behaviour cloning; its advantage signal is smoother than one computed as a difference of predicted values, and it is intended to steer $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ towards actions that make progress at deployment. \emph{Train--Deploy Alignment} (TDA) expands $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ towards $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ with heuristic DAgger and spatio-temporal augmentation, and it counters the latency-induced mismatch at execution time with temporal chunk-wise smoothing.

The evaluation covers collaborative long-horizon garment manipulation with two dual-arm robots, spanning flattening, folding, conditional sorting and hanging. The full system was compared with its base policy on one task only, Task~A (T-shirt flattening and folding); Tasks~B and~C were used for component ablations. On Task~A, over 30 real-robot trials per policy, the success rate rises from about 27\% (about 8 of 30 trials) for the state-of-the-art $\pi_{0.5}$ policy to about 97\% (about 29 of 30) for \chiz, a relative improvement of about 250\% (Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA}). This difference is far larger than the sampling error of 30 trials: the approximate 95\% Wilson intervals are about 14--44\% and 83--99\%. All values in this chapter are read from bar charts and are therefore approximate (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:protocol}). The improvement is obtained with about 20 hours of demonstrations per task and 8 A100 GPUs for post-training. These figures describe the data and hardware for post-training a heavily pre-trained $\pi_{0.5}$ policy, not for training a policy from scratch, and this is the sense in which this chapter calls the approach resource-aware; the budget in GPU, robot and operator hours is not reported (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:limitations}). Because scale was not varied at a matched budget, and because the largest single gain in the chapter comes from targeted DAgger data (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_tda}), the claim that inconsistency rather than scale limits robustness remains a hypothesis, supported by this integrated system result on Task~A.

\section{Motivation}\label{kai0:sec:intro}

Achieving production-ready robustness is a grand challenge of modern robotics. Autonomous vehicles, a specific form of navigation robot, already operate in complex urban environments~\cite{Kusano2025waymocrash,tesla2025fsd,waymo2025blog}. Reaching similar reliability for robotic manipulation in unstructured settings, and earning a comparable degree of human trust, remains an open problem. A manipulation policy has to operate in a vast search space and cope with the large environmental variability of the physical world. The prevailing industrial response has therefore been to scale, leveraging massive computational resources to train ever larger foundation models~\cite{black2024pi0,black2025pi05,amin2026pistar06,generalist2025gen0,sunday2025act1,zheng2025xvla,beingbeyond2026beingh05}. Large data platforms follow the same logic on the data side; AgiBot World~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros} (co-authored by the author), for example, comprises more than one million trajectories collected by over 100 robots.

Architectural progress and resource scaling are essential. Our hypothesis is that they are not the only decisive factor in robust policy execution: within the vast search space of the real world, a ``hidden devil'' that hinders robustness is \emph{the inconsistency among the distributions that govern the three pillars of robot learning: data collection, model training and policy deployment}. These inconsistencies are often not evident in success rates alone. They also show up in the smoothness of execution, in the throughput of the system and in the number of retries needed to complete a task~\cite{chen2025benchmarking,bu2025agibot_iros,shi2025diversity,amin2026pistar06,jiang2025behavior}. To organise the discussion, we describe the robot learning pipeline with three distributions that span the whole research cycle:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\bm{P_{\mathrm{train}}}$, the distribution of human expert demonstrations used to train an imitation policy;
  \item $\bm{Q_{\mathrm{model}}}$, the policy learned from $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, \ie the distribution over actions that the trained model assigns to each state (Eq.~\eqref{kai0:eq:qmodel}); it reflects both the demonstrations and the inductive bias of the model;
  \item $\bm{P_{\mathrm{test}}}$, the distribution of action trajectories executed during real-robot deployment, which differs from the actions output by the policy because of delays and physical limits.
\end{itemize}

In our real-world deployments, the failures we observed fall into three systematic inconsistencies among these distributions (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:teaser}). First, because the task is extremely high-dimensional, $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ is inherently sparse relative to the full manifold of solutions, and the learned $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ is heavily biased towards the limited training distribution. Second, the latency between model inference ($Q_{\mathrm{model}}$) and control-level execution ($P_{\mathrm{test}}$) introduces a temporal mismatch that renders theoretically optimal plans suboptimal during inference~\cite{black2025rtc,tang2025vlash}. Third, although failures are frequent during deployment, the policy lacks the ability to recover from them: even from states that lie within $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, minor perturbations in $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ can trigger a catastrophic divergence from which the system cannot recover~\cite{hu2025rac,pan2026sop,amin2026pistar06}.

The broader machine learning literature addresses such distribution shifts through dataset scaling~\cite{wiles2022fine,higgins2017beta,radford2021learning}, heuristic or learned augmentation~\cite{wiles2022fine,cubuk2020randaugment,cubuk2019autoaugment} and adaptive learning~\cite{wiles2022fine,liu2021just,schneider2020improving}. Applying these general-purpose methods off the shelf to robotic manipulation, however, runs into domain-specific constraints: expert demonstrations are prohibitively expensive to collect, the latency between inference and execution is substantial, and training large-scale models carries a heavy computational burden.

\chiz is a framework that targets these misalignments one at a time within the constraints of physical robotics. Each of its three components targets one pair of distributions (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:teaser}, bottom):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item \textbf{Model Arithmetic (MA)} is intended to align the learned policy ($Q_{\mathrm{model}}$) with the demonstrations ($P_{\mathrm{train}}$). By merging the weights of checkpoints trained on different subsets of $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, it aims to let the merged policy capture modes of $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ that a single training run misses.
  \item \textbf{Stage Advantage (SA)}, developed by a co-author, is intended to bias the learned policy ($Q_{\mathrm{model}}$) towards actions that make progress at deployment ($P_{\mathrm{test}}$). It decomposes a long-horizon task into semantic sub-goals, called stages, and provides stage-aware advantage signals that weight behaviour cloning~\cite{peng2019awr}. It is trained on the collected episodes, and its signal is smoother than that of a value-difference baseline inspired by $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$~\cite{amin2026pistar06} (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}).
  \item \textbf{Train--Deploy Alignment (TDA)} expands $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ towards $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ with heuristic DAgger and spatio-temporal augmentation, intended to cover more of the states met at deployment. It further introduces temporal chunk-wise smoothing, which blends consecutive action chunks to mitigate the latency between inference and actuation; on Task~A it gave a higher throughput than RTC-only execution~\cite{black2025rtc} in two of three augmentation settings (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_tda}).
\end{enumerate}

We evaluate \chiz on collaborative long-horizon garment manipulation: flattening, folding and hanging different clothes. The contact-rich, deformable dynamics of cloth, and the need to recover from arbitrary cloth states, magnify each of the distributional shifts described above. The experiments bear on this view as follows. We report each effect task by task, in the direction observed; all values are read from bar charts, and each configuration is evaluated in 30 trials, so differences of a few trials are within sampling error (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:protocol}).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{System.} On Task~A, the full system raised the success rate of $\pi_{0.5}$ from about 27\% to about 97\%. This integrated result is the main evidence for the distributional view. The system was not compared with $\pi_{0.5}$ trained on the same total amount of plain demonstrations, so the result does not separate consistency from scale.
  \item \textbf{Train--Deploy Alignment.} DAgger-style recovery data gave the largest single gain in the chapter: on Task~A, heuristic or standard DAgger raised the success rate of $\pi_{0.5}$ from about 23\% to about 87--93\%, and on Task~C from about 10\% to about 43--53\%. This gain comes from targeted data collection. Heuristic DAgger raised the retry cost in every case, whereas standard DAgger left it roughly unchanged except for $\pi_{0}$ on Task~A. Spatio-temporal augmentation gave no clear gain on Task~A. Chunk-wise smoothing gave a higher success rate than RTC in two of three augmentation settings on Task~A but the highest retry cost in two settings; combined with RTC, it gave the best configuration without augmentation.
  \item \textbf{Model Arithmetic.} On Task~C, every merged policy had a higher mean success rate and throughput than both the best single-subset policy and the policy trained on all data, but only the best variant differed from the full-data policy by more than sampling error, and some strategies raised the retry cost. On Tasks~A and~B, the merged policies were better in most but not all cases. On Task~A, adding MA alone to $\pi_{0.5}$ did not raise the success rate (about 30\% against about 27\%) and lowered the average score (about 35 against 41), whereas adding it to TDA or to SA raised the success rate by about 30 points. Validation on DAgger data gave the best merged policy on Task~C, but its advantage over in-domain validation was within sampling error there and less consistent on Tasks~A and~B.
  \item \textbf{Stage Advantage.} Stage Advantage gave smoother advantage signals than the value-difference baseline on all three tasks. Smoothness is not correctness, and the signal was not validated against held-out progress labels. Task performance improved on Task~A (success rate about 80\% against about 67\%, within sampling error). On Task~B, Stage Advantage improved the retry cost and the average score but not the success rate or the throughput (success rate about 17\% against about 23\%); on Task~C, the success rate, throughput and average score dropped.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{kai0:sec:related}

\subsection{Imitation Learning and Real-World Policy Deployment}\label{kai0:sec:related_il}
Imitation learning has become a dominant paradigm for robot manipulation, ranging from lightweight transformer-based policies~\cite{zhao2023aloha,chi2024umi,chi2023diffusion,zeng2024learning} to foundation models trained on massive collections of robot demonstrations~\cite{brohan2023rt1,zitkovich2023rt2,kim2024openvla,team2024octo,bu2025univla,black2025pi05,amin2026pistar06}. Among these, the $\pi$ series~\cite{black2024pi0,black2025pi05,amin2026pistar06} stands out for the strong generalisation it obtains from a large-scale pre-training dataset. Collecting such datasets~\cite{khazatsky2024droid,oneill2024openx,bu2025agibot_iros,jiang2025galaxea,walke2023bridgedata}, however, demands substantial resources. For data efficiency, prior work explores DAgger-style data aggregation~\cite{ross2011dagger,kelly2019hgdagger,hu2025rac,amin2026pistar06} and data augmentation~\cite{laskin2020reinforcement,kostrikov2020image,yu2023scaling,li2025gr}. DAgger, however, requires real-time human supervision during policy rollouts~\cite{ross2011dagger}, which keeps data collection time-consuming. Real-world deployment introduces a further challenge: the latency between inference and control causes a mismatch between the model outputs and the physical execution. Prior work mitigates this with execution-side optimisations~\cite{zhao2023aloha,black2025rtc,tang2025vlash}, at the price of additional inference overhead. Existing methods thus address individual phases rather than jointly enforcing distributional consistency across the robot learning cycle of data collection, model training and deployment~\cite{chen2025retaining,tajwar2024preference}. This chapter describes the challenge in terms of $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{test}}$, and \chiz targets all three pairwise mismatches.

\subsection{Model Merging and Weight Interpolation}\label{kai0:sec:related_merge}
Model merging has emerged as an efficient way to consolidate the knowledge of several neural networks. Early work in computer vision and natural language processing showed that interpolating the weights of checkpoints obtained with perturbed hyper-parameters~\cite{wortsman2022soups}, or of models fine-tuned on different tasks~\cite{wortsman2022soups,ilharco2023task,yadav2023ties}, can improve generalisation and robustness. These techniques have since been extended to large language models~\cite{Akiba2024EvolutionaryOO,Yang2024ModelMI,Team2025KimiKS}, motion planning~\cite{Lee2025InteractionMergedMP} and robot learning~\cite{wang2024fleet}. They often rely on in-distribution metrics to select the merging strategy, which may not account for the narrow distribution shifts that are common in complex manipulation. In parallel with this work, RETAIN~\cite{Yadav2025RobustFO} applies model merging to adapt VLA policies, improving out-of-distribution (OOD) generalisation on the target task while better retaining generalist skills. Model Arithmetic specifically mitigates the model bias caused by incomplete training coverage when expert demonstrations are limited. It introduces a validation protocol based on OOD data, namely recovery trajectories collected with DAgger, intended to favour merged policies that generalise to unseen states. It also compares several merging strategies, namely uniform weighting, inverse-loss weighting, gradient descent and greedy search, to identify the most effective way of mitigating training-data bias.

\subsection{Advantage Estimation for Long-Horizon Tasks}\label{kai0:sec:related_adv}
Prior work conditions policies on rewards, values and advantages to guide action selection in long-horizon tasks~\cite{schmidhuber2019reinforcement,emmons2022rvs,zheng2022online,wu2023elastic,kuba2023advantage}. This includes advantage-weighted objectives such as advantage-weighted regression (AWR), which biases behaviour cloning towards actions with higher advantage~\cite{peng2019awr}. Building on these ideas, $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$ trains a distributional value model to estimate state-action advantages and uses them for advantage-conditioned VLA training~\cite{amin2026pistar06}. A key limitation in practice is numerical instability: advantages computed as differences of values can be noisy and high-variance, especially under long-horizon real-world dynamics. Stage Advantage, a co-author's component of \chiz, addresses this limitation by predicting the advantage directly from paired observations and conditioning it on semantic stages. The resulting supervision signal is smoother (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}), and it can be discretised into a binary optimality indicator for policy learning~\cite{chen2025robot,amin2026pistar06}.

\section{Distributional Inconsistencies in Robot Learning}\label{kai0:sec:problem}

This section formalises the distributional view introduced in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:intro} and defines the three inconsistencies that \chiz addresses. The formalism names the quantities that each component is meant to bring into agreement; it is not used to measure them (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:distributions}).

\subsection{Preliminaries}\label{kai0:sec:prelim}
We consider a finite-horizon Markov decision process (MDP) with state space $\mathcal{S}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$ and horizon $H$. A trajectory $\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots, s_H)$ evolves under the real-world dynamics $s_{t+1} \sim T(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t, \xi)$ with a fixed environment parameterisation $\xi$ and an initial-state distribution $\mu(s_0)$. The trajectory distribution induced by a stochastic policy $\pi(a \mid s; \phi)$ can then be written as~\cite{schulman2015trust,levine2018reinforcement}
\begin{equation}
  P_{\pi}(\tau) = \mu(s_0) \prod_{t=0}^{H-1} \pi(a_t \mid s_t; \phi)\, T(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t, \xi),
  \label{kai0:eq:traj_distrib}
\end{equation}
abbreviated as $P$ when the policy $\pi$ is unambiguous.

\subsection{Three Distributions of the Robot Learning Cycle}\label{kai0:sec:distributions}
We first define $P_{\mathrm{real}}$ as the distribution over successful trajectories, \ie trajectories that complete the task. Let $\mathcal{G}$ denote the set of successful trajectories. Then
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{real}}(\tau) \propto \mathbf{1}\{\tau \in \mathcal{G}\}
  \label{kai0:eq:preal}
\end{equation}
characterises the manifold of all valid action sequences that complete the task under real-world dynamics. With $P_{\mathrm{real}}$ as the reference, we now define the three distributions that underlie the alignment objective of \chiz.

\paragraph{Training distribution.} The training distribution $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ is induced by human expert demonstrations on real robots. Let $\mathcal{D} = \{\tau^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ denote the set of demonstrations drawn from $P_{\mathrm{train}}$.

\paragraph{Model distribution.} Throughout this chapter, $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ denotes the learned policy, $Q_{\mathrm{model}} \triangleq \pi(a \mid s; \hat{\phi})$, whose parameters are obtained by maximising $\sum_{t} \log \pi(a_t \mid s_t; \phi)$ over the demonstration set $\mathcal{D}$~\cite{ross2011dagger,osa2018algorithmic}, that is,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\phi} = \argmax_{\phi} \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{D}} \sum_{t} \log \pi(a_t \mid s_t; \phi).
  \label{kai0:eq:qmodel}
\end{equation}
The inductive bias of the policy class and of pre-training determines how $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ generalises beyond $\mathcal{D}$; it is a property of the learned policy, not a separate object.

\paragraph{Test distribution.} Real-robot execution produces $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ by composing $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ with an inference operator $I(\tilde{a}_t \mid a_t, s_t)$, where $\tilde{a}_t$ denotes the action that is actually executed:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{test}}(\tau) = \mu(s_0) \prod_{t=0}^{H-1} \pi(a_t \mid s_t; \hat{\phi})\, \tilde{T}(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t, \xi),
  \label{kai0:eq:ptest}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{T}(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t, \xi) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{a}_t \sim I(\cdot \mid a_t, s_t)} \big[ T(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, \tilde{a}_t, \xi) \big].
  \label{kai0:eq:ttilde}
\end{equation}
The inference operator formalises the observation of Section~\ref{kai0:sec:intro} that the executed trajectory differs from the output of the policy because of delays and physical limits.

\paragraph{Status of the formalism.} None of the three distributions is estimated in this chapter, and no divergence or coverage statistic between them is computed before or after applying a component. Statements such as ``Model Arithmetic aligns $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ with $P_{\mathrm{train}}$'' therefore describe the intended effect of a component, not a measured change, and the t-SNE plots of Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:consistency} are qualitative illustrations. Operationalising the distributions, for example as the likelihood of held-out DAgger states under each model or as the coverage of test-time states by training states in an embedding space, is left for future work.

\subsection{Three Systematic Inconsistencies}\label{kai0:sec:inconsistencies}
We group the failures observed in real-world deployment into three systematic inconsistencies among these distributions, which arise in different phases of the robot learning cycle:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \textbf{Coverage deficiency.} $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ undersamples the high-dimensional manifold $P_{\mathrm{real}}$, which biases $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ towards the limited support of the training data.
  \item \textbf{Temporal mismatch.} Long-horizon tasks contain states that look similar but belong to semantically different stages, which leads $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ to misapply temporal knowledge; in addition, the latency between inference and control causes a temporal mismatch at the level of execution. Both appear in $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ as failures or as the robot staying still.
  \item \textbf{Failure cascade.} Because recovery behaviours are absent from $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, the policy cannot correct itself after perturbations in $P_{\mathrm{test}}$.
\end{enumerate}
The components of \chiz address each inconsistency with a targeted alignment strategy, described in the next section.

\section{Method}\label{kai0:sec:method}

\subsection{Overview of the Framework}\label{kai0:sec:pipeline}
To address the three inconsistencies of Section~\ref{kai0:sec:inconsistencies} coherently, \chiz is designed to be resource-efficient and integrates three complementary components across the robot learning cycle (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:pipeline}). Model Arithmetic (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:ma}) aims to expand the coverage of the policy by merging, in weight space, models trained on complementary subsets of the data. Stage Advantage (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:sa}) addresses temporal mismatch in the policy learning phase with stage-aware advantage estimation, which is intended to provide smoother supervision over long horizons. Train--Deploy Alignment (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:tda}) closes the loop between deployment and training through inference optimisation and complementary data augmentation.

\subsection{Model Arithmetic}\label{kai0:sec:ma}
Limited expert demonstrations in the initial data collection phase lead to coverage deficiency in $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, which in turn biases the learned policies towards narrow manipulation patterns. A straightforward remedy is to scale up expert demonstrations until $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ sufficiently approximates $P_{\mathrm{real}}$. For garment manipulation this is prohibitively expensive, because each collection cycle demands extensive operation time. This raises a fundamental question: \emph{how can the model bias be mitigated efficiently without scaling the data?}

Model Arithmetic (MA) is a weight-space merging strategy that combines policies trained on complementary data subsets, guided by validation-based optimisation. Unlike a mixture of experts (MoE), which requires explicit routing mechanisms and a complex training design~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch}, and unlike model ensembling, which combines the outputs of models~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017simple}, MA directly merges parameters into a single unified policy. MA starts by randomly partitioning the training set $\mathcal{D}$ into non-overlapping subsets $\{\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2, \dots, \mathcal{D}_n\}$, all sampled from $P_{\mathrm{train}}$. It trains policies $\{\theta_1, \theta_2, \dots, \theta_n\}$ independently on these subsets and synthesises their weights by interpolation:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{\mathrm{merged}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i \theta_i, \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad \alpha_i \ge 0, \quad \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i = 1.
  \label{kai0:eq:arith}
\end{equation}
The coefficients $\{\alpha_i\}$ are optimised by minimising a held-out loss on a validation split, and $\theta_{\mathrm{merged}}$ serves as the final $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ for deployment. The motivation is that, because each subset has limited coverage, the individual policies may converge to different regions of the solution manifold (this is not measured here), so that the key design question becomes how to merge them.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{ch-kai0/images/arithmetic_v4.pdf}
  \caption[Souping strategies in Model Arithmetic]{\textbf{Souping strategies in Model Arithmetic.} Policies trained on separate subsets are merged by weighted interpolation. \textbf{Top}: inverse-loss weighting assigns higher coefficients to models with lower validation loss. \textbf{Bottom}: the other strategies.}
  \label{kai0:fig:arithmetic}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Out-of-distribution validation.} In practice, the critical design choice is the selection of the validation set. We construct a validation set that is out of distribution (OOD) with respect to all training subsets, which are in-domain, to avoid selecting the merged policy on data drawn from the training distribution. Specifically, we use trajectories collected with DAgger~\cite{ross2011dagger,kelly2019hgdagger} from the models trained on individual subsets, because these recovery behaviours are naturally absent from all of the original training data. Collecting them requires running the subset policies on the robots with an operator ready to intervene, so Model Arithmetic adds no demonstrations but does use robot and operator time; this cost is not reported separately (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Merging strategies.} On this validation set, we implement and compare four souping strategies~\cite{wortsman2022soups} for obtaining $\theta_{\mathrm{merged}}$, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:arithmetic}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Average weighting}~\cite{wortsman2022soups} assigns uniform weights by setting $\alpha_i = 1/n$.
  \item \emph{Inverse-loss weighting}~\cite{maiti2025souper} sets each coefficient inversely proportional to the validation loss $L_i$ of checkpoint $i$ and then normalises the coefficients:
  \begin{equation}
    \alpha_i \propto \frac{1}{(L_i + \delta)^{p}},
    \label{kai0:eq:invloss}
  \end{equation}
  with constants $\delta$ and $p$.
  \item \emph{Gradient descent}~\cite{ilharco2023task}, together with an adaptive variant of it, parameterises the coefficients with a softmax and minimises the validation loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}$ of the merged model $\theta_{\mathrm{merged}}$ by iterative updates:
  \begin{equation}
    \bm{\alpha} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{w}), \qquad \min_{\mathbf{w}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}\Big(\sum_{i} \alpha_i \theta_i\Big).
    \label{kai0:eq:gd}
  \end{equation}
  \item \emph{Greedy search}~\cite{wortsman2022soups} iteratively adds the checkpoints that reduce the validation loss the most under uniform averaging of the selected candidates.
\end{itemize}
Through validation-guided synthesis in weight space, MA aims to combine the subset policies into a single policy that covers more modes of the data, and thereby to mitigate the bias of $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ induced by coverage deficiency without additional demonstrations. Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_ma} compares the strategies; the strategy used by the full system is discussed in Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:ma}.

\subsection{Stage Advantage}\label{kai0:sec:sa}
Stage Advantage was developed by a co-author, Lirui Zhao (see the statement of contribution); it is described here because the full system uses it. Model Arithmetic targets the bias of $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$, but the merged policy still struggles with long-horizon execution in $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ because of temporal mismatch. Visually similar states in different stages of a task cause the policy to misapply behaviours, which leads to compounding errors and eventually to task failure over long horizons. This stage ambiguity calls for explicit progress signals that can disambiguate the quality of an action within the context of task progress~\cite{chen2025robot}. The key question is therefore \emph{how to provide stable and accurate progress signals during long-horizon execution}.

\paragraph{Advantage as a difference of values.} A prior approach~\cite{amin2026pistar06} uses the advantage as the progress signal and combines it with advantage-weighted regression~\cite{wu2023elastic,kuba2023advantage} to train policies on advantage-weighted training samples. It obtains the advantage implicitly, as the difference between two independently predicted progress values:
\begin{equation}
  A(s, a) = V(s') - V(s).
  \label{kai0:eq:adv_vdiff}
\end{equation}
This formulation amplifies frame-wise estimation noise and yields high-variance training signals. Moreover, estimating global task progress without stage awareness makes $V(s)$ multi-valued in multi-stage tasks, which further degrades the quality of the advantage.

\paragraph{Direct advantage.} To obtain a smoother advantage signal for training, Stage Advantage treats the advantage itself as the modelling target:
\begin{equation}
  A(s, a) = f_{\theta}(s, s'),
  \label{kai0:eq:adv_direct}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\theta}$ predicts the relative progress from $s$ to $s'$. The regression target is itself a difference of progress values, namely the difference between the timestamp-based progress of $s'$ and that of $s$ (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:sa}). The expected benefit therefore does not come from avoiding a difference but from predicting it jointly. For two independent value predictions with errors $e$ and $e'$, the error of $V(s') - V(s)$ has variance $\operatorname{Var}(e) + \operatorname{Var}(e') - 2\operatorname{Cov}(e, e')$ with $\operatorname{Cov}(e, e') \approx 0$; a single network that sees both observations can make errors that are positively correlated across the pair, which lowers this variance. This is an argument for a smoother signal, not evidence that the signal is more accurate (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}). In practice, the advantage estimator is a VLM-based architecture that takes a pair of images as input (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:pipeline}). To avoid overfitting to a fixed temporal discretisation, training pairs are constructed by randomly sampling a time span $\Delta$ and setting $s' = s_{t+\Delta}$.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.58\linewidth]{ch-kai0/images/advantage_v4_1_compress.pdf}
  \caption[Cumulative value based on Stage Advantage]{\textbf{Cumulative value based on Stage Advantage.} Red and green denote negative and positive advantage. \textbf{Top}: Task~A, showing a slipping failure and the recovery from it. \textbf{Middle}: Task~B, showing fetching and a misplaced cloth. \textbf{Bottom}: Task~C, showing pulling over and visual occlusion.}
  \label{kai0:fig:advantage}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Stage conditioning.} To further resolve the multi-valued ambiguity of progress estimation in long-horizon tasks, Stage Advantage decomposes the task into a sequence of semantic stages, each corresponding to a meaningful sub-goal. Instead of evaluating actions over the full task horizon, it estimates whether each action advances the current stage, which gives a stage-aware progress signal:
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{stage}}(s, a, g) = f_{\theta}(s, s' \mid g), \qquad g \in \Big\{0, \frac{1}{S}, \dots, \frac{S-1}{S}\Big\}.
  \label{kai0:eq:adv_stage}
\end{equation}
In practice, the stage $g$ is a normalised scalar derived from manually annotated stage labels, and $S$ is the number of stages (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:pipeline}). Figure~\ref{kai0:fig:advantage} shows the cumulative value obtained from Stage Advantage for the three tasks defined in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:tasks}.

\paragraph{Binary optimality indicator.} Following~\cite{chen2025robot,amin2026pistar06}, the continuous advantage predictions are thresholded into a binary optimality indicator
\begin{equation}
  I = \mathbf{1}[A_{\mathrm{stage}} > \epsilon],
  \label{kai0:eq:indicator}
\end{equation}
where the threshold $\epsilon$ separates progress from non-progress. It is set as a quantile rather than as a fixed value: the training samples are ranked by their predicted advantage and the top fraction $q = 0.3$ is labelled positive, so that $\epsilon$ is the 70th percentile of the predicted advantages (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:sa}). In advantage-weighted policy learning, the indicator shifts the weight towards the samples of $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ that make progress within their stage. Its intended effect is on execution ($P_{\mathrm{test}}$), namely fewer idle or repeated actions; Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa} measures its effect on task metrics, not on the distributions. Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:sa} details the construction of training pairs, the choice of the threshold and the stages of each task.

\subsection{Train--Deploy Alignment}\label{kai0:sec:tda}
Even a robust policy with long-horizon planning ability meets new inconsistencies between $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ when it is deployed. The latency between inference and control misplaces the executed actions and accumulates drift errors. This is especially harmful for action-chunking policies, which output chunks of actions: the gap between model inference and chunk execution breaks the temporal continuity across consecutive chunks, which leads to abrupt transitions and degrades the stability of manipulation. Prior work addresses this problem through chunk interpolation at inference time~\cite{zhao2023aloha,black2025rtc,tang2025vlash}. \chiz additionally adopts \emph{temporal chunk-wise smoothing} to keep action execution coherent during deployment (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:consistency}, bottom left).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.68\linewidth]{ch-kai0/images/consistency_v4.pdf}
  \caption[Train--Deploy Alignment strategies and t-SNE visualisations]{\textbf{Train--Deploy Alignment strategies and t-SNE visualisations.} \textbf{Left}: three complementary strategies for distribution alignment. \textbf{Right}: t-SNE visualisations showing progressive alignment of the distributions as each strategy is applied.}
  \label{kai0:fig:consistency}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Temporal chunk-wise smoothing.} Let the current action buffer $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}$ contain the residual commands from the previous inference cycle, and let $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}$ denote the newly predicted action chunk. We maintain a consumption index $k$ that tracks the actions already executed from the current buffer, a drop threshold $d_{\max}$ that discards commands made stale by inference latency, and a minimum overlap length $m_{\min}$ that keeps the interpolation stable. Algorithm~\ref{kai0:alg:chunk_smoothing} gives the smoothing procedure. After its stale prefix is dropped, the new chunk is blended with the old buffer over their overlap using linearly decaying weights $w_i$, so that execution fades from the old commands to the new ones.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
  \caption{Temporal chunk-wise smoothing}\label{kai0:alg:chunk_smoothing}
  \KwIn{current buffer $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}$, consumption index $k$, new chunk $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}$, drop threshold $d_{\max}$, minimum overlap $m_{\min}$}
  \KwOut{updated buffer $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{buf}}$ and reset index $k \leftarrow 0$}
  $d \leftarrow \min(k, d_{\max})$\tcp*{compute drop amount}
  \If{$d \ge |\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}|$}{
    \Return{$\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}, k$}\tcp*{ignore update}
  }
  $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{rem}} \leftarrow (a^{\mathrm{new}}_{d}, \dots)$\tcp*{remaining new commands}
  \If{$|\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}| < m_{\min}$}{
    pad $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}$ by repeating its last command\;
  }
  $L \leftarrow \min(|\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{old}}|, |\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{rem}}|)$\tcp*{overlap length}
  \For{$i \leftarrow 0$ \KwTo $L-1$}{
    $w_i \leftarrow 1 - i/\max(L-1, 1)$\;
    $\tilde{a}_i \leftarrow w_i\, a^{\mathrm{old}}_i + (1 - w_i)\, a^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{rem},i}$\;
  }
  $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{buf}} \leftarrow (\tilde{a}_0, \dots, \tilde{a}_{L-1}) \,\Vert\, (\text{suffix of } \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{rem}})$\;
  $k \leftarrow 0$\tcp*{reset consumption index}
  \Return{$\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{buf}}, k$}\;
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Relation to other execution methods.} Chunk-wise smoothing is a linear cross-fade between the remainder of the current chunk and the new chunk. It differs from the temporal ensembling of ACT~\cite{zhao2023aloha}, which queries the policy at every control step and averages all overlapping predictions for the same step with exponentially decaying weights: chunk-wise smoothing blends only two chunks and needs no additional inference. It also differs from RTC~\cite{black2025rtc}, which conditions the generation of the new chunk on the actions that will be executed while inference runs, an inpainting formulation of the sampling process: chunk-wise smoothing leaves the output of the model unchanged, acts only on the executed commands, and compensates for latency only by dropping the stale prefix. Because the two methods act at different points, they can be combined, as in Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_tda}.

\paragraph{Closing the loop from deployment to training.} With a robust policy and a reliable deployment pipeline in place, a natural question arises: \emph{can rollout experience from $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ expand $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ without extended data collection effort?} Static demonstrations lack recovery behaviours, which leaves the policy vulnerable to failure cascades. \chiz addresses this final inconsistency by closing the loop between deployment and training with two complementary strategies.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item On-policy DAgger~\cite{ross2011dagger,kelly2019hgdagger} expands $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ towards failure-adjacent regions, but it is time-consuming, because it requires waiting for failures to occur naturally during policy rollouts. \emph{Heuristic DAgger} instead initialises the system directly in manually designed failure states, such as misaligned grasps or partial drops, and collects human recovery demonstrations from them; no policy is executed during collection (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:consistency}, top left). This front-loads failure experience into data collection. Both variants add demonstrations targeted at the states where the policy fails, so their gains are gains from targeted data.
  \item To diversify $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ further at zero robot time, \chiz applies \emph{spatio-temporal augmentation}: horizontal flipping with swapping of the left and right arms~\cite{li2025gr}, and partial frame skipping that synthesises variations in speed (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:consistency}).
\end{enumerate}
Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:tda} details the protocols of standard and heuristic DAgger.

\section{System and Data Setup}\label{kai0:sec:setup}

The evaluation targets collaborative long-horizon garment manipulation, which covers flattening from arbitrary states, folding, handover and hanging. \subsection{Metrics}\label{kai0:sec:metrics}
Each configuration is evaluated in 30 trials, 10 for each of three garment types, and we report four metrics, each as a mean over the 30 trials (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:protocol} discusses the error bars).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Success rate (SR)} is the percentage of trials that complete the task successfully (higher is better).
  \item \textbf{Throughput (TP)} is the estimated number of tasks completed per hour (higher is better).
  \item \textbf{Retry cost} is the average number of action retries per episode during evaluation (lower is better).
  \item \textbf{Average score} is derived from a rule-based evaluation protocol that defines task-specific milestones, assigns partial credit when each milestone is completed and normalises the credits to 100 (higher is better). Table~\ref{kai0:tab:score} in Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:exp} lists the milestones and their scores.
\end{itemize}
For the ablation of Stage Advantage, we additionally quantify the numerical stability of the advantage signal with the smooth frame ratio (SFR, higher is better) and the mean squared temporal difference (MSTD, lower is better). Both measure how smoothly the predicted advantage varies over time, not whether it is correct: a constant predictor would be perfectly smooth.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Uncertainty}\label{kai0:sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Protocol.} All evaluations run in closed loop on the two robots of Section~\ref{kai0:sec:platform}, in the scene in which the training data were collected, so that errors can compound within a trial. Each configuration is evaluated in 30 trials, 10 for each of three garment types. A trial is judged by the success criterion and time limit of its task (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:tasks} and Table~\ref{kai0:tab:tasks}) and scored with the milestones of Table~\ref{kai0:tab:score}. The garment starts from an arbitrary configuration; during data collection, the initial conditions were randomised in object state and illumination (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:data}). The guidelines for real-robot policy evaluation of Kress-Gazit~\etal~\cite{kressgazit2024robot} also call for stating how initial states are generated and matched across policies, whether the evaluator knows which policy is running, whether policies are run interleaved or in blocks, and whether the evaluation objects appear in the training data. These details are not recorded in this thesis \authorcheck{describe how initial garment states were generated and matched across policies, whether evaluators were blind to the policy, whether policies were run interleaved or in blocks, which garments were used for evaluation and whether they appeared in the training data, how throughput was estimated and how a retry was counted}. Without them, the evaluation tests closed-loop execution under the conditions of the training data, not transfer to new robots, scenes or garments.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.} All values in this chapter are read from bar charts and are accurate to about one unit of the respective axis. The error bars in the figures were reported as an ``accumulative standard error'', a statistic that is not defined, and some of them are narrower than 30 binary trials allow. For example, the success rate of +SA in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple} (about 47\%, that is, 14 of 30 trials) carries an error bar of about $\pm 1$ point, whereas the standard error of the three per-garment success rates is at least about 3 points and the binomial standard error about 9 points (computed from the approximate count) \authorcheck{replace the chart-read values in this chapter with the exact success counts per configuration and garment type from the evaluation records, and define the error-bar statistic from the evaluation scripts or replace the error bars with binomial intervals}. We therefore do not use the error bars to compare configurations. For success rates, we instead infer counts from the bar heights, which with 30 trials lie on multiples of about 3.3 points (a bar at about 27\% corresponds to 8 of 30 trials), and report approximate 95\% Wilson score intervals and two-sided Fisher exact tests computed from these counts. With 30 trials per configuration, a Wilson interval near a success rate of 50\% is about 34 percentage points wide, and a difference of four or fewer successes between two configurations is never distinguishable from sampling error (Fisher $p > 0.1$ for every such pair). Throughput, retry cost and average score are reported without intervals, because the per-trial values are not available. The ablation grids of Figs.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_souping} and~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control} contain many pairwise comparisons, so even a difference at the conventional 5\% level would need a correction for multiple comparisons. None of these derived statistics replaces the exact counts.

\subsection{Data Collection and Training}\label{kai0:sec:data}
We curate about 20 hours of expert demonstrations per task, capturing diverse garments (colour and material), initial states and lighting conditions. Multiplying the episode counts of Table~\ref{kai0:tab:tasks} by their mean durations gives about 22.5, 33.6 and 32.4 hours of recorded data for Tasks~A, B and~C, which include DAgger interventions in addition to the expert demonstrations. The base policy is fine-tuned with all parameters on 8$\times$A100 GPUs using a flow-matching objective~\cite{black2024pi0}. These figures describe the data and hardware for post-training a heavily pre-trained $\pi_{0.5}$ policy~\cite{black2025pi05}, not for training a policy from scratch; they are not a compute or robot-time budget (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:limitations}). Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:exp} lists the hyper-parameters (Table~\ref{kai0:tab:hyperparams}) and the details of data collection.

\section{Experiments}\label{kai0:sec:exp}

All evaluations are performed on the real robots of Section~\ref{kai0:sec:setup}. They address four research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{System efficacy.} How do the individual components work together to improve the overall performance, and do they conflict when integrated? This question is studied on Task~A only. (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_overall})
  \item \textbf{Model Arithmetic.} Can merging subset-trained candidates outperform both the best single candidate among them and a candidate trained on the full data? Which validation split, in-domain or OOD, gives consistently better results across merging strategies? (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_ma})
  \item \textbf{Stage Advantage.} Does predicting a stage-conditioned advantage give more stable supervision than a baseline inspired by RECAP in $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$~\cite{amin2026pistar06}, which derives the advantage from a difference of predicted values, and how does this translate into policy success? (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa})
  \item \textbf{Train--Deploy Alignment.} Does expanding $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ with heuristic DAgger improve performance while incurring only a marginal increase in retry cost compared with standard DAgger? How do different control methods work with spatio-temporal data augmentation? (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_tda})
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Baselines and Ablation Design}\label{kai0:sec:baselines}

\paragraph{Base policy.} We select $\pi_{0.5}$ as the primary base policy, complemented by $\pi_{0}$, because these were the only two of the open-source policies we tried that achieved viable performance on the tasks. GO-1, introduced with the AgiBot World platform~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros} (co-authored by the author), X-VLA~\cite{zheng2025xvla} and DexVLA~\cite{wen2025dexvla} did not complete the tasks reliably even after training on the full 20-hour dataset, and neither did UniVLA and OpenVLA in preliminary evaluations (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:questions}, Q3). Their success rates are not reported in this thesis.

\paragraph{MA ablations.} We establish two baselines: a \emph{single-best candidate}, selected among the policies trained on individual subsets, and a \emph{full-data candidate}, trained on the aggregated dataset. We then compare the merging strategies of Section~\ref{kai0:sec:ma}, namely average weighting, inverse-loss weighting, gradient descent and its adaptive variant, and greedy search, each with an in-domain and an OOD validation split, to examine which split gives consistently better results.

\paragraph{SA ablations.} We compare against a value-difference baseline inspired by RECAP, the advantage-conditioning method of $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$~\cite{amin2026pistar06}, which we implemented ourselves. It is built on the PaliGemma backbone of $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{black2025pi05} and trained to estimate progress from the current frame, and its advantage is the difference between the predicted progress of the current frame and that of the frame 50 steps later in the same trajectory. It is a simplified re-implementation, not $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$ itself: RECAP learns a distributional value function from demonstrations, on-policy rollouts and expert interventions (Section~\ref{sec:bg:manip}), whereas the baseline regresses a scalar progress value from the current frame. In the figures, this baseline is denoted $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$-style; it is compared with direct advantage estimation (Direct, Eq.~\eqref{kai0:eq:adv_direct}) and with the full Stage Advantage (Direct+Stage, Eq.~\eqref{kai0:eq:adv_stage}).

\paragraph{TDA ablations.} We compare temporal chunk-wise smoothing against synchronous and asynchronous inference~\cite{tang2025vlash}, temporal ensembling~\cite{zhao2023aloha} and RTC~\cite{black2025rtc}. The control methods are evaluated under different spatio-temporal augmentation settings to identify the best configuration. In addition, we assess the impact of the recovery data by comparing standard DAgger~\cite{ross2011dagger} with heuristic DAgger, examining both the difference in performance and whether the findings hold for both the $\pi_{0}$~\cite{black2024pi0} and the $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{black2025pi05} architectures.

\subsection{System Efficacy Breakdown}\label{kai0:sec:exp_overall}
Figure~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple} breaks down the contribution of each module of \chiz on Task~A, and Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA} lists the values. Each module uses the configuration selected in the ablations of Section~\ref{kai0:sec:ablation}. The effects are not uniform across configurations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Adding TDA raised the success rate in every pairing, by about 17 to 53 points. Its effect on the retry cost depended on the configuration: the cost rose when TDA was added to +MA or to +SA (from about 1.8 to 2.6 and from about 1.1 to 2.3) and fell when TDA was added to $\pi_{0.5}$ or to +MA+SA. One interpretation, not tested separately, is that recovery data encourage the policy to keep retrying, which can raise completion at the cost of more retries.
  \item Adding SA raised both the success rate and the throughput in every pairing, and the configurations with SA had the highest throughput.
  \item MA alone did not raise the success rate (about 30\% against about 27\%, 9 against 8 of 30 trials) and lowered the average score (about 35 against 41). Added to +TDA or to +SA, however, MA raised the success rate by about 30 points (from 16 to 25 and from 14 to 24 of 30 trials; Fisher $p \approx 0.025$ and $0.015$, before any correction for multiple comparisons).
  \item The full system had the highest success rate and average score, but its throughput (about 66) was similar to that of +SA+TDA (about 68), and its success rate cannot be distinguished at $n = 30$ from that of any of the three pairwise configurations (29 against 24 to 26 of 30 trials; Fisher $p \ge 0.10$). No single component differs from $\pi_{0.5}$ by more than sampling error either (Fisher $p \ge 0.06$); the differences that exceed it involve at least two components.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ablation Studies}\label{kai0:sec:ablation}

This section analyses each component of \chiz in isolation, following research questions 2 to 4 of Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp}.

\paragraph{Consistency across figures.} The ablations were not run on the base configuration of Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple}, and their values do not always agree with it or with each other:
\begin{itemize}
  \item On Task~A, the $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline reaches a success rate of about 27\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple} and about 23\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_a}, and the synchronous-inference control reaches about 37\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control}.

Heuristic DAgger also raises the retry cost from about 3.0 to about 8.6 in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_a}, whereas +TDA lowers it from about 3.0 to about 1.7 in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple}.
  \item Chunk-wise smoothing alone has a retry cost of about 12 in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control}, against about 1.1 for the full system. The same configuration (chunk-wise smoothing without augmentation) appears to be shown in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_tda_a_abs}: its throughput, retry cost and average score coincide with those in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control}, but its success rate is about 70\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_tda_a_abs} and about 77\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_aug_control}.
  \item The value-difference baseline of the Stage Advantage ablation reaches about 67\% on Task~A (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_advantage}), Direct+Stage about 80\%, and the full-data candidate of the Model Arithmetic ablation about 83\% (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_soup_a}), whereas +SA reaches about 47\% and $\pi_{0.5}$ about 27\% in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_triple}.
  \item On Task~C, the reference configurations reach about 10\% (Figs.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_c} and~\ref{kai0:fig:app_sa_c}), about 20\% (full-data candidate, Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_souping}) and about 37\% (synchronous inference, Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_tda_c_abs}).

\subsection{Model Arithmetic}\label{kai0:sec:exp_ma}
Figure~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_souping} compares the MA variants with the baselines without merging on Task~C across all metrics, and it quantifies the differences between merging strategies.

\paragraph{Merging.} Every merged variant has a higher mean success rate and throughput than both the single-best candidate and the full-data candidate, although some strategies increase the retry cost. Most of these differences are within sampling error. Only the best variant, greedy search with OOD validation (about 15 of 30 successes), differs from the full-data candidate (about 6 of 30) at the conventional 5\% level (Fisher $p \approx 0.03$), and only before any correction for multiple comparisons, since it was picked as the best of the merged variants; against the single-best candidate (about 7 of 30), the same comparison gives $p \approx 0.06$. The single-best candidate also has a higher average score (about 70) than most merged variants; only greedy search and OOD inverse-loss weighting exceed it. On this task, every merged policy thus had a higher mean success rate and throughput than training on the combined dataset (joint training), mostly within sampling error. The reason is not established. One possible explanation, which rests on a single task and was not tested, is parameter redundancy in fine-tuned VLAs, akin to that reported for large language models~\cite{yu2024language}.

\paragraph{Validation split.} OOD validation raised the success rate of three of the four strategies that use a validation split (inverse-loss weighting from about 30\% to 47\%, gradient descent from about 27\% to 33\%, greedy search from about 40\% to 50\%) and left adaptive gradient descent at about 40\%. It lowered the retry cost of three strategies, but it reduced the throughput of adaptive gradient descent and greedy search. For each strategy, the difference between the two splits is within sampling error (for example 12 against 15 successes for greedy search, Fisher $p \approx 0.60$). These results are consistent with the idea that OOD data such as DAgger rollouts reflect the deployment conditions better than in-domain data, and hence with using them to weight the modes of $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, but they do not establish it. Among the merging strategies, greedy search gave the highest (or joint highest) success rate, the highest average score and the lowest retry cost on Task~C under both splits, but not the highest throughput. Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:ablation_ma} reports the same ablation on Tasks~A and~B, where the benefit of merging holds in most but not all cases and the benefit of selection by validation loss is less consistent. The strategy used by the full system of Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA} is discussed in Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:ma}.

\subsection{Stage Advantage}\label{kai0:sec:exp_sa}
Figure~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_advantage} compares Stage Advantage (Direct+Stage), a co-author's component, with the value-difference baseline ($\pi^{*}_{0.6}$-style) and with direct advantage estimation without stages (Direct) on Tasks~A and~B.

\paragraph{Smoothness of the advantage signal.} On both tasks, Direct+Stage gives a smoother advantage signal than the baseline: its SFR is higher (about 60\% against 46\% on Task~A and about 77\% against 52\% on Task~B), and its MSTD is lower on Task~B (about 0.58 against 0.82) and about the same on Task~A (about 1.0 against 1.06). Without stages, direct estimation is smoother than the baseline by the SFR but not by the MSTD on Task~B, where it also has the lowest success rate and the highest retry cost of the three; this is consistent with the need for stage-aware labels. Smoothness, however, is not correctness: a constant predictor would be perfectly smooth, and the advantage signal was not validated against held-out progress labels or against segments known to succeed or fail.

\paragraph{Task performance.} The effect on the policy differs by task. On Task~A, Direct+Stage improves every task metric over the baseline, but its success rate (about 24 of 30 trials) is within sampling error of that of the baseline (about 20 of 30; Fisher $p \approx 0.38$). On Task~B, the long-horizon conditional task, it lowers the retry cost (about 10 against 17) and raises the average score (about 48 against 37), which is consistent with the intended effect of biasing the policy against idling and spurious retries; its success rate (about 17\% against 23\%) and throughput (about 4 against 6), however, are below those of the baseline. On Task~C (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:ablation_sa}), it lowers the retry cost but also lowers the success rate, the throughput and the average score. The training loss of Stage Advantage settles at a lower value than that of the baseline (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:loss}); a lower training loss is not by itself evidence of a better advantage signal.

\subsection{Train--Deploy Alignment}\label{kai0:sec:exp_tda}

\paragraph{Heuristic DAgger.} Figures~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_a} and~\ref{kai0:fig:exp_dagger_c} evaluate heuristic and standard DAgger across models ($\pi_{0.5}$ and $\pi_0$) and tasks (Tasks~A and~C). DAgger-style recovery data give the largest gains observed in this chapter. On Task~A, heuristic DAgger raises the success rate of $\pi_{0.5}$ from about 23\% to about 87\% (about 7 against 26 of 30 trials) and that of $\pi_0$ from near zero to about 83\%; on Task~C, it raises the success rate from about 10\% to about 43\% (3 against 13 of 30). These data are additional demonstrations targeted at the states where the policy fails, so the gain is a gain from targeted data rather than from alignment without data. Heuristic DAgger also raises the retry cost in every case, for $\pi_{0.5}$ on Task~A from about 3.0 to about 8.6. The low retry cost of the base policy reflects an absence of self-correcting behaviour rather than efficient recovery. Standard DAgger reaches similar success rates (about 93\% for $\pi_{0.5}$ on Task~A and about 53\% on Task~C; the differences from heuristic DAgger, 26 against 28 and 13 against 16 of 30 trials, are within sampling error), with lower retry costs (about 3.4 against 8.6 for $\pi_{0.5}$ on Task~A, and about 1.5 against 2.4 on Task~C) and a higher average score on Task~C (about 83 against 62). For $\pi_{0.5}$ on Task~A, standard DAgger also gives a higher throughput (about 50 against 36), a better trade-off. Heuristic DAgger needs no policy inference during collection, so it should cost less robot and operator time per demonstration; the time spent on either procedure is not reported, so the saving is not quantified.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{kai0:sec:limitations}

\chiz targets distribution shifts across the robot learning cycle: Model Arithmetic, Stage Advantage and Train--Deploy Alignment target the failure modes of coverage deficiency, temporal mismatch and failure cascade, and on Task~A the integrated system raised the success rate of $\pi_{0.5}$ from about 27\% to about 97\%. The evidence behind this result has clear limits, which we state first; the limitations of the approach follow.

\paragraph{Evidence for the central hypothesis.} The claim that inconsistency rather than scale limits robustness is a hypothesis. It is supported by an integrated system result on one task, Task~A, with 30 trials per configuration. Scale was not varied at a matched budget: we did not train $\pi_{0.5}$ on the same total robot and operator hours of plain demonstrations, which is the control that would separate consistency from scale. The largest single gain in the chapter comes from DAgger-style recovery data (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_tda}), which is targeted data collection, so the results do not separate the two cleanly. Component effects also depend on the task and on the configuration (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:ablation}). The full system was evaluated only on Task~A, the easiest of the three tasks; Tasks~B and~C have component ablations but no full-system comparison.

\paragraph{Resource budget.} We call the approach resource-aware because it post-trains a pre-trained policy with about 20 hours of demonstrations per task on 8 A100 GPUs. This describes data and hardware, not a budget. The GPU hours per component (the four subset models of Model Arithmetic, the advantage estimator of Stage Advantage and the final policy), the robot and operator hours of data collection, including standard and heuristic DAgger and the DAgger validation data of Model Arithmetic, and the corresponding figures for the $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline are not reported \authorcheck{report the GPU hours per component, the robot and operator hours per task including DAgger (and how the hours of Table~\ref{kai0:tab:tasks} split into expert demonstrations and DAgger data), and the same figures for the $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline; state whether the 80{,}000 training steps of Table~\ref{kai0:tab:hyperparams} apply to each subset model}. Without these numbers, the resource claim cannot be compared with scaling alternatives.

\paragraph{What the evaluation tests.} The evaluation is closed loop on real robots, so errors compound within a trial. It is run on the same two robots and in the same scene as the training data, and it is not recorded whether the evaluation garments appeared in training (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:protocol}). The chapter therefore tests robustness to varied initial states and to the consequences of the policy's own actions, not transfer to new robots, scenes or garments.

\paragraph{Statistics and consistency.} With 30 trials per configuration, differences of up to four successes are within sampling error, and several of the component comparisons fall in this range (Sections~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_overall} and~\ref{kai0:sec:ablation}). The values are read from bar charts, the error-bar statistic is not defined, and some results disagree across figures (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:ablation}). The ablations therefore support only the direction of the larger effects.

\paragraph{Scalability.} The prominence of robot foundation models rests on their promise of broad generalisation. This study, however, did not explicitly evaluate how well pre-trained priors are retained during post-training. Future work should address this retention and evaluate whether \chiz extends to the manipulation of rigid objects. It also remains to be seen whether Model Arithmetic can integrate policies for distinct tasks, rather than merely policies trained on subsets of the data of one task, to advance general-purpose robotics.

\paragraph{Data valuation.} In our experience, the utility of data varies widely: we observed that data quality strongly affects policy performance, with success rates between 20\% and 60\% under otherwise identical settings (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:questions}). This is an informal observation, not the result of a controlled experiment. Validating data quality currently requires costly full training runs or slow replay checks that cannot be parallelised, in which a successful replay indicates high utility. Establishing efficient, predictive metrics of data quality without these overheads remains a key challenge for future research.

\paragraph{Advantage design and failure modes.} Stage Advantage relies on a heuristic proxy, temporal progress, to label the advantage, and this proxy assumes that task completion is strictly monotonic. A more robust solution would be an unsupervised advantage estimator that distinguishes truly instrumental actions from noise, independently of temporal linearity. The advantage signal was, moreover, evaluated for smoothness rather than for correctness; validating it against held-out progress labels or against segments known to succeed or fail is future work. The two dominant failure modes, spatial misalignment and policy stagnation, point to a lack of fine-grained spatial understanding and of long-horizon task planning in current pre-trained models. We expect Model Arithmetic and Stage Advantage to mitigate them only partly, as extrinsic corrections; the failure analysis does not measure this per component (Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:questions} and Appendix~\ref{kai0:app:failure}).

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{kai0:sec:summary}

This chapter addressed RQ3(b): how a learned manipulation policy can remain reliable when deployment departs from training, under the data and compute constraints of real robots. It put forward the hypothesis that the fragility of long-horizon manipulation stems not only from insufficient scale but from inconsistencies among three distributions, namely the expert demonstrations $P_{\mathrm{train}}$, the learned policy $Q_{\mathrm{model}}$ and the executed trajectories $P_{\mathrm{test}}$, which manifest as coverage deficiency, temporal mismatch and failure cascades; the three distributions serve as conceptual vocabulary and are not measured. \chiz targets each pairwise mismatch with a dedicated component. Model Arithmetic merges policies trained on data subsets, with weights chosen by the validation loss on out-of-distribution DAgger data. Stage Advantage, developed by a co-author, provides a direct, stage-conditioned advantage signal for advantage-weighted behaviour cloning. Train--Deploy Alignment combines heuristic DAgger, spatio-temporal augmentation and temporal chunk-wise smoothing.

On Task~A of collaborative garment manipulation with two dual-arm robots, the only task on which the full system was evaluated, \chiz raised the success rate of $\pi_{0.5}$ from about 27\% to about 97\% over 30 trials (about 8 against 29 successes, far outside sampling error), a relative improvement of about 250\%, with a roughly tenfold increase in throughput (Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA}). It did so by post-training the heavily pre-trained $\pi_{0.5}$ policy with about 20 hours of demonstrations per task on 8 A100 GPUs; the budget in GPU, robot and operator hours is not reported. The ablations, reported task by task, give a mixed picture. DAgger-style recovery data gave the largest single gain (on Task~A, from about 23\% to 87--93\%), which is a gain from targeted data. Merged policies outperformed the best single-subset policy and the full-data policy in most cases, but MA alone did not raise the success rate on Task~A, and on Task~C only the best merged variant differed from full-data training by more than sampling error. Validation on OOD DAgger data gave the best merged policy on Task~C but was less consistent on Tasks~A and~B. Stage Advantage gave smoother advantage signals than a value-difference baseline on all three tasks, but it raised the success rate only on Task~A, and there within sampling error. Chunk-wise smoothing combined with RTC gave the best control configuration on Task~A, although smoothing alone had the highest retry cost in two settings. The limitations are equally clear. The claim that inconsistency rather than scale limits robustness remains a hypothesis, since scale was not varied at a matched budget. The evaluation is confined to garment manipulation on the robots and in the scene of the training data, with 30 trials per configuration and values read from charts, and the ablations do not share a stated base configuration. The retention of pre-trained priors, rigid-object tasks and the merging of policies for different tasks remain untested, and data quality, which in our experience moved success rates between 20\% and 60\% (an informal observation), still lacks an efficient predictive measure.

Across the two chapters of the Act pillar, a common lesson emerges. In driving (Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}) and in manipulation (this chapter), robustness improved when the loop between training and deployment was closed. Centaur adapts at test time using its own uncertainty, on logged frames in non-reactive simulation, whereas \chiz feeds deployment experience back into training: DAgger recovery data becomes both training data and the validation signal for merging, and execution-aware smoothing reconciles what the model predicts with what the robot does. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} synthesises these findings into lessons for self-correcting architectures.
