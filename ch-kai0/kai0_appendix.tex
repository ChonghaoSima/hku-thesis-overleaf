\section{Motivating Questions}\label{kai0:app:questions}

This section collects questions that readers may raise, together with questions that reach beyond the scope of this work.

\paragraph{Q1. What is the relationship between Stage Advantage and reinforcement learning?}
Stage Advantage follows the paradigm of advantage-weighted regression (AWR)~\cite{peng2019awr}, which can be seen as offline reinforcement learning (RL)~\cite{sutton1998reinforcement} in which the Monte Carlo reward is computed directly from data. Because such learned rewards show a large variance, advantage-weighted diffusion policies~\cite{frans2025diffusion}, and their large-scale validation in $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$~\cite{amin2026pistar06}, instead parameterise a value function learned from data. Stage Advantage, a co-author's component of \chiz, follows the paradigm of $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$. Compared with our value-difference baseline inspired by it, Stage Advantage gives smoother advantage estimates on all three tasks; the policy trained with it performed better on Task~A, within sampling error, but not on Tasks~B and~C in success rate (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}).

\paragraph{Q2. Why not use online RL such as PPO?}
PPO~\cite{schulman2017PPO} and its adaptations to diffusion-based policies, such as DSRL~\cite{wagenmaker2025dsrl}, represent another trend in RL, referred to as online RL, which learns directly from interaction with the environment. These methods are fundamentally limited by sample inefficiency in the real world. Unlike in simulation, scaling physical experiments is constrained by the prohibitive costs of parallelisation and resetting, which constitute a major bottleneck. In contrast, AWR~\cite{peng2019awr} incurs only a marginal labelling cost to bias the learning objective towards actions that make high progress, and it thereby maximises the utility of both human demonstrations and policy rollouts.

\paragraph{Q3. Why choose $\pi_{0.5}$ as the primary baseline?}
To identify a robust starting point, we conducted preliminary evaluations of several prominent VLA models, including X-VLA~\cite{zheng2025xvla}, GO-1 (introduced with AgiBot World~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros}, co-authored by the author), DexVLA~\cite{wen2025dexvla}, UniVLA~\cite{bu2025univla} and OpenVLA~\cite{kim2024openvla}. These models did not complete the experimental tasks reliably. Even after extensive post-training with aligned hardware settings and hyper-parameter tuning, with a converged training loss, they performed too poorly in 30 trials (10 trials per garment) to serve as baselines; their success rates are not reported in this thesis. These models were therefore excluded from the primary comparison, so that the analysis focuses on baselines capable of meaningful task completion.

\paragraph{Q4. How can an informative advantage be defined and designed?}
This is a critical direction for future work. Naive behaviour cloning treats all demonstrated actions equally, although many of them lack explicit utility. Training on such non-informative segments can bias the policy towards meaningless behaviours, resulting in local optima in which the robot repeats actions without making progress. Advantage weighting offers a mechanism to filter out these behaviours by emphasising actions that yield high returns. The current formulation, however, relies on a heuristic proxy, namely using temporal progress to label the advantage, which assumes that task completion is strictly monotonic. A more robust solution would replace this heuristic with an unsupervised advantage estimator capable of distinguishing truly instrumental actions from noise, independently of temporal linearity.

\paragraph{Q5. How can good robot data be defined?}
In our experience, data quality strongly affects policy performance: we observed success rates between 20\% and 60\% under otherwise identical settings. This is an informal observation, not the result of a controlled experiment. We also observe a ``masking effect'': some algorithmic improvements yield substantial gains on low-quality data but saturate or vanish when the policy is trained on high-quality demonstrations. Data quality appears to be central to the current trend of imitation learning, yet little of the literature discusses it seriously. To address this overlooked variable, we state \emph{replay-ability} as one of the most important principles of data validity. A trajectory is replay-able if its open-loop re-execution from a similar initial state leads to task completion, or at least to substantial progress. This criterion not only filters out corrupted demonstrations but also serves as a diagnostic tool that exposes systematic hardware inconsistencies between the data collection environment ($P_{\mathrm{train}}$) and the inference environment ($P_{\mathrm{test}}$).

\paragraph{Q6. What are the common failure cases?}
We observe two primary failure modes: (1)~\emph{spatial misalignment}, in which the policy fails to localise the correct grasping affordance, and (2)~\emph{policy stagnation}, in which the robot enters a ``dead loop'' of repetitive, non-productive actions. Both are illustrated in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:failure_case}. These failures reveal two fundamental deficiencies of current pre-trained models: a lack of fine-grained spatial understanding and insufficient long-horizon task planning. We expect Model Arithmetic to mitigate the former and Stage Advantage the latter only partly, as extrinsic corrections; the failure analysis of Section~\ref{kai0:app:failure} does not measure this per component. We argue that future pre-trained weights must possess stronger intrinsic capabilities for spatial grounding and sequential logic to resolve these issues fully.

\paragraph{Q7. What fundamentally distinguishes different robot foundation models?}
Consistent with the failure modes above, the core distinction lies not in the zero-shot capabilities of the models but in their fine-tuning dynamics, specifically in their plasticity in acquiring new spatial understanding and task-planning skills during post-training. In our preliminary evaluations (Q3), $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ adapted to our tasks far better than the other models we tried, which suggests that their pre-trained weights provide a more robust initialisation for downstream learning. The field therefore needs new metrics that benchmark the intrinsic representational quality of foundation models, rather than relying solely on success rates in simplistic evaluation environments.

\section{Additional Related Work}\label{kai0:app:related}

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Robustness}\label{kai0:app:related_rl}
Reinforcement learning has become a prevalent approach for post-training VLA foundation policies to improve the robustness and precision of manipulation, with applications both in simulation~\cite{liu2023libero,mu2025robotwin,mees2022calvin,lu2025vla,li2025simplevla,chen2025tgrpo} and in the real world~\cite{luo2025hilserl,xiao2025PLD,luo2024serl}. A central challenge is to fine-tune large pre-trained models without destabilising the learned representations. Advantage-conditioned approaches~\cite{amin2026pistar06,frans2025diffusion,kumar2019reward} allow optimisation of the full model by conditioning generation on estimated advantages, which avoids differentiating through the denoising processes of diffusion or flow matching~\cite{lei2025rl}. The effectiveness of such methods hinges on the quality of the advantage signal. A popular recipe uses vision-language models to score task progress~\cite{ma2024gvl,zhang2025rewind,zhai2025vlac,ghasemipour2025self}, recovers advantages from the scores as $A(s,a) = V(s') - V(s)$~\cite{amin2026pistar06} and uses them in advantage-weighted regression~\cite{wu2023elastic,kuba2023advantage}. This difference-of-values formulation, however, compounds independent estimation errors into noisy training signals. Moreover, without stage awareness the value function produces ambiguous predictions when visually similar states appear in different phases of a multi-stage task~\cite{amin2026pistar06,li2025gr}. Stage Advantage, a co-author's component of \chiz, addresses both limitations. Instead of subtracting two independent value predictions, it predicts the advantage jointly from the pair of observations, $f_{\theta}(s, s')$, so that its errors on the two states can be correlated and partly cancel (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:sa}); the resulting signal is smoother in our measurements (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}), although smoothness does not establish accuracy. Instead of estimating progress under a single global objective, it conditions on the goal of the current stage, which is intended to resolve the multi-valued ambiguity and to provide stage-aware supervision for long-horizon policy optimisation.

\subsection{Control Methods}\label{kai0:app:related_control}
Asynchronous inference reduces latency, but existing methods face substantial implementation hurdles. SmolVLA~\cite{shukor2025smolvla} employs a naive chunk-switching strategy, which results in severe misalignment between prediction and execution and in unstable control. Similarly, the concurrent A2C2~\cite{sendai2025leave} addresses the misalignment by adding auxiliary correction heads, which necessitates architectural modifications. In contrast, \chiz augments asynchronous inference with temporal chunk-wise smoothing, which adds only a linear blend of two action buffers (Algorithm~\ref{kai0:alg:chunk_smoothing}) and no architectural change.

\section{Method Details}\label{kai0:app:method}

\subsection{Origin of the Name}\label{kai0:app:name}
The approach is grounded in the principle of \textbf{K}inetics \textbf{A}ligned to \textbf{I}ntelligence. ``Kinetics'' defines the operational domain, spanning training ($P_{\mathrm{train}}$) and deployment ($P_{\mathrm{test}}$), while ``Intelligence'' denotes the learned policy encoded in the parameters ($Q_{\mathrm{model}}$). This initial iteration is designated KAI\textsubscript{0}. Noting the phonetic resemblance between ``KAI'' and the Greek letter $\chi$, the system is named \chiz, in tribute to the state-of-the-art $\pi$ series of policies.

\subsection{Model Arithmetic}\label{kai0:app:ma}
We combine 4 checkpoints with Model Arithmetic, each trained on a different subset of the training data. In the implementation described here, the checkpoints are weighted with inverse-loss weighting: the validation loss is computed as the mean prediction error on a held-out set, and checkpoints with lower loss receive proportionally higher weights. The Task~C ablation (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_ma}), however, found greedy search the most effective strategy, so the strategy and validation split used by the full system of Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA} are to be confirmed \authorcheck{state which merging strategy (inverse-loss weighting or greedy search) and which validation split (in-domain or OOD) the full system of Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA} uses}. We additionally consider two baselines: a \emph{single-best} candidate, selected as the individual checkpoint with the lowest validation loss, and a \emph{full-data} candidate, trained on the complete dataset merged across all subsets.

\subsection{Stage Advantage}\label{kai0:app:sa}
The advantage estimator of Stage Advantage (a co-author's component) is trained on pairs of frames sampled at arbitrary timestamps from the same episode. The supervision target is their relative progress, obtained by subtracting the progress of the two states as given by their timestamps within the episode. When training \chiz, the threshold $\epsilon$ of Eq.~\eqref{kai0:eq:indicator} is set as a quantile, following the choice of $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$: the training samples are ranked by their predicted advantage, the top fraction $q = 0.3$ is labelled positive and the rest is treated as negative, so that $\epsilon$ is the 70th percentile of the predicted advantages. Stages correspond to semantic sub-goals within each long-horizon task: two stages for Task~A (flattening and folding), four for Task~B (retrieving, flattening, folding and handover) and three for Task~C (retrieving, dressing the rack and hanging).

\subsection{Train--Deploy Alignment}\label{kai0:app:tda}
Standard DAgger data are collected in three steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The environment is initialised and policy inference starts.
  \item When the policy fails repeatedly, defined as failing to grasp the clothes for more than 10 consecutive attempts or producing repetitive action patterns without progress for more than 10 steps, it is interrupted. On the software side, the policy stops receiving new observations and produces no further actions; on the hardware side, the robot is held in its current state and switched to teleoperation mode to accept human correction.
  \item Both the human correction and the preceding policy rollout are saved.
\end{enumerate}
Heuristic DAgger removes the policy from this process. The operator places the garment and the robot directly in a manually designed failure state of the kind at which standard DAgger interrupts the policy, such as a misaligned grasp or a partial drop, and records a human expert demonstration from that state. No policy is executed during collection (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:tda} and Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:consistency}).

\section{Experimental Details}\label{kai0:app:exp}

\subsection{Data, Training Strategy and Evaluation Criteria}\label{kai0:app:data}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Data scale and diversity.} Data were collected at 30\,Hz under a standard operating protocol (SOP) that ensures consistent execution quality and duration. The dataset comprises 2668 episodes for Task~A (mean duration 30.41\,s), 3519 for Task~B (34.40\,s) and 2988 for Task~C (39.01\,s); these means include shorter DAgger interventions. To increase diversity, the initial conditions of data collection are randomised, including the object state (position, crumpling configuration, size and colour) and environmental factors (illumination intensity and direction).
  \item \textbf{Training strategy and hyper-parameters.} We fine-tune all parameters of the open-source $\pi_{0.5}$ model with a flow-matching objective. Each task is fine-tuned independently. Table~\ref{kai0:tab:hyperparams} lists the hyper-parameters.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation criteria (average score).} A rule-based evaluation protocol assigns partial credit for the completion of sub-goals. Table~\ref{kai0:tab:score} lists the sub-goals and their scores.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hardware Configuration and Inference Details}\label{kai0:app:hardware}
As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:robot_setup}, the experimental setup includes two bimanual systems: one built from AgileX Robotics Piper arms and the other from ARX Robotics X5 arms. Both dual-arm platforms have two 6-DoF arms equipped with 1-DoF parallel grippers. A key kinematic difference lies in the configuration of the fifth joint, which is a pitch joint on the AgileX Piper arm and a yaw joint on the ARX X5. For perception, each system is equipped with three Intel RealSense D435i cameras (one fixed head view and two wrist views) that capture $640 \times 480$ RGB images. The visual sampling rate is synchronised at 30\,Hz for both data collection and inference, while the low-level joint controllers operate at a higher frequency of 100--200\,Hz. All inference is performed on an Ubuntu 20.04 workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

\section{Failure Case Analysis}\label{kai0:app:failure}
To answer Q6 (Section~\ref{kai0:app:questions}), we conducted a qualitative analysis that visualises and categorises all failure instances recorded during the evaluation on Task~A, pooled over all evaluated policies (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:failure_case}). The flattening stage is the bottleneck of Task~A: it accounts for 1010 of the 1026 categorised failure instances, most of them prolonged re-grasping (874) and redundant flattening (85). Because the instances are pooled, the figure does not show how the failures of $\pi_{0.5}$ and of \chiz differ, and its categories are not mapped to the two failure modes of Q6 \authorcheck{give the failure breakdown of Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:failure_case} separately for $\pi_{0.5}$ and \chiz, and map its categories to spatial misalignment and policy stagnation}. The concentration of failures in flattening is consistent with the large gains of DAgger and heuristic DAgger on this task, since standard DAgger intervenes after repeated failed grasps (Section~\ref{kai0:app:tda}), the kind of failure that dominates this phase.
