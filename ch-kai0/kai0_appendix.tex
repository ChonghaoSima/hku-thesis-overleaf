\section{Motivating Questions}\label{kai0:app:questions}

Below we address several recurring conceptual questions alongside broader open problems raised by our study.

\paragraph{Q1. How does Stage Advantage relate to reinforcement learning?}
The formulation of Stage Advantage builds upon advantage-weighted regression (AWR)~\cite{peng2019awr}, an offline reinforcement learning (RL)~\cite{sutton1998reinforcement} method that weights behavioural cloning updates using empirical returns from recorded trajectories. Because raw Monte Carlo returns suffer from high variance, recent advantage-weighted diffusion controllers~\cite{frans2025diffusion} and their large-scale extension $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$~\cite{amin2026pistar06} instead fit a parameterised value estimator to offline demonstrations. Developed by co-author Lirui Zhao for \chiz, Stage Advantage adopts this value-guided paradigm while regressing relative progress directly from frame pairs. Relative to our value-difference baseline modelled on $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$, Stage Advantage produces lower-variance advantage trajectories across all three manipulation benchmarks; policies trained under this signal achieved higher completion rates on Task~A within sampling variability, though no corresponding completion-rate gain appeared on Tasks~B and~C (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}).

\paragraph{Q2. Why favour offline advantage weighting over online RL such as PPO?}
On-policy algorithms such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017PPO} and diffusion-based extensions like DSRL~\cite{wagenmaker2025dsrl} belong to the online RL paradigm, where policy updates rely on continuous environment interaction. Deploying such methods on physical manipulators is severely constrained by sample complexity: unlike simulated environments, physical testbeds incur high operational costs whenever rollouts must be parallelised or deformable objects manually reset. By contrast, AWR~\cite{peng2019awr} requires only light annotation overhead to reweight the imitation objective toward high-progress transitions, allowing the learner to extract greater utility from both human teleoperation logs and offline policy rollouts.

\paragraph{Q3. Why was $\pi_{0.5}$ selected as the primary baseline?}
Before selecting a reference backbone, we ran pilot experiments on several prominent VLA architectures, specifically X-VLA~\cite{zheng2025xvla}, GO-1 (released with the AgiBot World benchmark~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros}, co-authored by the thesis author), DexVLA~\cite{wen2025dexvla}, UniVLA~\cite{bu2025univla}, and OpenVLA~\cite{kim2024openvla}. None of these candidates solved our deformable manipulation tasks reliably. Even after extensive fine-tuning under matched camera setups and tuned optimisation schedules with converged training losses, their completion rates over 30 evaluation rollouts (10 episodes per test garment) remained too low to serve as informative baselines. We therefore excluded them from the main tables so that our comparative evaluation focuses on backbones capable of completing the full manipulation sequence.

\paragraph{Q4. How should an informative advantage signal be formulated?}
Constructing a more principled advantage estimator remains an important direction for future work. Standard behaviour cloning assigns equal weight to every recorded timestep, even though many trajectory segments contribute little toward task completion. Fitting a policy to such unproductive intervals can trap optimisation in local optima where the robot repeats hesitant motions without advancing the task state. Weighting updates by estimated advantages counteracts this tendency by emphasising transitions with high empirical return. Still, our present implementation approximates progress through elapsed trajectory time, a heuristic that assumes strictly monotonic execution. Replacing this temporal proxy with an unsupervised progress estimator that separates decisive manipulation actions from idle motion without assuming linear time progression would strengthen the method.

\paragraph{Q5. What characterises high-utility robot demonstration data?}
Throughout our experiments, demonstration quality strongly governed downstream policy performance, with task completion rates varying from 20\% to 60\% when all other training factors were held constant. We note this range as an empirical observation from our workflow rather than a formal controlled study. We also noticed a masking phenomenon: certain algorithmic modifications yield clear improvements when trained on noisy demonstrations, yet their gains saturate or disappear once demonstration quality is high. Although data quality is central to modern imitation learning, prior literature rarely examines it systematically. To establish a practical standard, we highlight \emph{replay-ability} as a core criterion for trajectory validity. A recorded demonstration satisfies replay-ability if executing its action sequence open-loop from a comparable initial state completes the task or achieves substantial forward progress. Beyond screening out corrupted episodes, this test diagnoses systematic hardware mismatches between the data collection platform ($P_{\mathrm{train}}$) and the deployment setup ($P_{\mathrm{test}}$).

\paragraph{Q6. Which failure modes dominate during physical rollouts?}
During evaluation we encounter two main error patterns: (1)~\emph{spatial misalignment}, where the gripper misses the target grasp region on the cloth, and (2)~\emph{policy stagnation}, where the arms enter a repetitive loop of ineffective motions (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:failure_case}). These breakdowns point to two underlying limitations of current pre-trained backbones: imprecise spatial grounding and fragile multi-step planning. While Model Arithmetic is intended to reduce spatial errors and Stage Advantage targets stagnation, both act only as external post-training remedies, and our breakdown in Section~\ref{kai0:app:failure} does not isolate their separate effects on failure counts. Resolving both failure modes more completely will require pre-trained representations with stronger built-in spatial awareness and sequential reasoning.

\paragraph{Q7. What distinguishes robot foundation backbones during post-training?}
In line with the failure patterns above, robot foundation models differ less in zero-shot execution than in their post-training adaptability, specifically how readily their weights acquire spatial precision and multi-stage planning during fine-tuning. In our pilot screening (Q3), $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ adapted to bimanual garment manipulation far more effectively than the other candidates tested, indicating that their pre-trained representations offer a more favourable initialisation for downstream adaptation. Evaluation benchmarks should therefore measure the representational plasticity of foundation models under fine-tuning rather than reporting only success rates in simple tabletop scenes.

\section{Additional Related Work}\label{kai0:app:related}

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Robustness}\label{kai0:app:related_rl}
Post-training VLA foundation models with reinforcement learning has become a common strategy for improving manipulation precision and robustness, both in simulated benchmarks~\cite{liu2023libero,mu2025robotwin,mees2022calvin,lu2025vla,li2025simplevla,chen2025tgrpo} and on physical robots~\cite{luo2025hilserl,xiao2025PLD,luo2024serl}. A central difficulty is updating high-capacity pre-trained networks without corrupting their learned representations. Advantage-conditioned formulations~\cite{amin2026pistar06,frans2025diffusion,kumar2019reward} update the full backbone by conditioning action generation on estimated advantages, bypassing backpropagation through iterative diffusion or flow-matching solvers~\cite{lei2025rl}. How well these methods perform depends directly on the fidelity of the advantage estimates. A widespread pipeline prompts vision-language models to score per-frame completion~\cite{ma2024gvl,zhang2025rewind,zhai2025vlac,ghasemipour2025self}, computes state-value differences $A(s,a) = V(s') - V(s)$~\cite{amin2026pistar06}, and plugs the resulting weights into AWR~\cite{wu2023elastic,kuba2023advantage}. Subtracting two separate value estimates, however, accumulates prediction noise from both states. Furthermore, a single global value estimator becomes ambiguous whenever visually similar cloth configurations recur across different phases of a composite task~\cite{amin2026pistar06,li2025gr}. Stage Advantage, developed by co-author Lirui Zhao for \chiz, targets both issues: rather than subtracting two separate state values, it regresses the transition advantage jointly from the observation pair via $f_{\theta}(s, s')$ so correlated errors cancel partially (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:sa}), yielding a smoother temporal trace (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_sa}), while conditioning on the active stage sub-goal $g$ disambiguates visually similar states across task phases.

\subsection{Control Methods}\label{kai0:app:related_control}
While asynchronous inference lowers control latency, prior implementations introduce practical trade-offs. SmolVLA~\cite{shukor2025smolvla} switches directly between consecutive action chunks, creating sharp boundary discontinuities between predicted and executed trajectories. Concurrent work in A2C2~\cite{sendai2025leave} reduces this mismatch by introducing auxiliary correction heads, which requires modifying the network architecture. By contrast, \chiz pairs asynchronous execution with temporal chunk-wise smoothing, linearly blending overlapping action buffers (Algorithm~\ref{kai0:alg:chunk_smoothing}) without altering the policy network.

\section{Method Details}\label{kai0:app:method}

\subsection{Origin of the Name}\label{kai0:app:name}
Our framework is built around the concept of \textbf{K}inetics \textbf{A}ligned to \textbf{I}ntelligence. Here, ``Kinetics'' refers to the physical execution domain spanning data collection ($P_{\mathrm{train}}$) and deployment ($P_{\mathrm{test}}$), whereas ``Intelligence'' refers to the learned policy parameterised by the network weights ($Q_{\mathrm{model}}$). We denote this first system generation as KAI\textsubscript{0}. Because ``KAI'' shares its pronunciation with the Greek letter $\chi$, we write the model name as \chiz, acknowledging the $\pi$ family of foundation policies.

\subsection{Model Arithmetic}\label{kai0:app:ma}
Under Model Arithmetic, we merge 4 policy checkpoints obtained from distinct partitions of the demonstration pool. In our default weighting scheme, checkpoints are combined via inverse-loss weighting: we evaluate the mean prediction loss of each candidate on a held-out validation set and assign proportionally larger weights to checkpoints with lower validation error. While the Task~C ablation (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:exp_ma}) identified greedy search as the highest-scoring merge rule on that benchmark, the full system evaluated in Table~\ref{kai0:tab:taskA} uses the in-domain inverse-loss weighted merged model. For comparison, our ablations also benchmark a \emph{single-best} policy, corresponding to the single checkpoint with minimum validation loss, alongside a \emph{full-data} policy trained jointly on the union of all data subsets.

\subsection{Stage Advantage}\label{kai0:app:sa}
To train the Stage Advantage estimator (a co-author's module), we sample pairs of video frames at arbitrary timestamps within the same demonstration episode. The regression target is the relative progress between the two frames, calculated as the difference between their normalised episode timestamps. During \chiz policy training, the indicator threshold $\epsilon$ in Eq.~\eqref{kai0:eq:indicator} is determined via quantile thresholding following $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$: all transitions are ordered by predicted advantage, the top proportion $q = 0.3$ receives a positive label, and the remaining transitions receive a negative label, placing $\epsilon$ at the 70th percentile. Semantic task stages are defined per benchmark: two stages on Task~A (flattening and folding), four on Task~B (retrieving, flattening, folding, and handover), and three on Task~C (retrieving, dressing the rack, and hanging).

\subsection{Train--Deploy Alignment}\label{kai0:app:tda}
Collecting standard DAgger trajectories proceeds in three phases:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We reset the workspace and launch autonomous policy rollouts.
  \item Whenever the policy enters repeated failure, defined as missing a garment grasp over more than 10 consecutive attempts or repeating unproductive motion loops for over 10 steps, execution is halted. At that moment, the software pipeline ceases observation updates and action generation, while the hardware controller holds the current joint configuration and transfers control to human teleoperation.
  \item Both the teleoperated recovery trajectory and the preceding autonomous rollout are logged to the dataset.
\end{enumerate}
Heuristic DAgger dispenses with live policy rollouts during data collection. Instead, an operator manually arranges the cloth and robot arms into representative failure configurations resembling those where standard DAgger halts execution, such as an off-centre grasp or a partially slipped garment, and records an expert recovery demonstration directly from that state without running a policy (Section~\ref{kai0:sec:tda} and Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:consistency}).

\section{Experimental Details}\label{kai0:app:exp}

\subsection{Data, Training Strategy and Evaluation Criteria}\label{kai0:app:data}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dataset scale and variation.} Demonstrations were recorded at 30\,Hz following a standard operating protocol (SOP) designed to maintain uniform motion quality and episode length. The resulting corpus contains 2668 trajectories for Task~A (mean duration 30.41\,s), 3519 for Task~B (34.40\,s), and 2988 for Task~C (39.01\,s), where the reported durations include shorter DAgger recovery clips. Scene diversity was promoted by varying initial garment states (placement, fold configuration, dimensions, and colour) as well as ambient lighting intensity and angle.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tuning setup and hyper-parameters.} All parameters of the pre-trained open-source $\pi_{0.5}$ backbone are updated end-to-end under a flow-matching loss, with separate models trained for each task. Optimisation settings appear in Table~\ref{kai0:tab:hyperparams}.
  \item \textbf{Scoring rubric (average score).} Partial task completion is quantified through a milestone-based grading rubric, with sub-goal weights detailed in Table~\ref{kai0:tab:score}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hardware Configuration and Inference Details}\label{kai0:app:hardware}
Our physical testbeds comprise two bimanual manipulation stations (Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:robot_setup}), one outfitted with AgileX Robotics Piper manipulators and the other with ARX Robotics X5 manipulators. Each station pairs two 6-DoF arms with 1-DoF parallel-jaw grippers. The main kinematic distinction between the two arm designs lies at the fifth joint, which rotates in pitch on the AgileX Piper and in yaw on the ARX X5. Visual observations are captured by three Intel RealSense D435i RGB cameras per station (one overhead third-person camera and two wrist-mounted cameras) streaming $640 \times 480$ frames at a synchronised rate of 30\,Hz during both teleoperation and policy deployment. Low-level joint impedance controllers track commands at 100--200\,Hz, while policy inference runs on an Ubuntu 20.04 workstation housing a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

Training loss trajectories for Stage Advantage and the $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$-style value-difference baseline are plotted in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:loss_curve_sa}. Under advantage-weighted behaviour cloning, Stage Advantage converges to a lower terminal loss than the value-difference formulation, a trend compatible with a less noisy supervision target, though lower training error alone does not prove higher advantage accuracy.

\subsection{Model Arithmetic on Tasks A and B}\label{kai0:app:ablation_ma}
Results for Model Arithmetic across Tasks~A and~B (30 evaluation rollouts per variant) appear in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_soup}. Weight-merged checkpoints surpass both the single-best checkpoint and the full-data policy across most metrics, though not uniformly on Task~A. Specifically, on Task~A uniform averaging records a slightly lower completion rate (around 80\%) than either baseline (around 83\%), and several merged variants exhibit lower throughput than the full-data policy, with all Task~A completion-rate gaps falling within roughly three rollouts out of 30. On Task~B, by contrast, every merged checkpoint improves upon both baselines in completion rate (roughly 70\% to 90\% versus 47\% and 60\%), throughput, and retry count. Ranking checkpoints by held-out validation loss proves less decisive here than on Task~C, a difference we trace to uneven OOD validation coverage: validation loss serves as an informative selection criterion only when the held-out split closely reflects the deployment distribution $P_{\mathrm{test}}$.

In Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_soup_b}, two plotted average-score bars, namely those for the full-data baseline (around 16) and for in-domain inverse-loss merging (around 6), lie below their corresponding completion rates (around 47\% and 76\%). Because any completed rollout receives 100 points under the milestone rubric in Table~\ref{kai0:tab:score}, those two plotted bar heights reflect a plotting discrepancy in the recorded figure, and our comparison on Task~B relies on the reported completion rates, throughput, and retry counts instead.

\subsection{Stage Advantage on Task C}\label{kai0:app:ablation_sa}
Ablation outcomes for Stage Advantage on Task~C are shown in Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:app_sa_c}. Although Stage Advantage again attains smoother advantage estimates than the value-difference baseline (MSTD around 0.77 versus 1.0, and SFR around 76\% versus 73\%), downstream execution metrics move in the opposite direction: completion rate (around 3\% versus 10\%, corresponding to 1 versus 3 successes in 30 rollouts, within sampling noise), throughput, and average score all decline, with only retry cost improving. We attribute this gap to the pre-training corpus of $\pi_{0.5}$, which lacks prior exposure to garment-hanging kinematics and thus limits high-level planning regardless of advantage smoothness. More broadly, this outcome shows that reducing variance in the advantage estimator does not automatically yield stronger closed-loop task execution.

\section{Failure Case Analysis}\label{kai0:app:failure}
Complementing the discussion in Q6 (Section~\ref{kai0:app:questions}), Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:failure_case} catalogues the 1026 failure instances logged during Task~A evaluations, pooled across all tested policies. Garment flattening represents the primary operational bottleneck on Task~A, accounting for 1010 of the 1026 recorded failures, predominantly through prolonged re-grasping (874 instances) and redundant flattening motions (85 instances). Because Fig.~\ref{kai0:fig:failure_case} aggregates these 1026 instances across all evaluated policies rather than separating counts for $\pi_{0.5}$ and \chiz, and organises outcomes by task stage rather than directly by the spatial misalignment and policy stagnation taxonomy of Q6, it provides an overall task-level profile rather than a per-model breakdown. Even so, the heavy concentration of errors during flattening explains why both standard DAgger and heuristic DAgger yield large gains on Task~A: standard DAgger triggers human intervention after repeated missed grasps or stalled loops (Section~\ref{kai0:app:tda}), targeting the exact failure patterns that dominate the flattening phase.
