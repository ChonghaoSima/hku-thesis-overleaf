\begin{abstract}

Physical agents such as autonomous vehicles and robot manipulators are increasingly built by learning from data, yet they are deployed in an open world that keeps presenting conditions their training data do not contain. This thesis calls this challenge the \emph{curse of reality} and studies how to make physical agents robust to it along the perceive--reason--act loop.

For perception, the thesis proposes to describe driving scenes by 3D semantic occupancy instead of bounding boxes. It introduces OccNet, a camera-based network that reconstructs occupancy through a cascade of voxel decoders, and OpenOcc, a dense occupancy benchmark, and shows that occupancy describes irregularly shaped objects better than boxes, transfers to detection and segmentation, and lowers the collision rate of a planner in open-loop evaluation. It further scales occupancy supervision with OpenScene, a large redistribution of real-world driving data used in community challenges on end-to-end driving and world models.

For reasoning, the thesis introduces Graph Visual Question Answering, which links perception, prediction and planning questions through logical dependencies, together with the DriveLM datasets and a vision-language baseline. The baseline transfers zero-shot to a new sensor configuration better than a driving-specific model, mostly owing to its pre-trained backbone; graph-structured reasoning adds further gains under such shift, and on unseen objects when the graph includes a question about them, but brings little benefit in familiar situations.

For acting, the thesis proposes Centaur, which lets an end-to-end driving planner correct itself at test time by minimising Cluster Entropy, a label-free measure of the planner's uncertainty. In non-reactive simulation on the NAVSIM benchmark, Centaur raises the driving score of a state-of-the-art planner from 90.3 to 92.6 in a single run, reaching 92.1 $\pm$ 0.3 over three seeds, without hand-designed rules; a new safety-critical split exposes the remaining gap to human driving. For robot manipulation, the thesis presents $\chi_0$, which traces the fragility of real-world policies to inconsistencies between demonstrations, model and execution, and addresses them with Model Arithmetic, Stage Advantage and Train--Deploy Alignment. Post-training a pre-trained generalist policy with about 20 hours of demonstrations per task and eight GPUs, $\chi_0$ raises its success rate on a real-robot T-shirt folding task from about 27\% to about 97\%.

Drawing on these contributions and on the author's co-authored work, the thesis distils a reflective, benchmark-driven research framework. It decomposes the curse of reality into perceptual, semantic, behavioural and deployment shift, and describes a cycle that turns failures observed in the field into public benchmarks, simple and scalable baselines, community challenges and self-correction at deployment. Synthesising the findings, the thesis proposes that a self-correcting physical agent would need an open and queryable representation, fast and slow decision paths, a monitor of its own competence, adaptation at deployment, and a learning loop that feeds residual failures back into data and benchmarks.
