\begin{abstract}

Physical agents such as autonomous vehicles and robot manipulators are increasingly built by learning from data, yet they are deployed in an open world that keeps presenting conditions their training data do not contain. This thesis calls this challenge the \emph{curse of reality} and studies how to make physical agents robust to it along the perceive--reason--act loop.

For perception, the thesis proposes to describe driving scenes by 3D semantic occupancy instead of bounding boxes. It introduces OccNet, a camera-based network that reconstructs occupancy through a cascade of voxel decoders, and OpenOcc, a dense occupancy benchmark, and shows that occupancy describes irregularly shaped objects better than boxes, transfers to detection and segmentation, and lowers the collision rate of a planner in open-loop evaluation. It further scales occupancy supervision with OpenScene, a large redistribution of real-world driving data used in community challenges on end-to-end driving and world models.

For reasoning, the thesis introduces Graph Visual Question Answering, which links perception, prediction and planning questions, together with the DriveLM datasets and a vision-language baseline. Most of the displayed transfer advantage over a driving-specific model comes from the pre-trained backbone. In two selected shift tests, graph-context conditions have higher single-run scores on some measures, while graph context does not improve the evaluated in-domain planning result; the experiments do not isolate graph topology from question content or co-training.

For acting, the thesis proposes Centaur, which updates an end-to-end driving planner at test time by minimising Cluster Entropy, a direction-cluster score-mass objective whose test-time use requires no new labels or explicit scene-dependent safety cost. In non-reactive NAVSIM, Centaur raises the reported planner score from 90.3 to 92.6 in a single run and reaches 92.1 $\pm$ 0.3 over three seeds; the fixed-bank fallback and safety-stress-set aggregates require source-record verification. For robot manipulation, the thesis presents $\chi_0$, which studies mismatches among demonstrations, model training and execution through Model Arithmetic, Stage Advantage and Train--Deploy Alignment. Source figures show higher success and throughput on a real-robot T-shirt task after post-training a pre-trained policy; exact success counts and total resource use are unavailable.

Drawing on these contributions and on the author's co-authored work, the thesis distils a reflective, benchmark-driven research framework. It organises the curse of reality into four non-exclusive diagnostic categories: perceptual, semantic, behavioural and deployment shift, and describes a cycle that turns failures observed in the field into public benchmarks, simple and scalable baselines, community challenges and self-correction at deployment. As a research agenda rather than an evaluated integrated system, the thesis proposes an open and queryable representation, fast and slow decision paths, a monitor of its own competence, adaptation at deployment, and a learning loop that feeds residual failures back into data and benchmarks.
