\begin{abstract}

Physical agents such as autonomous vehicles and robot manipulators are increasingly built by learning from data, yet they are deployed in an open world that keeps presenting conditions their training data do not contain. This thesis calls this challenge the \emph{curse of reality} and studies how to make physical agents robust to it along the perceive--reason--act loop.

For perception, the thesis describes driving scenes by 3D semantic occupancy instead of bounding boxes. It introduces OccNet, a camera-based network with a cascade of voxel decoders, and OpenOcc, a dense occupancy benchmark. Occupancy describes irregularly shaped objects of known classes better than boxes and transfers to detection and segmentation. In open-loop evaluation on nuScenes, planning on predicted occupancy gave slightly lower collision rates than planning on boxes predicted by the same network (0.06--0.24 percentage points, single runs; not statistically established). Occupancy opens geometry but not semantics, and its robustness to distribution shift was not tested. OpenScene scales occupancy supervision to over 120 hours of driving; its data later supported challenges on end-to-end driving and world models.

For reasoning, the thesis introduces Graph Visual Question Answering, which links perception, prediction and planning questions through logical dependencies, together with the DriveLM datasets and a vision-language baseline. Transferred zero-shot to a new sensor configuration, the baseline had a lower trajectory error than a driving-specific model, mostly owing to its pre-trained backbone, although the models differ in camera input and size. Graph context improved speed decisions under this shift but degraded open-loop planning and behaviour accuracy in-domain; on an unseen object class it helped only through a question written with knowledge of that class.

For acting, the thesis proposes Centaur, which lets an end-to-end driving planner adapt at test time by minimising Cluster Entropy, a label-free measure of its uncertainty, without test-time rules (the planner distils rule-based scores during training). On logged frames in the non-reactive simulation of NAVSIM, where errors cannot compound, Centaur raised the driving score of a state-of-the-art planner from 90.3 to 92.6 in a single run (NAVSIM v1.0) and reached 92.1 $\pm$ 0.3 over three seeds on v1.1, without a matched three-seed baseline; a safety-critical split exposes the remaining gap to human driving. For manipulation, $\chi_0$, a co-led project, addresses inconsistencies between demonstrations, model and execution. Post-training a generalist policy with about 20 hours of demonstrations per task, it raised the success rate on a real-robot T-shirt folding task from about 27\% to about 97\% over 30 trials. That inconsistency rather than scale limits robustness remains a hypothesis: scale was not varied at a matched budget, and targeted recovery data gave the largest single gain.

As a reflective synthesis, the thesis reconstructs the benchmark-driven research cycle behind this work, decomposes the curse of reality into perceptual, semantic, behavioural and deployment shift, and records which shift each chapter tests. It closes with a research agenda for self-correcting physical agents: an open, queryable representation, fast and slow decision paths, a competence monitor, adaptation at deployment, and a learning loop that feeds residual failures back into data and benchmarks. Only adaptation at deployment is demonstrated, and only on logged frames in non-reactive simulation.
