\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

A vehicle drives through a city it has never visited and meets a truck carrying an oddly shaped load that overhangs its bed. A robot that has learned to fold shirts from hours of human demonstrations is handed a garment crumpled in a way that no demonstrator ever produced. Neither situation is exotic; each happens every day somewhere. Yet each is, for the agent concerned, a condition that its training data did not contain. This thesis is about such conditions, and about how physical agents can be built to cope with them.

\section{Physical Agents and the Curse of Reality}\label{sec:intro:curse}

A \emph{physical agent} perceives the world through sensors, reasons about what it perceives, and acts on the world through actuators, in a closed loop. Autonomous vehicles and robot manipulators are the two kinds of physical agents studied in this thesis. Over the past decade, both have increasingly been built by learning from data: perception models are trained on annotated sensor recordings, planners and control policies are trained to imitate human demonstrations, and, most recently, foundation models pre-trained on internet-scale data have been adapted to driving and manipulation.

Learning from data brings a fundamental difficulty with it. A learned model is reliable only on data that resemble its training data, whereas a physical agent is deployed in an open world. Rare events are individually improbable, but so numerous that, taken together, they occur all the time; for autonomous vehicles this has been called the \emph{curse of rarity}~\cite{liu2024curse}. In addition, a physical agent acts in closed loop: its own actions determine the situations it encounters next, so a policy that has only seen an expert's behaviour drifts into states the expert never visited, and its errors compound~\cite{ross2011dagger}. This thesis refers to the combination of these difficulties as the \emph{curse of reality}: whatever data an agent is trained on, the real world will keep presenting conditions the agent has never seen. The term is an organising label rather than a new theoretical concept. It gathers distribution shift, the long tail of rare events and the open-world character of deployment under one heading, so that remedies at different stages of an agent can be compared; Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} makes it more precise by decomposing it into four kinds of shift.

The curse of reality is more severe for physical agents than for most other applications of machine learning, for four reasons. Their mistakes have physical consequences, so failures on rare inputs cannot be dismissed as statistically negligible. Their data are expensive, because every recording requires a vehicle or a robot and every annotation of 3D structure or of a manipulation trajectory requires skilled effort. Their loop is closed, so small errors do not stay small. And their deployment platforms impose hard limits on latency, memory and computation, so that robustness cannot be bought simply by running larger models.

\section{From Modular Pipelines to End-to-End Physical Agents}\label{sec:intro:context}

The research in this thesis was carried out between 2021 and 2026, a period in which the design of physical agents changed substantially. Autonomous driving systems were traditionally built as modular pipelines, in which separately trained perception, prediction and planning modules communicate through hand-designed interfaces such as lists of 3D bounding boxes. Two developments challenged this design. The first was the rise of unified scene representations learned directly from multiple cameras, such as the bird's-eye-view (BEV) representation of BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer}, which made it possible to train one network for many perception tasks and to share features across them~\cite{li2023bevdevils}. The second was end-to-end planning: UniAD showed that when all modules are trained jointly towards the final planning objective, planning error and collisions drop substantially~\cite{hu2023uniad}.

A third development followed: foundation models. Vision-language models (VLMs) trained on web-scale data carry broad knowledge about the world and can express their reasoning in language, which raised the prospect of drivers that can explain their decisions and generalise to unfamiliar situations~\cite{sima2024drivelm}. In robotics, vision-language-action models and large, openly released robot datasets promised generalist manipulation policies~\cite{bu2025agibot_iros}. Each of these developments helps against the curse of reality in some respects and introduces new risks in others: unified representations must still describe objects they were not designed for, end-to-end planners are hard to guard with rules, language outputs can look competent without being grounded~\cite{xie2025vlms}, and large policies trained on large data still fail when training and deployment conditions diverge~\cite{sima2026kai0}. This thesis studies these risks and proposes ways to address them.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:intro:rq}

The central question of this thesis is: \emph{how can physical agents be made robust to the conditions that the real world presents but their training data do not contain?} Following the perceive--reason--act loop, it is divided into four research questions.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm, labelwidth=1cm, itemsep=4pt]
  \item[RQ1] \textbf{Perceive.} How should a physical agent represent its 3D surroundings so that the representation remains valid for the open set of objects and structures met in the real world, while remaining learnable from cameras and useful for planning?
  \item[RQ2] \textbf{Reason.} How can the general knowledge of vision-language models be grounded in driving in a structured and verifiable way that mirrors how humans reason from perception through prediction to planning, and does such reasoning help a driving agent generalise to unseen situations?
  \item[RQ3] \textbf{Act.} How can a learned policy remain reliable when the deployment distribution departs from its training distribution: (a) for end-to-end driving, can a planner correct its own per-frame decisions at test time, without test-time rules, cost functions or additional labels; and (b) for real-world robot manipulation, under inconsistent demonstrations and a limited budget of data and computation?
  \item[RQ4] \textbf{Architecture.} What design lessons for self-correcting physical agents, in which perception, reasoning and action can correct one another after deployment, follow from the answers to RQ1--RQ3?
\end{description}

RQ1 to RQ3 are each answered by one or two core chapters. Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} studies RQ3(a) on logged driving frames in non-reactive simulation, where the planner's errors cannot compound, so it answers the question for per-frame decisions and leaves closed-loop behaviour open. RQ4 is a synthesis question: Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} answers it with a set of design lessons drawn from RQ1--RQ3 and from related work, and grades each lesson by the strength of the evidence behind it. No integrated self-correcting system is built or evaluated in this thesis.

\section{Approach}\label{sec:intro:approach}

The research can be described by a benchmark-driven framework, which Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} reconstructs from the author's projects. A project begins with a failure observed, or anticipated, in the real world. The failure is formalised as a task with a representation and a metric, turned into a public benchmark, and addressed with a simple and scalable baseline method; the benchmark is then opened to the community, often through a public challenge. The residual failures found in this way start the next iteration. Each method was evaluated at one level of fidelity, which is stated with each claim: open-loop evaluation on logged data for perception and reasoning (Chapters~\ref{ch:occnet} and~\ref{ch:drivelm}), non-reactive simulation on logged driving frames for the driving policy (Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}), and real robots for manipulation (Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}). Higher-fidelity evaluation was feasible for Chapters~\ref{ch:drivelm} and~\ref{ch:centaur} but was not attempted (Section~\ref{sec:method:evaluation}).

The two ingredients of the curse of reality are not tested to the same extent. Conditions absent from training are tested mainly in Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}, with a new sensor configuration and an unseen object class; compounding errors in closed loop arise only in the real-robot experiments of Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}. Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} tests no distribution shift, and Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} evaluates on logged frames, where errors cannot compound. Table~\ref{tab:method:tests} in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} records what each core chapter tests. Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:overview} shows how the core chapters of the thesis map onto the perceive--reason--act loop and the research questions.

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:intro:contributions}

The contributions of this thesis are the following. C1--C4 are tested in the core chapters; C5 is a reflective synthesis and research agenda, not a contribution on a par with them.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Occupancy as a geometry-open scene representation (Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}).} We propose to describe driving scenes by 3D semantic occupancy instead of 3D boxes, together with OccNet, a camera-based network that reconstructs occupancy through a cascade of voxel decoders, and OpenOcc, a dense occupancy benchmark with 34,149 frames, more than 1.4 billion annotated voxels and flow for foreground objects. Occupancy is open in geometry, since it can express the shape of whatever occupies space, but its semantics remain a closed set of 16 classes. On OpenOcc, OccNet reaches 26.98 mIoU in semantic scene completion, against 23.67 for TPVFormer retrained on the same benchmark; occupancy describes irregularly shaped objects of known classes better than voxelised boxes, and it serves as a pre-training target for detection when fine-tuning on small subsets of the training data. In open-loop evaluation on nuScenes, planning on predicted occupancy gave slightly lower collision rates than planning on boxes predicted by OccNet's detection head (0.06--0.24 percentage points, single runs; not statistically established; experiments led by co-authors). Robustness to distribution shift was not tested. OccNet builds on the BEV encoder of BEVFormer (co-authored by the author), and its 3D deformable attention extends 2D deformable attention to voxels, close in spirit to the deformable voxel queries of the concurrent VoxFormer~\cite{li2023voxformer}; what is new is the cascade voxel decoder with voxel-based temporal attention, the annotation pipeline of OpenOcc, and, at the time of publication, the use of one occupancy descriptor across perception and planning tasks. We also describe OpenScene, a data release led by the author that redistributes more than 120 hours of nuPlan driving with occupancy labels; its data, rather than its occupancy labels, later supported the end-to-end driving and predictive world-model tracks of the CVPR 2024 Autonomous Grand Challenge and the NAVSIM track in 2025.
  \item \textbf{Structured reasoning with vision-language models (Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}).} We introduce Graph Visual Question Answering, in which perception, prediction, planning, behaviour and motion questions are linked by logical dependencies, together with the DriveLM-nuScenes and DriveLM-CARLA datasets (4,871 frames with 91.4 question--answer pairs per frame, and about 1.6 million question--answer pairs, respectively) and the DriveLM-Agent baseline, which combines BLIP-2 with the trajectory tokenisation of RT-2; the contribution lies in the task, the data and the metrics rather than in the model. In zero-shot transfer from nuScenes to the Waymo sensor configuration, the baseline had a lower trajectory error than a driving-specific model (ADE 2.63 against 4.16), mostly owing to its pre-trained vision-language backbone. This comparison is confounded by input asymmetry (the driving-specific UniAD-Single loses its rear camera, whereas DriveLM-Agent uses the front camera only) and by model scale (3.9B against 132M parameters). Graph context improved speed decisions under this shift (speed accuracy 43.90 to 54.29), but in-domain it degraded open-loop planning and behaviour accuracy (ADE 1.39 to 1.74, collision rate 1.67 to 1.89, behaviour accuracy 61.45 to 57.49). On pedestrians unseen in CARLA training, the gain relied on an added question written with knowledge of the missing class, an injected human prior (experiment led by co-authors). All of these are single runs. The benchmark underpinned the ``Driving with Language'' track of the CVPR 2024 Autonomous Grand Challenge, with 152 participating teams.
  \item \textbf{Test-time adaptation for end-to-end driving (Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}).} We propose Centaur, which adapts a trajectory-scoring planner by test-time training on Cluster Entropy, a new label-free measure of the planner's uncertainty over five driving directions. It uses no rules at test time, although the planner distils rule-based scores during training. Evaluated on logged frames in the non-reactive simulation of NAVSIM v1, where the planner's errors cannot compound, Centaur raised the driving score of Hydra-MDP, a planner developed by other authors, several of whom co-authored Centaur, from 90.3 to 92.6 in a single run on NAVSIM v1.0. Its three-seed mean of 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 on NAVSIM v1.1 ranked first among three-seed entries on the \texttt{navtest} leaderboard at the time of submission (March 2025), but no matched three-seed baseline without test-time training was run. Every test-time training variant improved its base planner by 0.4 to 2.3 PDMS (single runs), whereas the uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories that we implemented lowered the score of every planner to which it was applied; the margin over a semantic-entropy variant of the same method is small (0.23 PDMS over three seeds). We also introduce \texttt{navsafe}, 229 safety-critical frames of \texttt{navtest} in ten categories; they were selected with the help of the base planner's scores and are not held out, and they expose the remaining gap to human driving. Behavioural shift and drift under repeated updates in closed loop are not tested.
  \item \textbf{Robust manipulation under distributional inconsistency (Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}).} In a co-led project, we analyse the failures of real-world manipulation policies as inconsistencies between the distributions of the demonstrations, of the learned policy, and of what is executed on hardware, and propose \chiz, which addresses them with Model Arithmetic, Stage Advantage and Train--Deploy Alignment. The author contributed to Model Arithmetic and Train--Deploy Alignment; Stage Advantage is a co-author's component (see the statement of contribution in Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0}). Post-training the pre-trained $\pi_{0.5}$ policy with about 20 hours of data per task and eight A100 GPUs, \chiz raised the success rate on a real-robot T-shirt flattening-and-folding task from about 27\% to about 97\% over 30 trials, and throughput roughly tenfold (approximate values read from a bar chart). That inconsistency rather than scale is the main obstacle to robustness is a hypothesis supported by this integrated result on one task: scale was not varied at a matched budget, and the largest single gain came from targeted recovery (DAgger) data.
  \item \textbf{A reflective synthesis and research agenda (Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology} and~\ref{ch:discussion}).} Drawing on the twenty publications to which the author contributed, including co-authored ones, Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} reconstructs in hindsight the benchmark-driven framework behind the research: a vocabulary of four kinds of shift, a research cycle, five design principles and a ladder of evaluation fidelity, together with a record of what each core chapter actually tests. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} synthesises the findings into a research agenda for self-correcting agents whose elements are graded by the strength of their evidence. Neither the framework nor its decision procedure has been tested on projects other than those from which it was derived.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}\label{sec:intro:outline}

The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:background} reviews the literature on distribution shift for physical agents, 3D scene representations, end-to-end driving, language models in driving, adaptation methods, robot manipulation learning, and benchmarks, and identifies the gaps that motivate the research questions. Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} presents the research methodology. Chapters~\ref{ch:occnet} to~\ref{ch:kai0} present the core contributions along the perceive--reason--act loop: occupancy perception, graph-structured reasoning, test-time training for driving, and robust manipulation. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets the findings across chapters, answers the research questions, and discusses limitations. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes the thesis and outlines directions for future work. The appendices contain supplementary material for each core chapter.
