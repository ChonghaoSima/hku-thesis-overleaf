\chapter{Act I: Robust End-to-End Driving via Test-Time Training}\label{ch:centaur}
\begin{contribution}
The material in this chapter adapts the preprint ``Centaur: Robust End-to-End Autonomous Driving with Test-Time Training''~\cite{sima2024centaur}, where the thesis author served as primary contact and co-first author alongside Kashyap Chitta. Conducted during an internship at NVIDIA Autonomous Vehicle Applied Research, the author's specific contributions comprised formulating test-time training for end-to-end trajectory planning, devising the Cluster Entropy criterion, integrating Centaur into Hydra-MDP, running the NAVSIM benchmarks, curating and manually verifying the \texttt{navsafe} evaluation suite, and performing the failure-detection, ablation, and qualitative studies. Co-authors led the manuscript drafting.
\end{contribution}

While Chapters~\ref{ch:occnet} and~\ref{ch:drivelm} examined the perception and reasoning components of the perceive--reason--act loop by constructing geometry-open 3D occupancy representations and grounding vision-language models in driving decisions, action remains the ultimate test of an embodied agent. Because an end-to-end planner outputs trajectories that directly govern vehicle motion on public roads, any planning mistake risks a real-world collision. At this action stage, the curse of reality becomes hardest to ignore: no matter how large the training corpus grows, an autonomous vehicle in deployment inevitably encounters rare, safety-critical situations absent from its training logs, whether an unfamiliar roundabout geometry, an ambiguous yellow signal requiring a split-second stop-or-go decision, or a sudden pedestrian crossing. In the autonomous driving literature, this long-tail exposure is termed the curse of rarity~\cite{liu2024curse}.

Because of these tail risks, production systems seldom rely on a neural planner alone. Instead, practitioners either wrap the policy in a hand-crafted \emph{fallback layer} that checks candidate plans for rule breaches and substitutes a pre-programmed manoeuvre, or they refine the output trajectory at deployment time by minimising a manually designed \emph{cost function} over auxiliary perceptual outputs such as drivable-area maps and obstacle bounding boxes. Both mechanisms require substantial human engineering, depend on expensive intermediate annotations, cannot improve automatically as more driving logs accumulate, and often induce overly cautious behaviour. Rather than surveying rule-based safety wrappers exhaustively, our experiments compare test-time weight adaptation against two specific baselines: an uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories implemented for this study, and the post-hoc cost optimisation of UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad}. These limitations motivate the first half of our third research question (RQ3a): \emph{how can an end-to-end driving policy maintain reliability under distributional shift at deployment without relying on hand-crafted rules, explicit cost functions, or ground-truth labels?} In practical terms, can a neural planner detect its own predictive uncertainty in an unfamiliar scene and adjust its weights on the fly without supervision? Here we address a bounded version of RQ3a: our evaluation measures per-frame decisions initialized from logged states where planning errors do not compound across time (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:setup}), and while no rules are invoked during deployment, the base planner itself is trained via knowledge distillation from rule-based metrics (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:prelim}).

To address this question, we propose \emph{Centaur} (\textbf{C}luster \textbf{En}tropy for \textbf{T}est-time tr\textbf{A}ining using \textbf{U}nce\textbf{r}tainty), a framework that updates a pre-trained planner via test-time training (TTT) rather than overriding its outputs. Before predicting a plan at each step, Centaur quantifies the dispersion of the planner's trajectory scores and takes a single gradient descent step to lower that dispersion, averaging gradients over a short ring buffer of recent frames. Because those gradients depend exclusively on past observations, backpropagation could in principle execute asynchronously on parallel hardware; in our current implementation, however, updates run sequentially and are counted in the reported latency. Three main contributions anchor this chapter. First, at the time of its release in March 2025, Centaur represented, to our knowledge, the first use of test-time training for an end-to-end planner on NAVSIM, contrasting with prior TTT methods for motion forecasting~\cite{park2024t4p} and test-time trajectory search methods that leave network weights fixed~\cite{yang2024diffusiones}. Second, we formulate \emph{Cluster Entropy}, an interpretable uncertainty objective for score-based planners that partitions candidate trajectories into five lateral steering directions and computes the Shannon entropy across those five directional clusters. Third, we curate \texttt{navsafe}, an internal 229-frame diagnostic subset of \texttt{navtest} spanning ten safety-critical categories informed by the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) pre-crash typology~\cite{najm2007pre} and selected after ranking \texttt{navtest} clips by the baseline score of Hydra-MDP. Whereas the NAVSIM evaluation suite~\cite{dauner2024navsim} and the Hydra-MDP architecture~\cite{li2024hydramdp} come from prior work, the TTT update rule, Cluster Entropy, and \texttt{navsafe} originate in this chapter.

Every experiment reported here uses non-reactive log replay in NAVSIM v1~\cite{dauner2024navsim}: at each frame, the ego vehicle starts from the recorded human state, unrolls a 4-second plan against non-reactive background traffic, and resets to the next recorded human state at the following step. Because states never evolve under the planner's own past actions, closed-loop behavioural shift (as defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}) and long-horizon parameter drift remain untested. On the NAVSIM v1.0 \texttt{navtest} split (single seed), Centaur lifts the PDM score (PDMS) of Hydra-MDP from 90.3 to 92.6, compared with 94.8 for logged human demonstrations. Across three random seeds on the official NAVSIM v1.1 \texttt{navtest} leaderboard, Centaur attains 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 PDMS, placing first among three-seed submissions as of March 2025, though without an unadapted three-seed Hydra-MDP entry to isolate the exact multi-seed TTT margin. On the 229-frame \texttt{navsafe} subset, Centaur improves over Hydra-MDP both on the preprint's reported Overall column (74.14 \vs 56.47) and under the transparent frame-weighted (68.86 \vs 49.36; 68.9 \vs 49.4 rounded) and simple (71.96 \vs 48.33; 72.0 \vs 48.3 rounded) category means in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navsafe_overall} (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe_results}). Because \texttt{navsafe} clips were initially ordered by Hydra-MDP scores, regression to the mean favours any modified variant of Hydra-MDP; moreover, Centaur drops from 71.6 to 59.3 on yielding scenes and still trails human demonstrations (94.65) by a wide margin.

Methodologically, this chapter connects directly to Chapters~\ref{ch:occnet} and~\ref{ch:drivelm}: NAVSIM draws its sensor logs from OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene} and nuPlan~\cite{caesar2021nuplan}, while Cluster Entropy adopts the five lateral steering bins of DriveLM~\cite{sima2024drivelm} (omitting DriveLM's five longitudinal speed bins).

\section{Motivation}\label{centaur:sec:intro}

\begin{figure}[t]

  \label{centaur:fig:teaser}

As automated vehicles increasingly operate alongside human traffic on public roads, handling rare interactions and unfamiliar road geometries safely is essential~\cite{shalevshwartz2017rss,liu2024curse}. End-to-end neural planners have gained traction for this task~\cite{chitta2023transfuser,hu2023uniad,weng2024paradrive,li2024hydramdp} because they scale directly with driving logs and perform competitively across diverse urban scenes~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey,li2023open}. When deployed on actual vehicles, however, neural policies are routinely paired with rule-based fallback modules that override the network with pre-programmed manoeuvres whenever hazard conditions are triggered~\cite{chitta2023transfuser,vitelli2022safetynet,waywe2024ad2}. Designing such fallback logic demands extensive domain engineering, does not scale with additional training logs, and often caps the agility of the underlying learned policy.

A second safeguard family post-processes the network's planned trajectory at inference time by minimising hand-crafted cost functions~\cite{hu2023uniad,Hu2023hoplan}. Such optimisers require explicit intermediate predictions, including semantic drivable surfaces or multi-agent motion forecasts, to penalise off-road excursions and potential collisions. Annotating those intermediate targets is expensive, which undercuts the primary advantage of end-to-end imitation and reinforcement learning from readily logged ego poses and control signals~\cite{Prakash2021transfuser,wu2022tcp}. As a result, pure end-to-end architectures rarely support post-hoc cost refinement out of the box. Our goal is therefore to improve deployment reliability in a purely data-driven manner at inference time, without hand-tuned fallback rules or auxiliary perceptual supervision during testing, while acknowledging that our base planner still distils rule-based simulation scores during offline training (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:prelim}).

Centaur accomplishes this online adaptation through test-time training~\cite{sun2020ttt,liang2025ttasurvey}, updating selected network weights by gradient descent on an unsupervised loss evaluated over recent deployment observations~\cite{wang2022cotta,liang2025ttasurvey,Gao2024fstta}. Rather than perturbing output waypoints via hand-crafted obstacle costs, Centaur adjusts the score decoder weights to reduce the planner's own predictive uncertainty without ground-truth labels. Moreover, because the gradient buffer relies solely on already observed frames, weight updates can in principle be computed asynchronously alongside forward inference with minimal latency penalty, though our current experiments evaluate them sequentially (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:latency}).

Formulating a differentiable uncertainty objective for end-to-end driving is not straightforward under classic trajectory regression architectures~\cite{hu2023uniad,chitta2023transfuser,weng2024paradrive,li2024ego}, where the network outputs a single continuous waypoint sequence from sensor inputs. Standard TTT methods were developed primarily for discrete classification~\cite{Gao2024fstta,wang2021tent}, where Shannon entropy over class logits provides a natural unsupervised loss. Recent trajectory-scoring planners~\cite{li2024hydramdp,Sadat2020P3,Casas2021mp3}, however, evaluate a discrete vocabulary of candidate trajectories in a manner analogous to classification. Building on this scoring formulation, Centaur defines Cluster Entropy by grouping candidate trajectories into five directional buckets and computing the entropy of their aggregated cluster scores.

Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:teaser} depicts this mechanism on Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp} as the ego vehicle approaches a roundabout. Before adaptation, the planner selects an isolated outlier in the `slight left' group; after a single TTT step minimising Cluster Entropy, score mass concentrates on the `slight right' group, preventing an illegal left turn into oncoming traffic. Beyond driving weight updates, Cluster Entropy can also serve as a binary monitor to flag frames where the base planner risks a catastrophic failure. At a fixed threshold, it attains the highest true-positive rate among tested uncertainty metrics, although its overall accuracy remains below a trivial never-flag baseline and threshold-free curves were not logged, leaving its standalone value as a failure detector inconclusive (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:failure}).

Lastly, as benchmark averages on standard test splits approach human demonstration scores, aggregate numbers obscure rare failure modes. We therefore assemble \texttt{navsafe}, an internal 229-frame diagnostic subset of \texttt{navtest} created through a semi-automated workflow combining baseline error sorting with manual inspection and NHTSA-guided categorisation. Benchmarking current end-to-end planners on \texttt{navsafe} exposes shared vulnerabilities across scenario types and a persistent deficit relative to human driving, though sorting candidate clips by Hydra-MDP performance makes \texttt{navsafe} more informative for cross-family failure diagnosis than for comparing Hydra-MDP directly against its own adapted variants (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}).

\section{Related Work}\label{centaur:sec:related}

\paragraph{End-to-end autonomous driving.} Modern end-to-end driving architectures map raw multi-view camera or LiDAR streams directly to future waypoints or control commands with a single trainable network, replacing classic modular stacks of separate perception, forecasting, and planning stages~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey,Prakash2021transfuser,chitta2021neat,jaeger2023hidden,zimmerlin2024tf,chen2024vadv2,jiang2023vad,hu2023uniad,hu2022model,jia2023think,wang2023drivedreamer,jia2023driveadapter,li2024hydramdp,liao2025diffusiondrive}, including the 3D occupancy pipeline studied in Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}~\cite{sima2023occnet}.

\paragraph{End-to-end planner deployment.} Ensuring reliable behaviour when deploying an opaque neural policy on roads remains an open engineering problem. Existing systems typically attach rule-based verifiers or fallback controllers to filter unsafe plans: UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad} refines its predicted trajectory against an obstacle cost map at inference time, TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser} triggers hard braking whenever LiDAR returns indicate a nearby obstruction, and SafetyNet~\cite{vitelli2022safetynet} validates kinematic feasibility and collision margins before falling back to a rule-based controller. Such wrappers either demand costly perceptual labels or bias driving behaviour toward excessive caution. By contrast, Centaur invokes no hand-crafted rules at deployment time, though its underlying base planner is trained offline to distil rule-based simulation scores (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:prelim}).

\paragraph{Test-time compute and training.} Allocating additional computation during inference has proven effective for strengthening model reasoning~\cite{openaio1}, while test-time adaptation and training (TTT)~\cite{liang2025ttasurvey} have improved robustness across image recognition~\cite{sun2020ttt,wang2021tent,hakim2023clust3,marsden2024universal}, multimodal alignment~\cite{shu2022test,yoon2024c}, vision-language navigation~\cite{Gao2024fstta}, motion forecasting~\cite{park2024t4p}, robotic manipulation~\cite{hansen2021pad}, and long-context language modelling~\cite{sun2024learning}. Most of these methods update model parameters online by minimising prediction entropy or a self-supervised proxy. Following this principle, Centaur introduced, to our knowledge, the first uncertainty-minimising TTT framework for an end-to-end driving planner on NAVSIM as of March 2025. Unlike T4P~\cite{park2024t4p}, which adapts multi-agent trajectory predictors rather than ego planning policies, and unlike inference-time trajectory search methods such as Diffusion-ES~\cite{yang2024diffusiones} or TOAD~\cite{xu2026toad} that alter output plans without updating weights, Centaur adapts the planner's decoder parameters directly. Broader background appears in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}, with supplementary discussion of Hydra-MDP ensembling, semantic entropy, safety benchmarks, and evidential regression in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:related}.

\subsection{Preliminaries}\label{centaur:sec:prelim}

An end-to-end driving policy $\pi$ maps current onboard sensor observations $\mathbf{x}$ and a high-level route command $\mathbf{c}$ directly to an ego plan $\mathbf{t}$. We represent each trajectory as a discrete sequence of $N$ Bird's Eye View (BEV) waypoints $\tilde{\mathbf{t}} = (\mathbf{p}_0, \mathbf{p}_1, \dots, \mathbf{p}_N)$ spaced uniformly across the planning horizon, predicted from the current sensor frame $\mathbf{x}_0$ (\eg multi-camera images and LiDAR) and discrete navigation command $\mathbf{c}_0$ (\eg turn left, turn right, or go straight) at step $0$. Classical \emph{trajectory regression} policies predict a single waypoint sequence trained to imitate human demonstrations. While our TTT framework can be paired with regression planners by adding evidential output heads (see Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:regression} for the UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad} and TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser} formulation and Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:ablations} for TransFuser experiments), we focus primarily on the higher-performing \emph{trajectory scoring} family exemplified by Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp}, VADv2~\cite{chen2024vadv2}, and RAD~\cite{gao2025rad}.

\paragraph{Trajectory scoring.} Rather than regressing continuous coordinates, a scoring planner evaluates a fixed \emph{planning vocabulary} of $k$ candidate trajectories and predicts a vector of scalar \emph{score features} $\{s_j\}_{j=1}^k$ for each candidate $\mathbf{t}_j$. These score dimensions reflect distinct driving criteria such as collision avoidance and forward progress, which a combining function $\phi$ maps to a scalar utility so that inference picks the highest-scoring candidate:
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:multi-score-output}
  \{s_j\}_{j=1}^k = \{ \pi_\theta(\mathbf{t}_j, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{c}_0) \}_{j=1}^k,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:cost-select}
  \hat{\mathbf{t}}_0 = \argmax_{\mathbf{t}_j} \{\phi(s_j)\}_{j=1}^k.
\end{equation}
Evaluating a discrete vocabulary naturally accommodates multi-modal futures and exposes a score distribution across candidates from which predictive uncertainty can be measured. Offline training optimises the network weights $\theta$ via knowledge distillation:
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:objective-score}
  \argmin_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})\sim D_{kd}} \left[\mathcal{L}_{kd}\big(e(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c}), \pi_\theta(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})\big)\right],
\end{equation}
where the teacher $e$ evaluates ground-truth sub-scores for candidate trajectories over a distillation dataset $D_{kd} = \{(\mathbf{t}, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{c}), \dots\}$ rolled out in a driving simulator~\cite{caesar2021nuplan,dosovitskiy2017carla} or learned world model~\cite{yang2024genad,gao2024vista}. Each score dimension is scaled to $(0,1)$ and supervised with binary cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_{kd}$. In Hydra-MDP, the teacher $e$ computes the exact rule-based sub-scores of the NAVSIM evaluation metric (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:setup}), meaning that the decoder heads later adapted by TTT are explicitly trained to approximate the benchmark sub-scores. Thus, while Centaur applies no hand-crafted rules at deployment time, its base policy distils rule-based metrics offline. Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:scoring} gives the full Hydra-MDP training objective, which augments Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:objective-score} with a human-trajectory imitation term.

\subsection{Cluster Entropy}\label{centaur:sec:ce}

Viewed through Eqs.~\eqref{centaur:eq:multi-score-output} and~\eqref{centaur:eq:cost-select}, trajectory scoring resembles a $k$-way classifier over the planning vocabulary. To convert this structure into a label-free adaptation signal that needs no teacher $e$ at deployment time (unlike Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:objective-score}), we introduce Cluster Entropy. A direct application of Shannon entropy across all $k$ candidates fails in practice because a dense vocabulary of several thousand trajectories places many spatially adjacent paths around common manoeuvres such as cruising straight, all receiving nearly identical scores. Minimising raw $k$-way entropy would penalise trajectories simply for having many close neighbours in the vocabulary. Cluster Entropy avoids this density artifact in three stages.

\paragraph{Trajectory candidates.} We begin by drawing a fixed subset of $M$ candidate trajectories $\{\mathbf{t}_m\}_{m=1}^M$ ($M \ll k$) from the full vocabulary prior to deployment, weighting each vocabulary member by its mean expert score $e$ across \texttt{navtrain} (see Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:entropy} for the exact sampling distribution). These $M$ trajectories remain fixed across all test frames, though weighting by \texttt{navtrain} averages means rare manoeuvres are under-represented in the subset. Computing Shannon entropy directly over those $M$ scores defines our Full Entropy baseline, whereas the next two stages group those candidates into directional buckets to improve interpretability and performance.

\paragraph{Direction-based sampling.} From the $M$ sampled candidates, we pick five directional anchors inspired by the steering taxonomy of DriveLM~\cite{sima2024drivelm}: \texttt{sharp left}, \texttt{slight left}, \texttt{forward}, \texttt{slight right}, and \texttt{sharp right}. Specifically, the two candidates with the most extreme left and right lateral endpoint displacements become \texttt{sharp left} and \texttt{sharp right}; the candidates whose lateral displacements lie closest to half of those extremes become \texttt{slight left} and \texttt{slight right}; and the candidate with minimal lateral displacement becomes \texttt{forward}. Whereas DriveLM pairs five steering bins with five longitudinal speed bins (Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}), Cluster Entropy retains only the five lateral steering anchors. Consequently, braking and cruising along the same lane fall into the same directional bucket, leaving longitudinal stop-or-go ambiguity unrepresented (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Score aggregation.} As depicted at the top of Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:pipeline}, we partition the $M$ candidates into five directional clusters $\mathcal{C}_1, \dots, \mathcal{C}_5$ by assigning each candidate to its closest anchor under Euclidean ($L_2$) waypoint distance. Summing the predicted scores within each cluster $\mathcal{C}_a$ produces an unnormalised cluster mass $\mathit{score}_a$, which we normalise into a five-way categorical distribution to compute the Shannon entropy $H$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:ce}
  p_a = \frac{\mathit{score}_a}{\sum_{a'=1}^{5} \mathit{score}_{a'}}, \qquad
  H(\mathbf{x}) = -\sum_{a=1}^{5} p_a \log p_a .
\end{equation}
Across five directional buckets, $H(\mathbf{x})$ is bounded between $0$ (all score weight concentrated in a single steering direction) and $\ln 5$ (equal weight across all five directions). Because cluster cardinalities are fixed offline and unequal, summing candidate scores within each cluster still weights larger clusters more heavily, retaining a milder form of the vocabulary-density bias whose exact cluster-size distribution we did not separately audit.

\subsection{Test-Time Training}\label{centaur:sec:ttt}

With the label-free objective $H(\mathbf{x})$ in hand, Centaur adapts the planner online by reducing directional score dispersion at deployment time. Following standard single-step TTT practice~\cite{Gao2024fstta}, we evaluate $H(\mathbf{x})$ via a forward pass of $\pi_\theta$ and backpropagate to obtain the parameter gradient $\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}$. Rather than applying each noisy single-frame gradient in isolation, we maintain a ring buffer of length $F=4$ containing the most recent gradients (Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:pipeline}, bottom). At evaluation step $i$, we average the buffered gradients into $\{\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}\}_{avg}$ and take a single step of size $\eta$ from the base decoder weights $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:ttt}
  \hat{\theta}_i = \theta - \eta \left\{\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}\right\}_{avg},
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ denotes the test-time learning rate. By penalising diffuse mass across directional buckets, Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ttt} dampens isolated high-scoring outliers in weakly supported directions, such as the errant `slight left' trajectory in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:teaser}. Because entropy minimisation only sharpens existing modes toward the cluster with the largest aggregate mass, it can reinforce an erroneous dominant direction when the base policy is confidently wrong (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:discussion}). Algorithm~\ref{centaur:alg:ttt} states the complete update loop.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \DontPrintSemicolon
  \caption{Test-time training in Centaur}\label{centaur:alg:ttt}
  \KwIn{pre-trained scoring policy $\pi_\theta$ with adaptable decoder weights $\theta$ (perception backbones frozen); vocabulary $\{\mathbf{t}_j\}_{j=1}^{k}$; $M$ sampled candidates partitioned around five directional anchors (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ce}); gradient buffer size $F$; step size $\eta$}
  \KwOut{selected plan $\hat{\mathbf{t}}_0$ at each evaluation step}
  $\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \emptyset$ \tcp*{FIFO ring buffer of past gradients}
  \ForEach{evaluation step $i$ with sensor observation $\mathbf{x}_0$ and route command $\mathbf{c}_0$}{
    \eIf{$\mathcal{B} \neq \emptyset$}{
      $\hat{\theta}_i \leftarrow \theta - \eta \left\{\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}\right\}_{avg}$ using the mean gradient across $\mathcal{B}$ \tcp*{Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ttt}}
    }{
      $\hat{\theta}_i \leftarrow \theta$\;
    }
    Evaluate vocabulary scores under $\pi_{\hat{\theta}_i}$ and pick $\hat{\mathbf{t}}_0$ \tcp*{Eqs.~\eqref{centaur:eq:multi-score-output}--\eqref{centaur:eq:cost-select}}
    \tcp{can execute asynchronously on parallel compute}
    Evaluate Cluster Entropy $H(\mathbf{x}_0)$ over the $M$ candidate scores \tcp*{Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ce}}
    Backpropagate $\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}$ and append to $\mathcal{B}$, retaining at most $F$ recent steps\;
  }
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Operational semantics.} Algorithm~\ref{centaur:alg:ttt} and Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ttt} define an episodic reset update rule with four key properties. First, every step $i$ updates from the base decoder weights $\theta$ as in Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ttt}: the adapted parameters $\hat{\theta}_i$ are used solely to score step $i$ and then discarded, preventing weight drift across long sequences and restricting temporal memory to the $F=4$ buffered gradients. Second, step $i$ depends only on gradients from the four preceding evaluation steps, leaving the very first step unadapted ($\hat{\theta}_1 = \theta$). Third, the ring buffer $\mathcal{B}$ averages gradients over the four preceding evaluation steps without resetting across clips. Fourth, adaptation modifies only the score decoder parameters $\theta$ via a single vanilla gradient step of size $\eta$, while keeping the visual and LiDAR backbones frozen. Although each NAVSIM sample bundles 1.5 seconds (four frames) of sensor history as input (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}), the $F=4$ buffer entries correspond to the four preceding evaluation steps rather than the within-sample input history; in either case, because NAVSIM replays human-driven logs, every buffered gradient comes from human-driven observations rather than states visited by the planner's own past actions.

\paragraph{Deployment.} Because step $i$ consumes only gradients already stored in $\mathcal{B}$ from earlier steps, the buffer not only smooths step-to-step gradient variance but also decouples backpropagation from the critical inference path, allowing gradient computation to run asynchronously on separate compute. Our current implementation does not parallelise this step; instead, backpropagation runs sequentially and is included in all latency measurements (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:latency}).

To inspect planner behaviour on rare, high-risk situations, we construct \texttt{navsafe}, an internal 229-frame diagnostic subset of \texttt{navtest} rather than a separate public release. All frames are drawn from NAVSIM~\cite{dauner2024navsim}, which itself curates dynamic driving logs from OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene} (Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}) and nuPlan~\cite{caesar2021nuplan}.

\paragraph{NAVSIM.} NAVSIM filters nuPlan logs to emphasise non-trivial manoeuvres where linear extrapolation of the ego vehicle's past motion fails. Its training split (\texttt{navtrain}) comprises 1,192 logs (103,288 frames) and its test split (\texttt{navtest}) comprises 136 logs (12,146 frames). At each evaluation frame, the policy receives 1.5 seconds (4 frames) of multi-camera and LiDAR history together with the ego status (current speed, acceleration, and discrete route command) and must output a 4-second future trajectory. All evaluations on \texttt{navsafe} preserve this exact input-output protocol.

\paragraph{Construction criteria.} As shown in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:navsafe}, we organize \texttt{navsafe} around real-world crash patterns documented in the NHTSA pre-crash scenario typology~\cite{najm2007pre,thorn2018framework}. That typology catalogues collision precursors ranging from frequent urban hazards to rare roadway events; its five most prevalent categories (shown in the top-left inset of Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:navsafe}) are ``Lead Vehicle Stopped'', ``Control Loss Without Prior Vehicle Action'', ``Vehicle(s) Turning at Non-Signalized Junctions'', ``Lead Vehicle Decelerating'', and ``Road Edge Departure Without Prior Vehicle Maneuver''~\cite{najm2007pre}. Guided by these crash categories, we select challenging frames from \texttt{navtest} through an iterative five-step loop:
\begin{enumerate}[label={Step~\arabic*.}, start=0, leftmargin=*]
  \item Rank the clips in \texttt{navtest} by the mean PDMS of the unadapted base planner Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp}.
  \item Inspect the lowest-scoring clips manually and assign candidate frames to existing or newly defined \texttt{navsafe} categories.
  \item Add verified frames to the corresponding scenario buckets.
  \item Consult the NHTSA typology to introduce additional scenario buckets not yet covered.
  \item Re-rank the remaining \texttt{navtest} clips and repeat the curation pass.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Methodological implications of the curation protocol.} Three aspects of Steps~0--4 govern how \texttt{navsafe} numbers should be interpreted. First, ranking candidate clips by Hydra-MDP's baseline PDMS in Step~0 systematically selects frames where Hydra-MDP underperforms. Because of regression to the mean, any modified version of Hydra-MDP (whether adapted via TTT or re-trained with a new random seed) will naturally tend to improve on those selected frames; we did not run an independent Hydra-MDP seed or random-weight perturbation control to isolate this selection effect, nor did we select frames using an external planner. Second, because \texttt{navsafe} is a 229-frame subset of \texttt{navtest} rather than an independent split (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:setup}), it samples the difficult tail of the same driving logs as \texttt{navtrain} rather than unseen geographical domains. Third, human annotators assigned frames to categories without a formal inter-annotator agreement study.

\paragraph{Highlights of \texttt{navsafe}.} The resulting subset comprises 229 frames from the 12,146 frames of \texttt{navtest}, partitioned into 10 diagnostic categories: ``Roundabout'', ``Yellow light, rush or wait?'', ``Exit ramp'', ``Unprotected left'', ``Enter ramp'', ``Uncommon traffic sign'', ``Overtaking with lane change'', ``No lane area (\eg parking lot)'', ``Bad weather'', and ``Yielding (\eg to pedestrians)''. Reporting per-category scores breaks down aggregate benchmark performance into interpretable driving competencies. Although the 2D OpenCLIP projection~\cite{ilharco2021openclip} in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:navsafe} suggests that many \texttt{navsafe} frames sit near the periphery of \texttt{navtest}, several overlap the dense core, and we did not compute exact embedding-distance percentiles relative to \texttt{navtrain}. Within this thesis, \texttt{navsafe} therefore serves as an internal 229-frame diagnostic subset of \texttt{navtest} rather than a separate public release, probing tail failures within the logged distribution against both human demonstrations and baseline planners.

\section{Experiments}\label{centaur:sec:exp}

We evaluate Centaur on the NAVSIM benchmark~\cite{dauner2024navsim}, reporting results on the full \texttt{navtest} split, the multi-seed official leaderboard, and the 229-frame \texttt{navsafe} diagnostic subset from Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{centaur:sec:setup}

\paragraph{Metrics.} Following the standard NAVSIM v1 protocol, we score each planned 4-second trajectory in non-reactive log replay, where surrounding agents follow their recorded trajectories without reacting to the ego vehicle, using the Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS):
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:pdms}
  \mathrm{PDMS} = \mathrm{NC} \times \mathrm{DAC} \times \left( \frac{5\,\mathrm{TTC} + 2\,\mathrm{C} + 5\,\mathrm{EP}}{12} \right),
\end{equation}
which combines two multiplicative penalty factors, No at-fault Collision (NC) and Drivable Area Compliance (DAC), with a weighted average of Time-to-Collision (TTC), Comfort (C), and Ego Progress (EP), all expressed as percentages~\cite{dauner2024navsim}. We pay particular attention to TTC, which checks whether constant-velocity projection of the ego state results in a collision within a 1-second window at any step along the 4-second horizon.

\paragraph{Scope of non-reactive evaluation.} Because each test frame initialises the ego vehicle at the human driver's logged state and the subsequent frame resets to the next logged human state, planning errors never accumulate over time, preventing any test of closed-loop behavioural shift or long-horizon drift (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:discussion}). While we include the PDMS of logged human trajectories as a benchmark reference, human driving is not a strict upper bound because EP is normalised against the privileged rule-based planner PDM-Closed~\cite{dauner2023parting}, whose standalone score is omitted here.

\paragraph{Base model.} Our primary base policy is Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp}, which voxelises LiDAR returns into a BEV grid encoded with ResNet34~\cite{he2016deep} and fuses them with multi-view camera features from a \mbox{V2-99}~\cite{park2021pseudo} backbone through TransFuser cross-attention blocks~\cite{chitta2023transfuser}. Its score decoder evaluates $k=8192$ candidate trajectories across five heads corresponding to NC, DAC, EP, C, and TTC, trained by distilling the rule-based NAVSIM metric (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:prelim}). To verify transfer to regression planners, we also evaluate a lighter ResNet34-based TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser} policy in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ablations} and Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:ablations}.

\paragraph{Deployment strategy.} To compare TTT against an uncertainty-gated override, we construct a fallback-to-prior-trajectories baseline. Offline, we rank the $k=8192$ vocabulary trajectories of Hydra-MDP by their mean PDMS on \texttt{navtrain} and cache the top 20 trajectories as safe priors. At deployment time, whenever a frame's estimated uncertainty exceeds a preset threshold (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class}), the planner discards its selected trajectory and substitutes the closest of the 20 cached priors in $L_2$ waypoint distance. Unlike production safety wrappers such as SafetyNet~\cite{vitelli2022safetynet}, which verify collision-free kinematic feasibility and hand control to a scene-aware rule-based planner, our simplified fallback triggers solely on scalar uncertainty and selects from 20 scene-agnostic priors without checking obstacles or invoking PDM-Closed~\cite{dauner2023parting}.

\paragraph{Uncertainty measures.} Alongside Cluster Entropy, we evaluate three baseline uncertainty criteria. \textbf{KL Divergence} converts each of the five predicted sub-score heads (NC, DAC, EP, C, and TTC) into a categorical distribution over the $k=8192$ vocabulary trajectories and sums the pairwise Kullback--Leibler divergences across all head pairs. \textbf{Full Entropy}~\cite{shannon1948entropy} computes Shannon entropy directly over the $M=100$ sampled candidate scores without directional clustering. \textbf{Semantic Entropy} adapts linguistic semantic uncertainty~\cite{kuhn2023semantic} to trajectory planning by clustering the $M=100$ candidates in the space of predicted score vectors $s_j$ rather than BEV waypoint space (see Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:entropy}); we denote Hydra-MDP adapted with Semantic Entropy as \textbf{Hydra-SE}.

\paragraph{Implementation.} Single-seed ablation studies (Tables~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation} and~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}) use NAVSIM v1.0, whereas the three-seed official leaderboard submission (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all}) uses NAVSIM v1.1, which introduces minor metric refinements. We train Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp} on \texttt{navtrain} for 20 epochs across 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with batch size 256 and AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2017decoupled} (learning rate $10^{-4}$, zero weight decay). At test time, Cluster Entropy and TTT use $M=100$ candidates, buffer length $F=4$, and a single gradient step with $\eta = 10^{-4}$ on the score decoder while keeping all perception backbones frozen. Because NAVSIM provides no separate validation split in our setup, the hyper-parameters $\eta$, $M$, $F$, and the Semantic Entropy threshold $\tau$ (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:exp}) were selected directly on \texttt{navtest}, from which \texttt{navsafe} is also drawn.

Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation} presents the primary evaluation outcomes along two dimensions: the deployment mechanism and the choice of uncertainty estimator.

\paragraph{Deployment strategy.} In Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}, triggering a fallback trajectory via any of the four uncertainty estimators improves the safety metrics (NC, DAC, and TTC) yet drops total PDMS from 90.3 to the 63.1--65.9 range, driven by an ego progress (EP) collapse from 86.5 to under 15. However, these fallback entries do not align numerically with the trigger thresholds reported for failure detection. Combining the 15.6\% failure prevalence with the TPR and accuracy figures in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class} implies that the thresholds activate on roughly 29\% (KL Divergence) to 33\% (Full Entropy) of \texttt{navtest} frames (see Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:consistency}). We next evaluate binary detection of planning failures across the four uncertainty functionals, a capability relevant to partially automated vehicles that request human takeover in difficult traffic. Treating a frame with a PDMS of 0 as a failure event, we flag any scene whose estimated uncertainty exceeds a fixed cutoff. Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class} lists the resulting true-positive rate (TPR) and classification accuracy. For the entropy-based estimators, the cutoff is set to $0.8 = 0.5\ln 5$, equal to half the maximum entropy of a uniform distribution over 5 clusters. At that cutoff, Cluster Entropy achieves both the highest TPR (62.8\%) and the highest accuracy (73.6\%) among the four methods. Several methodological caveats qualify this comparison. Because the Select All row indicates a 15.6\% failure prevalence, a trivial classifier that flags nothing (Select None) attains 84.4\% accuracy, surpassing every uncertainty estimator including Cluster Entropy and making raw accuracy an unhelpful metric here. Furthermore, Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class} evaluates a single cutoff per estimator, and a KL cutoff of 1500 is not calibrated to match the false-positive rate of an entropy cutoff of 0.8. Without threshold-independent summaries such as the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) or precision--recall curve (AUPRC), or TPR matched at a common false-positive rate, these numbers do not establish uniform dominance across operating points.

In addition, the 15.6\% failure prevalence in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class} cannot arise from a strict $\text{PDMS}=0$ criterion under the numbers in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}. If 15.6\% of frames scored 0, the average PDMS of Hydra-MDP would be bounded above by 84.4 rather than reaching 90.3. By Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:pdms}, $\text{PDMS}=0$ requires either $\text{NC}=0$ or $\text{DAC}=0$ (since a 100.0 mean comfort score precludes TTC, C, and EP simultaneously vanishing), and the 98.4 mean NC and 97.8 mean DAC of Hydra-MDP cap the fraction of zero-PDMS frames at 3.8\% (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:consistency}). The 15.6\% prevalence for Hydra-MDP and the corresponding 24.1\% prevalence for TransFuser in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class_supp} therefore reflect a broader low-score failure indicator logged in the original evaluation script rather than strict $\text{PDMS}=0$; because all four estimators in Tables~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class} and~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class_supp} were scored against the same indicator labels, their relative ordering at the tested cutoffs remains internally consistent, though the absolute base rates should be interpreted with that definition in mind. In view of these results, subsequent experiments concentrate on the two top-performing entropy measures at the tested threshold: Semantic Entropy and Cluster Entropy.

Under TTT, the ordering of the four estimators by TPR in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class} matches their ordering by PDMS in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation} (from KL Divergence at the bottom to Cluster Entropy at the top), although the three entropy variants lie within 0.8 PDMS of one another in single-seed runs. Under the fallback strategy, the discrepancy between the trigger rates implied by Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class} and the EP values in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation} prevents a parallel comparison: Full Entropy rather than Cluster Entropy has the highest implied trigger rate, and fallback PDMS moves in the same direction as the implied trigger rate rather than inversely (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:consistency}). Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:ablations} reports additional threshold and planner ablations.

To examine behaviour across distinct traffic hazards, we evaluate the planners on the ten scenario categories of \texttt{navsafe} in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navsafe}. Several categories expose weaknesses shared by all unadapted baselines: on exit ramps (EXR) and unlaned areas (NLA), every planner without TTT remains below 50 PDMS. Category breakdowns also reveal model-specific patterns, though five categories contain between 5 and 12 frames and their per-category scores carry corresponding sampling noise. On roundabouts (RDBT) and overtaking with lane changes (OTLC), for instance, Hydra-MDP performs near the blind Ego Status MLP baseline, whereas the camera-only LTF architecture outscores full sensor-fusion baselines such as TransFuser on yellow lights (YLLT) and entry ramps (ENR). Across the ten categories, Centaur ranks first in seven, and its preprint Overall figure of 74.14 exceeds the 56.47 of Hydra-MDP and the 62.84 of Hydra-SE. Two qualifications apply to this comparison. First, because \texttt{navsafe} clips were chosen by sorting on Hydra-MDP's score (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}), regression to the mean will inflate gains for any modified version of Hydra-MDP in the absence of an independent control run. Second, the thesis relies on the explicit frame-weighted (68.9 \vs 49.4) and simple (72.0 \vs 48.3) category means in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navsafe_overall} rather than the unannotated Overall column: under both explicit aggregations Centaur remains the top planner, whereas Hydra-MDP drops behind LTF and TransFuser. Moreover, Centaur's gains are not uniform across scenarios: on yielding (YLD), its score drops from 71.6 for Hydra-MDP to 59.3, the sole category where TTT degrades its base model, and it trails the top baseline on both BWTH and YLLT (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:discussion}). Even with TTT, a substantial gap persists relative to logged human trajectories (74.14 \vs 94.65 in the Overall column).

\section{Analysis}\label{centaur:sec:analysis}

We now analyse how test-time updates alter trajectory selection, whether the approach transfers to regression-based architectures, how hyper-parameters affect performance, and what computational overhead TTT introduces. Full numerical tables for the latter three questions appear in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:ablations}.

Grouping candidate trajectories into directional clusters also makes the effect of the test-time update straightforward to inspect visually. Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:vis} contrasts two example scenes. In the upper example, unadapted Hydra-MDP selects a slight-left candidate that leaves a narrow time-to-collision buffer; after the Cluster Entropy gradient step, Centaur shifts its top score to a forward trajectory with a wider safety margin. \subsection{Generality and Design Choices}\label{centaur:sec:ablations}

\paragraph{Trajectory regression planners.} The TTT procedure applies beyond vocabulary-scoring architectures. Adding an evidential uncertainty head (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:regression}) to the regression outputs of TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser} and adapting with Semantic Entropy produces TransFuser-SE, lifting single-run NAVSIM v1.0 PDMS from 83.4 to 85.7 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}), the same 2.3-point increment observed when going from Hydra-MDP to Centaur. Adapting TransFuser via evidential uncertainty directly yields 84.2 PDMS, whereas the uncertainty-triggered fallback again depresses EP below 15 and total PDMS to roughly 60 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}), exhibiting the same threshold-rate mismatch noted for Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation} in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:consistency}.

\paragraph{Test-time optimisation with explicit representations.} Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp} also evaluates two baselines that incorporate explicit intermediate representations or hand-crafted post-processing on NAVSIM v1.0. In UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad}, refining the initial plan with an expert-designed cost function lowers overall PDMS from 83.4 to 75.2. Rather than trading progress for safety as in the fallback rows of Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}, this post-hoc optimisation degrades all five components in a single-seed run: NC drops from 97.8 to 95.4, DAC from 91.9 to 86.0, TTC from 92.9 to 87.3, EP from 78.8 to 71.9, and C from 100.0 to 99.3. In PARA-Drive~\cite{weng2024paradrive}, adding an explicit 3D occupancy prediction head shifts single-run PDMS from 84.0 to 83.6, a difference within single-seed noise. These two comparisons indicate only that the specific hand-crafted additions tested here did not help on NAVSIM v1.0, not that explicit occupancy or cost-based refinement is detrimental in general; because protocol, dataset, and planner architecture all differ from the open-loop evaluations in Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}, the two settings are not directly comparable.

\paragraph{Combining historical gradients.} Rather than averaging gradients across the history buffer with equal weights, one can project the buffered gradients via singular-value decomposition (SVD) following~\cite{Gao2024fstta} to extract dominant update directions. Applied to TransFuser-SE, SVD aggregation yields 83.8 PDMS versus 85.7 for uniform averaging (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}).

\paragraph{Buffer length.} Varying the history window length $F$ produces only minor differences: across $F=1$, $2$, $3$, and $4$, Hydra-SE records 91.4, 91.3, 91.5, and 91.8 PDMS, respectively (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}). Every window length improves upon the unadapted 90.3 PDMS of Hydra-MDP, and while the default $F=4$ achieves the highest number, the 0.5-point spread across single runs on \texttt{navtest} is too narrow to establish a monotonic trend.

\paragraph{Image backbone.} Substituting a ViT-L visual encoder for the default \mbox{V2-99} backbone in Hydra-MDP reduces PDMS slightly both before adaptation (89.9 \vs 90.3) and under TTT with KL Divergence (90.9 \vs 91.5). Under the KL-triggered fallback, ViT-L records 67.4 versus 63.1 for \mbox{V2-99} (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}), though those fallback entries carry the threshold inconsistency detailed in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:results}.

\subsection{Computational Cost}\label{centaur:sec:latency}

Per-frame amortised inference latency on \texttt{navtest}, benchmarked on one NVIDIA A100 GPU, is listed in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}. Unadapted Hydra-MDP requires 243.9\,ms per frame, whereas TransFuser runs in 103.1\,ms with its lighter ResNet-34 encoder at a cost of roughly 7 PDMS points. Because our synchronous evaluation loop executes backpropagation and weight updates inline with forward inference, enabling TTT on Hydra-MDP raises per-frame latency to 312.5\,ms under Semantic Entropy and 357.1\,ms under KL Divergence; timing for Centaur with Cluster Entropy was not separately logged. On an A100 GPU, these timings correspond to roughly 2.8 to 4.1 planning updates per second (derived from Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}), which exceeds the 2\,Hz frame rate of NAVSIM (four frames spanning 1.5 seconds), although deployment on embedded vehicle compute at higher control frequencies remains untested. Latency overhead can be curtailed either by overlapping gradient computation with the subsequent frame's forward pass (outlined in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ttt} but not implemented here) or by gating TTT to run only when uncertainty exceeds a cutoff, which brings Hydra-SE down to 279.6\,ms at 91.3 PDMS.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{centaur:sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.} Within the non-reactive replay protocol of NAVSIM v1, adapting a planner via its own predictive uncertainty raised single-seed Hydra-MDP performance from 90.3 to 92.6 PDMS on NAVSIM v1.0, driven primarily by a TTC increase from 93.9 to 98.0 alongside a minor EP decrease from 86.5 to 85.9. Across three seeds on NAVSIM v1.1, Centaur reached 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 PDMS, though without a matched three-seed unadapted Hydra-MDP entry for comparison. Across all architectures and uncertainty functionals tested in this chapter, single-seed TTT runs improved over their unadapted counterparts by 0.4 to 2.3 PDMS (Tables~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}, \ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}, and~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}). While the update step itself requires no ground-truth labels, hand-crafted rules, or external cost terms at deployment, the underlying scoring heads still rely on rule-based supervision distilled during offline training. Because non-reactive replay resets the ego state to the human log at every frame, these gains reflect single-horizon choices on logged states rather than multi-step recovery in closed loop.

\paragraph{Comparison with the fallback.} In Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}, replacing uncertain plans with a fixed prior trajectory lifted NC, DAC, and TTC at the expense of cutting EP below 15 and PDMS to 63.1--65.9, whereas TTT improved the safety metrics with minimal loss in progress. Two factors restrict the generality of that contrast. First, substituting one of 20 static trajectories whenever uncertainty crosses a cutoff is a simplistic heuristic compared with industrial safety monitors such as SafetyNet~\cite{vitelli2022safetynet}, which verify kinematic feasibility and collision risk before invoking a complete rule-based fallback planner. Second, the tabulated fallback numbers do not match the trigger rates implied by the failure-detection cutoffs (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:results}). The other hand-crafted baseline in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}, UniAD's post-hoc cost optimisation, reduced every sub-score including NC, DAC, and TTC, and thus does not represent a safety-for-progress trade-off either. Accordingly, the experiments support a specific conclusion: under non-reactive replay on logged scenes, TTT raised PDMS whereas our heuristic trajectory-replacement fallback reduced it, without implying that test-time gradient updates outperform full rule-based safety envelopes in general. Similarly, while Centaur's preprint Overall score on \texttt{navsafe} exceeds Hydra-MDP's by 17.67 points (and by 68.9 \vs 49.4 under frame-weighted averaging or 72.0 \vs 48.3 under simple averaging in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navsafe_overall}) compared with 2.3 PDMS on \texttt{navtest}, that gap is confounded by selection bias because \texttt{navsafe} was filtered using Hydra-MDP's own errors (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}). Adaptation also introduces scenario-specific regressions, particularly on yielding (YLD), where Centaur drops from 71.6 to 59.3.

\paragraph{How much of the gain is due to Cluster Entropy?} The 90.3-to-92.6 PDMS improvement comes from a single seed on NAVSIM v1.0 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}). Averaged across three seeds on the NAVSIM v1.1 benchmark, Centaur exceeds its Semantic Entropy counterpart Hydra-SE by just 0.23 PDMS (92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 \vs 91.87 $\pm$ 0.19 in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all}), a difference smaller than the 0.33 inter-seed standard deviation of Centaur and not backed by a formal significance test. In addition, all four uncertainty objectives in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation} (three entropy formulations plus KL Divergence) lift Hydra-MDP, adding 1.2 PDMS with KL Divergence and up to 2.3 PDMS with Cluster Entropy. Together, these observations indicate that the primary accuracy benefit stems from generic entropy-style sharpening at test time rather than the specific formulation of Cluster Entropy, whose clearer advantage lies in directional interpretability (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:qualitative}).

\paragraph{Interpretability and failure detection.} Partitioning trajectories into five lateral directional bins allows practitioners to see which manoeuvres compete for probability mass on a given frame (Figs.~\ref{centaur:fig:teaser} and~\ref{centaur:fig:vis}). By contrast, the empirical case for using Cluster Entropy as an automated takeover trigger is inconclusive: although it achieves the highest TPR (62.8\%, Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class}) at the tested cutoff, its 73.6\% accuracy falls short of the 84.4\% attained by never flagging a failure, raw accuracy is uninformative under a 15.6\% base rate, curve-wide metrics (AUROC, AUPRC) were not recorded, and the logged failure indicator diverges from a strict $\text{PDMS}=0$ definition (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:failure}). Consequently, its reliability as a human-intervention alert in partially automated driving remains unproven.

\paragraph{Threats to validity: what the evaluation cannot reveal.} Under NAVSIM v1, each 4-second trajectory is rolled out from a human-logged state while surrounding traffic replays its recorded paths, after which the simulator resets to the next human-logged frame. Because the ego vehicle never visits states induced by its own prior actions, compounding trajectory errors cannot arise, hiding closed-loop failure modes such as overly timid driving that stalls progress along a route, frame-to-frame oscillation between competing manoeuvres, or distribution shift induced by the adapting policy itself. Likewise, the four-frame TTT buffer is populated by human-driven observations rather than self-generated states, and resetting decoder weights to $\theta$ at every step (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ttt}) prevents the experiments from testing whether continual online updates would suffer from parameter drift, representation collapse, or catastrophic forgetting. Beyond this, no separate validation split was used: design choices and ablations were evaluated directly on \texttt{navtest}, from which \texttt{navsafe} is also drawn, which can introduce optimistic selection bias. Except for the three-seed leaderboard table, all NAVSIM v1.0, ablation, and \texttt{navsafe} comparisons rely on single runs without frame-level paired tests or confidence bounds.

\paragraph{Threats to validity: what the gain may reflect.} Two confounding mechanisms could account for part of the observed PDMS gains. First, because TTT applies a single step with learning rate $\eta=10^{-4}$ and improves PDMS under every tested uncertainty objective (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}), the improvement could reflect generic temperature sharpening or score re-calibration of the decoder heads rather than adaptation to the local traffic scene. Ruling out generic re-calibration would require two control experiments that were not performed: updating the decoder on buffers sampled from unrelated driving clips, and applying a static entropy-minimisation pass on \texttt{navtrain} prior to evaluation. If a static pre-test update captured most of the gain, the mechanism would be better characterised as offline score sharpening than as online scene adaptation. Second, the evaluation metric and the architecture are coupled: Hydra-MDP trains its decoder heads directly to imitate the PDMS components NC, DAC, EP, C, and TTC (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:prelim}), and the $M$ candidate trajectories are pre-filtered using their mean PDMS on \texttt{navtrain} (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:entropy}). Sharpening those distilled heads can therefore elevate PDMS partly by construction, and we have not tested whether gains persist on independent metrics such as those introduced in NAVSIM v2~\cite{cao2025pseudo}.

\paragraph{Threats to validity: what the objective cannot represent.} Because the five cluster anchors vary solely in lateral endpoint offset (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ce}), Cluster Entropy cannot distinguish between maintaining speed and braking to a stop within the same lane. Consequently, the objective neither quantifies nor resolves longitudinal decision uncertainty, which governs yielding (YLD), yellow-light (YLLT), and NHTSA ``Lead Vehicle Stopped'' scenarios. This structural limitation offers a plausible account for why Centaur drops from 71.6 to 59.3 on YLD and trails LTF on YLLT, although we have not verified this hypothesis by testing joint longitudinal--lateral anchors. In addition, entropy minimisation purely concentrates probability mass around whichever mode already dominates: if the planner's initial preference is wrong, the gradient step can reinforce the mistake rather than reverse it~\cite{wang2022cotta}, consistent with the failure case in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:vis}. A systematic audit of frames where TTT degrades PDMS was not conducted; gating updates by an uncertainty cutoff, as explored for Hydra-SE in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}, may limit harmful updates on confident frames but was only assessed for aggregate PDMS and latency on \texttt{navtest}.

\paragraph{Threats to validity: \texttt{navsafe}.} As noted in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}, constructing \texttt{navsafe} by selecting clips where Hydra-MDP scored poorly introduces regression to the mean in any comparison between Hydra-MDP and its modified variants, and no independent seed of unadapted Hydra-MDP was run as a control. The subset is drawn from \texttt{navtest} rather than a separate holdout split, its peripheral position in the feature distribution is supported only by a qualitative projection (Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:navsafe}), five of its ten categories contain just 5 to 12 frames without confidence intervals, inter-annotator agreement for scenario labels was not measured, and the thesis relies on the explicit category means in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navsafe_overall} alongside the unannotated preprint Overall column in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navsafe}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Several practical constraints remain for future work. First, Centaur has not been tested in reactive closed-loop simulators, under the pseudo-simulation protocol of NAVSIM version~2~\cite{cao2025pseudo}, or on physical vehicles; adapting the method to closed-loop control under tight compute budgets is an important next step. Second, onboard autonomous driving hardware imposes strict latency budgets: our synchronous implementation includes backward-pass timing in per-frame latency, the asynchronous pipeline sketched in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ttt} was not implemented, and standalone latency for Centaur with Cluster Entropy was not logged (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:latency}). Third, despite leading on aggregate \texttt{navsafe} metrics, Centaur regresses on YLD from 71.6 (Hydra-MDP) to 59.3, falling behind both Hydra-SE (84.6) and TransFuser (61.6) in scenarios involving pedestrians and other vulnerable road users; it also scores below Hydra-SE (64.4) and LTF (62.8) on BWTH (60.2), below LTF on YLLT (65.0 \vs 70.2), and slightly below Hydra-MDP on \texttt{navtest} ego progress (85.9 \vs 86.5). Finally, the method requires a planner that outputs a trajectory distribution (via discrete vocabulary scoring or an evidential regression head as in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:regression}) and updates only the terminal scoring head while keeping the perception encoder frozen; extending test-time adaptation to upstream perception and reasoning representations connects to the broader architectural question (RQ4) examined in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{centaur:sec:summary}

This chapter investigated the first half of the third research question (RQ3a): how an end-to-end driving policy can maintain planning quality under deployment shift without relying on hand-crafted rules, explicit cost functions, or additional ground-truth supervision at test time. Our answer applies to per-frame trajectory selection on logged states, avoiding rules at deployment while still relying on rule-based scores distilled during offline training. We presented Centaur, a test-time training framework for trajectory-scoring planners driven by Cluster Entropy, an unsupervised uncertainty objective that pools predicted candidate scores across five lateral driving directions and measures their Shannon entropy. At each planning step, Centaur resets the scoring decoder to the trained parameters and applies one gradient update computed by averaging over the four most recent frames, a structure compatible with asynchronous execution alongside inference (though evaluated synchronously here). To probe rare traffic hazards, we also curated \texttt{navsafe}, a 229-frame subset of \texttt{navtest} organised into ten NHTSA-motivated scenario classes by ranking clips on Hydra-MDP performance.

Because all experiments run under the non-reactive replay protocol of NAVSIM v1, errors do not accumulate across steps and neither closed-loop state shift nor long-horizon parameter drift is evaluated. In single-seed NAVSIM v1.0 testing on \texttt{navtest}, Centaur lifts Hydra-MDP from 90.3 to 92.6 PDMS, led by a time-to-collision gain from 93.9 to 98.0 alongside a minor ego-progress drop from 86.5 to 85.9; by contrast, the heuristic prior-trajectory fallback lowers PDMS to 63.1--65.9 with ego progress below 15, in rows whose trigger frequencies do not match the failure-detection cutoffs. Averaged across three seeds on the official NAVSIM v1.1 \texttt{navtest} leaderboard (March 2025), Centaur placed first among three-seed submissions at 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 PDMS using a single model per seed and default Hydra-MDP training hyper-parameters, although the leaderboard lacks a matched three-seed unadapted Hydra-MDP baseline. Because Centaur's three-seed margin over Hydra-SE is just 0.23 PDMS and every TTT variant tested improved its base planner by 0.4 to 2.3 PDMS in single runs, the empirical gain is primarily attributable to entropy-based test-time updating as a general mechanism rather than to Cluster Entropy specifically. For failure flagging at a fixed cutoff, Cluster Entropy achieves the top TPR (62.8\%) among compared estimators, but its 73.6\% accuracy sits below the 84.4\% Select None baseline under the logged 15.6\% failure base rate and no threshold-free curve metrics were computed. On \texttt{navsafe}, Centaur achieves the top category-average performance (68.9 \vs 49.4 frame-weighted and 72.0 \vs 48.3 simple mean in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navsafe_overall}, or 74.14 \vs 56.47 in the preprint's Overall column) in a setup that favours Hydra-MDP variants by construction, while regressing on yielding (from 71.6 to 59.3) and remaining well below the 94.65 human reference. The same TTT paradigm adds +2.3 PDMS to the regression planner TransFuser with Semantic Entropy, whereas UniAD's hand-crafted cost refinement reduces every sub-score and drops overall PDMS from 83.4 to 75.2. Key limitations include open-loop state resetting, the lack of a separate validation split, potential confounding from generic calibration of metric-distilled heads, lateral-only cluster anchors that omit longitudinal stopping decisions, extra test-time compute, and the inability of entropy minimisation to reverse a confident erroneous prediction.

Within non-reactive replay on logged driving scenes, converting a planner's own predictive uncertainty into a test-time gradient signal thus improved trajectory scoring; verifying whether those gains carry over to reactive closed-loop control remains future work. Moving from autonomous driving to robotic manipulation widens the train-to-deployment gap further, as human teleoperation data are noisy, demonstrations and compute are scarce, and physical hardware dynamics shift at deployment. Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} addresses this regime.
