\begin{contribution}
This chapter is based on the preprint ``Centaur: Robust End-to-End Autonomous Driving with Test-Time Training''~\cite{sima2024centaur}, of which the author is an equal-contribution first author with Kashyap Chitta and the primary contact. The work was carried out during the author's internship at NVIDIA Autonomous Vehicle Applied Research. \authorcheck{state which parts the author designed, implemented, evaluated and wrote (for example Cluster Entropy, the test-time training pipeline and the navsafe benchmark), and which were contributed by co-authors.}
\end{contribution}

Chapters~\ref{ch:occnet} and~\ref{ch:drivelm} addressed the first two stages of the perceive--reason--act loop: how a physical agent can build a universal 3D representation of its surroundings, and how the knowledge of vision-language models can be grounded in driving through structured reasoning. The final and most direct test of a physical agent, however, is acting. The trajectory produced by an end-to-end planner is executed on a public road, so any error in it becomes a potential safety incident. This is also where the curse of reality is felt most acutely. However large its training set, a deployed vehicle keeps meeting rare, safety-critical situations that it has not seen during training, such as an unfamiliar roundabout, a yellow light that forces a choice between rushing and waiting, or a pedestrian who must be yielded to. This problem has been described as the curse of rarity for autonomous vehicles~\cite{liu2024curse}.

In current practice, a learned planner is therefore rarely trusted on its own. It is either wrapped in a \emph{fallback layer}, which checks the planned trajectory for rule violations and replaces it with a pre-defined safe action when necessary, or its decisions are adjusted at test time to minimise a pre-defined \emph{cost function} computed from additional predictions such as road layouts and detected obstacles. Both safeguards are engineered by hand. Fallback layers require considerable domain expertise to design, and cost functions depend on explicit representations that are costly to annotate. Neither can learn and improve with new training data, and both often result in overly conservative behaviour, as the experiments in this chapter confirm. This motivates the first part of the third research question of this thesis (RQ3a): \emph{how can a learned policy stay reliable when the deployment distribution departs from training, in end-to-end driving and without hand-engineered rules, cost functions or extra labels?} Put concretely, can a planner recognise that it is uncertain about the situation in front of it and correct itself at test time, without any labels?

This chapter answers the question with \emph{Centaur} (\textbf{C}luster \textbf{En}tropy for \textbf{T}est-time tr\textbf{A}ining using \textbf{U}nce\textbf{r}tainty), which improves a deployed planner through test-time training (TTT) instead of overriding it. Centaur measures the uncertainty of the planner's own decisions and, before each inference step, applies a single gradient update that reduces this uncertainty, using gradients computed on a small buffer of previously observed frames. Because only past frames are needed, the gradient computation can run asynchronously and in parallel with inference. The chapter makes three contributions. First, to the best of our knowledge, at the time of publication, it was the first work to demonstrate that test-time training can substantially improve end-to-end autonomous driving. Second, it introduces \emph{Cluster Entropy}, a simple and interpretable uncertainty measure for trajectory-scoring planners, which groups candidate trajectories by driving direction and computes the entropy of the resulting five-way distribution of scores. Third, it releases \texttt{navsafe}, a benchmark of 229 safety-critical frames in ten scenario categories, selected following the pre-crash scenario typology of the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA)~\cite{najm2007pre}. All results are obtained in the non-reactive simulation of the NAVSIM benchmark~\cite{dauner2024navsim}, which scores a planned trajectory over a short horizon while the other road users do not react to it. In a single training run evaluated on the \texttt{navtest} split with NAVSIM v1.0, Centaur raises the PDM score (PDMS) of the state-of-the-art planner Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp} from 90.3 to 92.6, close to the 94.8 obtained by human driving trajectories. Averaged over three training seeds, Centaur scores 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 PDMS on the official NAVSIM v1.1 \texttt{navtest} leaderboard, ranking first among the entries submitted with three seeds at the time of the paper's submission (March 2025). On \texttt{navsafe}, it obtains the best overall score of the evaluated planners, raising that of Hydra-MDP from 56.47 to 74.14, although it regresses on the yielding category (from 71.6 to 59.3) and remains far from the 94.65 of human trajectories.

The chapter also builds on its predecessors. NAVSIM builds upon data from OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene}, described in Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}, and nuPlan~\cite{caesar2021nuplan}, and the five direction anchors used by Cluster Entropy follow DriveLM~\cite{sima2024drivelm} (Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}).

The remainder of the chapter is organised as follows. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:intro} explains why today's safeguards are insufficient and why defining the uncertainty of a planner is non-trivial. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:method} presents Centaur, including Cluster Entropy and the test-time training procedure. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe} introduces the \texttt{navsafe} benchmark. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:exp} reports results on \texttt{navtest} and \texttt{navsafe}, and Section~\ref{centaur:sec:analysis} analyses qualitative behaviour, generality, design choices and computational cost. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:discussion} discusses the findings and their limitations, and Section~\ref{centaur:sec:summary} summarises the chapter. Additional related work, methodological details and ablations are given in Appendix~\ref{app:centaur}.

\section{Motivation}\label{centaur:sec:intro}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{ch-centaur/images/main_fig_v15.pdf}
  \caption[Reducing entropy when uncertain with test-time training]{\textbf{Reducing entropy when uncertain with test-time training (TTT).} The end-to-end planner Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp}, state-of-the-art on \texttt{navtest}~\cite{dauner2024navsim}, predicts a score for every trajectory in a fixed set. In the scatter plots, trajectory scores are plotted against their lateral endpoint position and clustered into five categories (coloured arrows in the upper visualisations). The result is a more confident planner (with less entropy) that in this scene prefers `slight right' turns, avoiding a collision.}
  \label{centaur:fig:teaser}
\end{figure}

Safe interaction with other road users and robustness in unforeseen situations are vital as autonomous vehicles increasingly share public streets with human-driven vehicles~\cite{shalevshwartz2017rss,liu2024curse}. There is rising interest in deploying end-to-end planners in autonomous vehicles~\cite{chitta2023transfuser,hu2023uniad,weng2024paradrive,li2024hydramdp}, owing to their scalability and strong empirical performance in challenging scenarios~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey,li2023open}. In practice, however, end-to-end planners often apply fallback layers for real-world deployment, which use pre-defined safe actions to ensure safety in critical situations~\cite{chitta2023transfuser,vitelli2022safetynet,waywe2024ad2}. While useful, fallback layers require considerable domain expertise and manual engineering, do not benefit from data-driven improvements, and constrain the performance potential of the learned algorithm.

Another approach to improving safety optimises the planned output trajectory at test time with expert-crafted cost functions~\cite{hu2023uniad,Hu2023hoplan}. It relies on explicit representations, such as drivable area maps or motion predictions of other vehicles, and assigns numerical costs to unsafe planning behaviours defined with respect to these representations, \eg driving off-road or colliding. Unfortunately, the representations needed for this approach are costly to annotate. They prevent it from fully leveraging the scalability of end-to-end learning, which typically requires only easy-to-obtain annotations such as vehicle trajectories or driving actions~\cite{Prakash2021transfuser,wu2022tcp}. Consequently, end-to-end planners are often not amenable to test-time optimisation with expert-designed cost functions. To deploy and improve end-to-end driving policies safely, we seek a data-driven approach that enhances safety without depending on fallback layers or explicit representations, and that can be scaled effectively.

Centaur achieves this through test-time training~\cite{sun2024learning,liang2025ttasurvey}, which computes a gradient of the network weights to minimise an objective defined on a small dataset collected during deployment~\cite{wang2022cotta,liang2025ttasurvey,Gao2024fstta}. In contrast to test-time trajectory optimisation with explicit representations and expert-designed cost functions, the TTT objective of Centaur optimises the network weights to reduce planning uncertainty, which can be estimated in an unsupervised manner. Furthermore, although Centaur trains at test time, it performs gradient computation and inference asynchronously and in parallel, which keeps the overhead in runtime latency minimal.

Defining a planning uncertainty that enables TTT is, however, non-trivial under the historically prevalent paradigm of trajectory regression in end-to-end driving~\cite{hu2023uniad,chitta2023transfuser,weng2024paradrive,li2024ego}, in which the planner directly regresses the desired trajectory from sensor inputs. Existing work on TTT focuses on classification~\cite{Gao2024fstta,wang2021tent}, where the objective is typically the entropy of the prediction, a quantity that is not easy to compute in a regression setting. An emerging class of end-to-end driving methods based on trajectory scoring~\cite{li2024hydramdp,Sadat2020P3,Casas2021mp3} instead assigns numerical scores to multiple trajectory candidates, much like a classifier. Focusing on this class, Centaur introduces Cluster Entropy, which aggregates the candidate trajectory scores of the planner into clusters based on driving direction and computes the entropy of this simple and interpretable distribution.

Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:teaser} illustrates the approach with Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp}, a trajectory-scoring planner. In the complex scene shown, the vehicle is entering a roundabout. After TTT, the distribution of high-scoring trajectories becomes more concentrated on the safer behaviour of a right turn, which helps the planner avoid illegally turning left into oncoming traffic. Besides its use for TTT, Cluster Entropy can also flag situations in which the model is likely to fail: at a fixed threshold, it gives the highest true-positive rate among the compared uncertainty measures. This suggests its potential as a mechanism for alerting human drivers to intervene, although the evidence is limited (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:failure}).

Finally, as planners draw closer to human-level performance on existing benchmarks, a single aggregate score says little about where they still fail. To advance safe autonomous driving, we therefore construct \texttt{navsafe}, a benchmark of edge cases and challenging driving scenarios, with a semi-automated data curation pipeline that involves human annotation and verification to sample safety-critical scenarios. Evaluating state-of-the-art planners, including Centaur, on \texttt{navsafe} reveals new insights into their strengths and weaknesses, as well as a substantial gap to human-level performance.

\section{Related Work}\label{centaur:sec:related}

\paragraph{End-to-end autonomous driving.} End-to-end autonomous driving represents a paradigm shift in autonomous vehicle technology~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey}. Its goal is to map raw sensor inputs directly to vehicle control outputs with neural networks, bypassing traditional modular components such as separate perception, planning and control systems~\cite{Prakash2021transfuser,chitta2021neat,jaeger2023hidden,zimmerlin2024tf,chen2024vadv2,jiang2023vad,hu2023uniad,hu2022model,jia2023think,wang2023drivedreamer,jia2023driveadapter,li2024hydramdp,liao2025diffusiondrive}, including the occupancy-based approach of Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}~\cite{sima2023occnet}.

\paragraph{End-to-end planner deployment.} Obtaining trustworthy outputs from an end-to-end system during deployment is challenging, given its ``black box'' nature. Rule-based planners and fallback layers are common ways to guardrail the planning output. For instance, UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad} uses test-time optimisation to refine its planning output, TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser} adopts forced braking when the LiDAR sensor encounters obstacles, and SafetyNet~\cite{vitelli2022safetynet} adopts fallback layers that ensure planning safety via sanity checks on collision and physical feasibility. However, unlike Centaur, these approaches either require heavy annotation during training or result in overly conservative actions.

\paragraph{Test-time compute and training.} Leveraging test-time compute has recently emerged as an important way to strengthen reasoning capabilities~\cite{openaio1}. In addition, test-time adaptation and training techniques~\cite{liang2025ttasurvey} have shown considerable potential in computer vision~\cite{sun2024learning,hakim2023clust3,marsden2024universal}, multimodal learning~\cite{shu2022test,yoon2024c}, vision-language navigation~\cite{Gao2024fstta}, trajectory prediction~\cite{park2024t4p}, robot manipulation~\cite{hansen2021pad} and long-context large language models~\cite{sun2024learning}. The key idea of these approaches is to minimise entropy or related proxies at test time. Centaur is aligned in spirit with them and was, to the best of our knowledge, at the time of publication, the first work to explore the potential of test-time training via uncertainty measurement for the vehicle motion planning task.

Further related work on Hydra-MDP and ensembling, semantic entropy, safety-critical scenario benchmarks and evidential uncertainty is discussed in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:related}.

\subsection{Preliminaries}\label{centaur:sec:prelim}

End-to-end self-driving methods seek to obtain a policy $\pi$ that directly outputs driving decisions, such as desired trajectories $\mathbf{t}$, based on sensor inputs $\mathbf{x}$ and a navigation command $\mathbf{c}$. Trajectories take the form $\tilde{\mathbf{t}} = (\mathbf{p}_0, \mathbf{p}_1, \dots, \mathbf{p}_N)$, a sequence of waypoints, which are Bird's Eye View (BEV) positions of the vehicle over $N$ evenly spaced time steps. The policy estimates trajectories from sensor inputs $\mathbf{x}_0$ (such as camera images) and a discrete navigation command $\mathbf{c}_0$ (indicating the driver's intention in ambiguous situations, \eg left, right or straight), both captured at the current time step, indexed with $0$. Among the existing paradigms for end-to-end driving, the straightforward approach of regressing trajectories using a dataset of human driving demonstrations is referred to as \emph{trajectory regression}. Although Centaur is compatible with trajectory regression, this chapter primarily addresses its application to an emerging paradigm with higher performance in the recent literature: \emph{trajectory scoring} (\eg Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp}, VADv2~\cite{chen2024vadv2} and RAD~\cite{gao2025rad}). The methodology for applying Centaur to trajectory regression methods such as UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad} and TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser} is described in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:regression}, and the corresponding results with TransFuser are reported in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:ablations}.

\paragraph{Trajectory scoring.} In this formulation, the planner outputs a set of scalar \emph{score features} $\{s_j\}_{j=1}^k$ for a \emph{planning vocabulary} of $k$ different trajectories. The score features numerically capture different aspects of desired driving behaviour, such as avoiding collisions and making progress. They can be aggregated into a single final score via an aggregation function $\phi$. During inference, the trajectory with the largest predicted final score is selected:
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:multi-score-output}
  \{s_j\}_{j=1}^k = \{ \pi_\theta(\mathbf{t}_j, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{c}_0) \}_{j=1}^k,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:cost-select}
  \hat{\mathbf{t}}_0 = \argmax_{\mathbf{t}_j} \{\phi(s_j)\}_{j=1}^k.
\end{equation}
As a result of this design, the planner is inherently multi-modal, and the distribution of scores over the planning vocabulary provides a way to represent planning uncertainty. For this type of planner, learning the policy parameters $\theta$ can be formulated as
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:objective-score}
  \argmin_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})\sim D_{kd}} \left[\mathcal{L}_{kd}\big(e(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c}), \pi_\theta(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})\big)\right],
\end{equation}
where $e$ is an expert algorithm that can provide ground-truth score features for any sampled trajectory, and $D_{kd} = \{(\mathbf{t}, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{c}), \dots\}$ is a knowledge distillation dataset generated by sampling this expert algorithm for different inputs, typically in a driving simulator~\cite{caesar2021nuplan,dosovitskiy2017carla} or a world model~\cite{yang2024genad,gao2024vista}. In practice, the score features are normalised to the range $(0,1)$, and $\mathcal{L}_{kd}$ is implemented as a cross-entropy loss. The complete training loss of the Hydra-MDP implementation used in this chapter, which adds an imitation term, is given in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:scoring}.

\subsection{Cluster Entropy}\label{centaur:sec:ce}

In essence, the scoring procedure of Eqs.~\eqref{centaur:eq:multi-score-output} and~\eqref{centaur:eq:cost-select} acts as a classifier over the $k$ classes of the planning vocabulary. We formulate a new uncertainty measure, Cluster Entropy, to translate this intuition into an unsupervised training objective for end-to-end planning, which, unlike Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:objective-score}, does not require an expert algorithm. The key challenge is that the planning vocabulary is often very large, typically encompassing several thousand possible trajectories. Even in simple driving scenarios, many neighbouring trajectories in the vocabulary therefore receive nearly identical scores. Simply minimising the entropy of the distribution of scores over the vocabulary would yield an objective that encourages the model to avoid trajectories with a larger number of nearby neighbours in the vocabulary, \eg driving straight. Such a naive entropy is therefore unsuitable for uncertainty estimation in this setting. Cluster Entropy instead proceeds in three steps.

\paragraph{Trajectory candidates.} First, we sample a set of $M$ trajectory candidates $\{\mathbf{t}_m\}_{m=1}^M$ from the planning vocabulary, where $M \ll k$. The sampling is weighted to prioritise candidates that have a high average score according to the expert algorithm $e$; the sampling weights are specified in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:entropy}. The entropy over this reduced set of $M$ scores already serves as a meaningful uncertainty measure, as the Full Entropy baseline in the experiments shows, but the next two steps further improve its effectiveness and interpretability.

\paragraph{Direction-based sampling.} Next, we use domain-specific criteria to select trajectory anchors from the $M$ candidates. Following DriveLM~\cite{sima2024drivelm}, we select five anchors: \texttt{sharp left}, \texttt{slight left}, \texttt{forward}, \texttt{slight right} and \texttt{sharp right}. The \texttt{sharp left} and \texttt{sharp right} anchors, the candidates with the largest lateral offsets, are selected first. The \texttt{slight left} and \texttt{slight right} anchors are the candidates whose lateral offsets are closest to half of those of \texttt{sharp left} and \texttt{sharp right}, and \texttt{forward} is the candidate with the smallest lateral offset.

\paragraph{Score aggregation.} As shown in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:pipeline} (top), we aim to construct a five-way categorical distribution that captures how the predicted scores are distributed over the five driving directions. For this, we form five clusters $\mathcal{C}_1, \dots, \mathcal{C}_5$ by assigning each of the $M$ candidates to its nearest anchor in terms of $L_2$ distance. Within each cluster $\mathcal{C}_a$, the predicted scores of all its trajectories are summed to obtain an anchor score $\mathit{score}_a$. Normalising the anchor scores yields a probability distribution, and Cluster Entropy, denoted $H$, is defined as the Shannon entropy of this distribution:
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:ce}
  p_a = \frac{\mathit{score}_a}{\sum_{a'=1}^{5} \mathit{score}_{a'}}, \qquad
  H(\mathbf{x}) = -\sum_{a=1}^{5} p_a \log p_a .
\end{equation}
Since the distribution has five outcomes, $H$ ranges from $0$, when all the score mass falls into a single driving direction, to $\ln 5$, when it is spread uniformly over the five directions.

\subsection{Test-Time Training}\label{centaur:sec:ttt}

Equipped with this unsupervised training objective, our goal is to minimise uncertainty at test time, which is the key idea of TTT approaches in the literature. We follow the standard practice of performing a single step of gradient descent to update the network parameters $\theta$~\cite{Gao2024fstta}, using the Cluster Entropy $H(\mathbf{x})$ as the objective function. Specifically, given $H(\mathbf{x})$ calculated after a forward pass through $\pi_\theta$, we obtain the gradient $\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}$ by backpropagation. Instead of directly updating $\theta$ with this gradient, however, we store $\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}$ in a buffer of length $F=4$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:pipeline} (bottom). At time step $i$, we update the network using the average $\{\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}\}_{avg}$ of all gradients currently in the buffer, obtaining
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:ttt}
  \hat{\theta}_i = \theta - \eta \left\{\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}\right\}_{avg},
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the learning rate. Intuitively, this update suppresses the ability of the model to assign high scores in multiple clusters. This, in turn, corrects the scores of outlier predictions that differ significantly from the other trajectories in their cluster, such as the high-scoring `slight left' outlier in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:teaser}. Algorithm~\ref{centaur:alg:ttt} summarises the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \DontPrintSemicolon
  \caption{Test-time training in Centaur}\label{centaur:alg:ttt}
  \KwIn{trained trajectory-scoring planner $\pi_\theta$, where $\theta$ are the score-decoder parameters (perception backbone frozen); planning vocabulary $\{\mathbf{t}_j\}_{j=1}^{k}$; $M$ trajectory candidates and their five direction anchors (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ce}); buffer length $F$; learning rate $\eta$}
  \KwOut{a planned trajectory $\hat{\mathbf{t}}_0$ at every time step}
  $\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \emptyset$ \tcp*{buffer of historical gradients}
  \ForEach{time step $i$, with current sensor inputs $\mathbf{x}_0$ and command $\mathbf{c}_0$}{
    \eIf{$\mathcal{B} \neq \emptyset$}{
      $\hat{\theta}_i \leftarrow \theta - \eta \left\{\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}\right\}_{avg}$, averaging the gradients in $\mathcal{B}$ \tcp*{Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ttt}}
    }{
      $\hat{\theta}_i \leftarrow \theta$\;
    }
    Score the vocabulary with $\pi_{\hat{\theta}_i}$ and output $\hat{\mathbf{t}}_0$ \tcp*{Eqs.~\eqref{centaur:eq:multi-score-output}--\eqref{centaur:eq:cost-select}}
    \tcp{may run asynchronously on separate hardware}
    Compute the Cluster Entropy $H(\mathbf{x}_0)$ from the predicted scores of the $M$ candidates \tcp*{Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ce}}
    Obtain $\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}$ by backpropagation and push it into $\mathcal{B}$, keeping only the $F$ most recent gradients\;
  }
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Deployment.} Note that the update at time step $i$ only uses gradients of \emph{historical time steps} that are already present in the buffer. Therefore, beyond the smoothness provided by gradient averaging, the buffer enables asynchronous and parallel execution. During deployment, the gradient calculation can be executed on independent computational hardware, so that the more critical inference process incurs minimal latency overhead. The latency measured in the experiments, which currently includes the gradient calculation, is analysed in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:latency}.

\section{\texorpdfstring{\texttt{navsafe}}{navsafe}: A Safety-Critical Scenario Set}\label{centaur:sec:navsafe}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ch-centaur/images/navsafe_v12.pdf}
  \caption[The \texttt{navsafe} benchmark]{\textbf{\texttt{navsafe}.} Definitions of safety-critical cases from NHTSA (National Highway Traffic Safety Administration) material~\cite{najm2007pre} are used to search \texttt{navtest} for matching frames. Following human checks, these frames form \texttt{navsafe}. CLIP-based clustering~\cite{ilharco2021openclip} of \texttt{navsafe} alongside \texttt{navtest} shows that \texttt{navsafe} consists of frames from the peripheral regions of the distribution.}
  \label{centaur:fig:navsafe}
\end{figure}

This section describes the construction and highlights of \texttt{navsafe}, a challenging new benchmark consisting of edge cases for end-to-end planning. The data used in the benchmark is sampled from NAVSIM~\cite{dauner2024navsim}, which in turn builds upon data from OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene} (Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}) and nuPlan~\cite{caesar2021nuplan}.

\paragraph{NAVSIM.} The framework on which \texttt{navsafe} is built is primarily designed to capture scenarios with significant changes in driving behaviour, where the historical trajectory of the ego vehicle does not allow for an accurate extrapolation of the future plan. It is split into two parts, \texttt{navtrain} and \texttt{navtest}, which contain 1,192 logs (103,288 frames) and 136 logs (12,146 frames), respectively. In NAVSIM, driving agents are required to plan a trajectory as a sequence of future positions spanning a horizon of 4 seconds. The input to an agent includes 1.5 seconds of past history (resulting in 4 frames of data) from onboard camera and LiDAR sensors, as well as the vehicle's current speed, acceleration and navigation command, collectively referred to as the ego status. We retain these default NAVSIM settings for evaluation on \texttt{navsafe}.

\paragraph{Construction criteria.} Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:navsafe} illustrates the data curation process for \texttt{navsafe}. The US NHTSA holds a collection of traffic accidents reported every year across the nation, which is shared publicly via a pre-crash scenario typology for crash avoidance research~\cite{najm2007pre}. It includes a wide variety of pre-crash scenario types, ranging from common ones such as ``Lead Vehicle Stopped'' to uncommon ones such as ``Animal Crash With Prior Vehicle Maneuver''. Supported by summary statistics on the types of pre-crash scenarios~\cite{thorn2018framework,najm2007pre}, \texttt{navsafe} is curated to highlight the safety-critical scenarios in which modern end-to-end planners could fail. We construct it with the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}[label={Step~\arabic*.}, start=0, leftmargin=*]
  \item Sort the video clips in the \texttt{navtest} split by the average performance of a state-of-the-art planner~\cite{li2024hydramdp}.
  \item Check the video clips with human annotators and try to assign frames to new or existing categories in \texttt{navsafe}.
  \item Populate the scenario types with new examples.
  \item Retrieve scenario type labels from the NHTSA file to define scenario types not yet in \texttt{navsafe}.
  \item Re-sort \texttt{navtest} and start a new cycle of the workflow.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Highlights of \texttt{navsafe}.} For the benchmark, we select 229 of the 12,146 frames of \texttt{navtest}, covering 10 categories: ``Roundabout'', ``Yellow light, rush or wait?'', ``Exit ramp'', ``Unprotected left'', ``Enter ramp'', ``Uncommon traffic sign'', ``Overtaking with lane change'', ``No lane area (\eg parking lot)'', ``Bad weather'' and ``Yielding (\eg to pedestrians)''. By providing independent scores for each scenario type, \texttt{navsafe} enables a fine-grained assessment of specific planning capabilities, unlike the overall aggregate performance provided by existing benchmarks such as \texttt{navtest}. The CLIP-based clustering in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:navsafe} shows that \texttt{navsafe} represents data points far from the centre of the distribution of the original \texttt{navtest} split. In the terms of this thesis, \texttt{navsafe} thus isolates the tail of the test distribution, where the curse of reality is expected to bite. In the experiments, we benchmark the performance of the human agent (ground-truth trajectories from NAVSIM) as well as state-of-the-art end-to-end planners to demonstrate the increased level of difficulty posed by this new benchmark.

\section{Experiments}\label{centaur:sec:exp}

This section evaluates Centaur on the NAVSIM framework~\cite{dauner2024navsim}, including the official \texttt{navtest} leaderboard and the \texttt{navsafe} scenarios of Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{centaur:sec:setup}

\paragraph{Metrics.} The experiments adopt the primary metric of NAVSIM, the PDM score (PDMS), to evaluate end-to-end planning models. NAVSIM v1 computes it in non-reactive simulation, \ie by unrolling the planned trajectory over the 4-second horizon while the other road users do not react to it:
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:pdms}
  \mathrm{PDMS} = \mathrm{NC} \times \mathrm{DAC} \times \left( \frac{5\,\mathrm{TTC} + 2\,\mathrm{C} + 5\,\mathrm{EP}}{12} \right),
\end{equation}
where the sub-scores NC (No at-fault Collision), DAC (Drivable Area Compliance), EP (Ego Progress), C (Comfort) and TTC (Time-to-Collision), each represented as a percentage, are combined into a single score. A detailed definition of each sub-score can be found in~\cite{dauner2024navsim}. Owing to its relevance for safety, several experiments discuss TTC in particular. TTC identifies whether the output trajectory of the planner results in near-collisions, \ie whether a forward projection at constant velocity would result in a collision within 1 second at any point along the trajectory.

\paragraph{Base model.} Designed specifically for NAVSIM, our base model Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp} takes LiDAR points as input, which are splatted onto the BEV plane and encoded by a ResNet34~\cite{he2016deep}. These features are fused with image features extracted by a \mbox{V2-99}~\cite{park2021pseudo} backbone via the sensor fusion modules of TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser}. The decoder then predicts scores for a planning vocabulary of $k=8192$ trajectories. The score functions output by the decoder are the five sub-scores (NC, DAC, EP, C and TTC) considered in the NAVSIM evaluation. Additional experiments with the more lightweight TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser}, which performs trajectory regression instead of scoring, are provided in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:ablations} and summarised in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ablations}.

\paragraph{Deployment strategy.} As a baseline for safer deployment, we implement a fallback layer following the description of SafetyNet~\cite{vitelli2022safetynet}. We first calculate the average PDMS of each trajectory in the planning vocabulary of Hydra-MDP and select the top 20 as fallback trajectories. During inference, we measure the $L_2$ distance between the predicted trajectory and the 20 fallback trajectories. If a failure is detected, based on an uncertainty value above a threshold, the closest of these 20 fallback trajectories replaces the predicted one as the safe action. The chosen thresholds are discussed with Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class}.

\paragraph{Uncertainty measures.} We compare Cluster Entropy with three baselines. For \textbf{KL Divergence}, we obtain the predicted categorical distribution over the planning vocabulary ($k=8192$ classes) for each score function (NC, DAC, EP, C and TTC), then calculate the KL divergence between each pair of distributions and sum over all pairs. \textbf{Full Entropy}~\cite{shannon1948entropy} is calculated like Cluster Entropy, but skips the clustering of the $M=100$ candidates into five clusters and simply computes the entropy of the full 100-way candidate score distribution. \textbf{Semantic Entropy} is our adaptation of semantic uncertainty~\cite{kuhn2023semantic} to autonomous driving. Its calculation is again similar to that of Cluster Entropy, but the candidates are clustered in a feature space in which each trajectory is represented by its predicted scores $s_j$ (details in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:entropy}). For compactness, TTT for Hydra-MDP with Semantic Entropy, the baseline used most frequently in the experiments, is referred to as \textbf{Hydra-SE}.

\paragraph{Implementation.} Except for Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all}, we adopt NAVSIM v1.0 for all training and local evaluations, and NAVSIM v1.1 for the official leaderboard submissions; the two versions differ slightly owing to minor changes in metric definitions. The NAVSIM v1.0 comparisons (Tables~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation} and~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}) are single training runs, whereas the leaderboard entries in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all} are means over three seeds. We train the base end-to-end model Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp} on the \texttt{navtrain} split for 20 epochs on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a total batch size of 256. The learning rate of AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2017decoupled} is set to $10^{-4}$, with weight decay disabled. For Cluster Entropy and TTT, we use $M=100$ candidate trajectories and perform one step of gradient descent with a learning rate $\eta$ of $10^{-4}$ on a dataset of $F=4$ history frames. Only the score decoder is updated during TTT; the perception backbones are kept frozen.

Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation} shows the main results, examining two key aspects: the deployment strategy and the uncertainty measure.

\paragraph{Deployment strategy.} Fallback layers notably decrease the PDMS, irrespective of the uncertainty measure employed. This decline can be primarily attributed to a considerable reduction in ego progress, from 86.5 to below 15. Unfortunately, the more failure cases the uncertainty measure identifies, the worse the PDMS with a fallback layer becomes, owing to the conservative fallback trajectories. Although the safety-related sub-scores (NC, DAC and TTC) improve, this approach would likely lead to an over-reliance on human intervention and be infeasible in practice. In contrast, TTT improves the safety-related sub-scores while keeping progress high, although EP still falls slightly, from 86.5 for Hydra-MDP to 85.9 for Centaur. The largest increase is observed in TTC (from 93.9 for Hydra-MDP to 98.0 for Centaur), highlighting the potential of TTT for improved safety.

\paragraph{Uncertainty measure.} In this single-run comparison, Centaur, which uses TTT with Cluster Entropy, improves by 2.3 PDMS over the prior state-of-the-art Hydra-MDP and by 0.6 PDMS over the Semantic Entropy baseline Hydra-SE. All four uncertainty measures improve the PDMS of Hydra-MDP when used for TTT, from 91.5 with KL Divergence and 91.8 with Full Entropy and Semantic Entropy up to 92.6 with Cluster Entropy. In this experiment, we perform binary classification of potential failure cases using the different uncertainty estimates. This task is of critical relevance for vehicles deployed with partial autonomy, which rely on human intervention in challenging situations. We define a failure as a scenario in which the model obtains a PDMS of 0, and consider an uncertainty above a threshold to indicate a possible failure. Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class} reports the true positive rate (TPR) and accuracy of identifying such failures. Here, we select a threshold of $0.8 = 0.5\ln 5$, which is half the entropy of a uniform distribution over 5 classes. At this fixed threshold, Cluster Entropy gives the highest TPR among the compared measures (62.8\%), as well as the highest accuracy (73.6\%). This evidence is limited, however. Failures are rare (the Select All baseline shows that 15.6\% of frames are failures), so the accuracy of every measure, including Cluster Entropy, is below the 84.4\% of the trivial Select None baseline, which never flags a failure. Moreover, Cluster Entropy is evaluated at a single threshold, and threshold-free metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) were not reported, so the table does not show that Cluster Entropy separates failures from successes better at every operating point. Based on these findings, the remaining experiments focus on the two measures with the highest TPR and accuracy, Semantic Entropy and Cluster Entropy.

Interestingly, when TTT is used, the PDMS is a reliable indicator of the quality of an uncertainty measure, but not when a fallback layer is used. For example, the measure with the highest TPR in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class}, Cluster Entropy, also obtains the best PDMS with TTT in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}, but it triggers the fallback mechanism most often, which leads to a lower PDMS with a fallback layer than Semantic Entropy. The effect of the threshold for other planners and uncertainty measures is examined in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:ablations}.

\subsection{Generality and Design Choices}\label{centaur:sec:ablations}

\paragraph{Trajectory regression planners.} Test-time training is not restricted to trajectory scoring. Equipping the regression heads of TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser} with evidential uncertainty (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:regression}) and applying TTT with Semantic Entropy yields TransFuser-SE, which raises the PDMS of TransFuser from 83.4 to 85.7 on NAVSIM v1.0 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}). This is the same gain of 2.3 PDMS that Centaur obtains over Hydra-MDP. TTT with evidential uncertainty reaches 84.2 PDMS, whereas fallback layers again reduce ego progress to below 15 and the PDMS to around 60 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}).

\paragraph{Test-time optimisation with explicit representations.} The NAVSIM v1.0 comparison in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp} also quantifies the cost of the hand-designed safeguards discussed in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:intro}. UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad}, which refines its plan through optimisation with an expert-designed cost function, drops from 83.4 to 75.2 PDMS with this optimisation step, owing to a reduction in ego progress from 78.8 to 71.9, much like the findings with a fallback layer in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}. Similarly, incorporating an explicit 3D occupancy head into PARA-Drive~\cite{weng2024paradrive} slightly reduces its PDMS from 84.0 to 83.6. These results show the need for an alternative means of ensuring safety during deployment, such as TTT.

\paragraph{Combining historical gradients.} Following~\cite{Gao2024fstta}, the historical gradients could alternatively be combined via a singular-value decomposition (SVD) that selects the most useful ones for updating the model. On TransFuser-SE, this variant lowers the PDMS from 85.7 to 83.8 compared with simple averaging (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}).

\paragraph{Buffer length.} The number of history frames $F$ has little effect: Hydra-SE obtains 91.4, 91.3, 91.5 and 91.8 PDMS for $F=1$, $2$, $3$ and $4$, respectively (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}). Performance increases slightly with longer buffers, with $F=2$ being the lowest, and the consistency across all settings indicates the robustness of the approach.

\paragraph{Image backbone.} Replacing the \mbox{V2-99} image backbone of Hydra-MDP with ViT-L gives slightly lower performance overall, \eg 89.9 \vs 90.3 PDMS without TTT and 90.9 \vs 91.5 with TTT and KL Divergence. With a fallback layer and KL Divergence, however, the ViT-L variant is around 4 PDMS better (67.4 \vs 63.1; Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}).

\subsection{Computational Cost}\label{centaur:sec:latency}

Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp} reports the amortised latency per frame on \texttt{navtest}, measured on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. The base Hydra-MDP takes 243.9\,ms, compared with 103.1\,ms for TransFuser, whose efficient ResNet-34 backbone comes at a cost of around 7 PDMS. In the current implementation, the latency of TTT includes the gradient calculation and the model update: with TTT and Semantic Entropy, Hydra-MDP takes 312.5\,ms, and 357.1\,ms with KL Divergence. This overhead could be reduced by computing the gradients in parallel with inference, as described in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ttt}, or by applying TTT only on frames whose uncertainty exceeds a threshold, which lowers the latency of Hydra-SE to 279.6\,ms at 91.3 PDMS.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{centaur:sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Self-correction instead of guardrails.} The experiments consistently show that safeguards which override or re-optimise a learned planner trade progress for safety. Fallback layers raise NC, DAC and TTC but reduce ego progress from 86.5 to below 15 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}), and the cost-function optimisation of UniAD reduces both ego progress and PDMS (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}). Such behaviour would likely lead to an over-reliance on human intervention. TTT instead adjusts the planner itself: the safety-related sub-scores improve, most notably TTC, while progress is nearly maintained (EP falls from 86.5 to 85.9 on \texttt{navtest}). Because the objective is the planner's own uncertainty, no rules, cost functions or labels are needed at test time, which is the requirement posed by RQ3a. In absolute terms, the benefit is also larger on the frames most exposed to the curse of reality: on \texttt{navsafe}, whose frames lie in the periphery of the \texttt{navtest} distribution, Centaur improves on Hydra-MDP by 17.67 points in the reported Overall score, compared with 2.3 points in the single-run \texttt{navtest} comparison, where scores are already close to those of human trajectories. TTT is not free of trade-offs, however: on \texttt{navsafe}, Centaur regresses on yielding, from 71.6 to 59.3.

\paragraph{How much of the gain is due to Cluster Entropy?} The headline improvement from 90.3 to 92.6 PDMS comes from a single training run evaluated with NAVSIM v1.0 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}). Over three seeds on the NAVSIM v1.1 leaderboard, Centaur leads Hydra-SE, its own Semantic Entropy variant, by only 0.23 PDMS (92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 \vs 91.87 $\pm$ 0.19; Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all}), less than Centaur's own standard deviation across seeds, and no significance test was run. Moreover, all four uncertainty measures used for TTT in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}, three entropies and a KL divergence, improve Hydra-MDP by between 1.2 PDMS (KL Divergence) and 2.3 PDMS (Cluster Entropy). This suggests that much of the benefit comes from minimising an entropy-style uncertainty at test time in general, rather than from the specific form of Cluster Entropy, whose distinctive advantage is better supported for interpretability (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:qualitative}) than for accuracy.

\paragraph{Interpretability and failure detection.} Because its five clusters correspond to driving directions, Cluster Entropy makes it possible to inspect which alternatives the planner is torn between (Figs.~\ref{centaur:fig:teaser} and~\ref{centaur:fig:vis}). At a fixed threshold, the same quantity also gives the highest true-positive rate among the compared measures for flagging failure frames (62.8\%, Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class}), which suggests its use for alerting a human driver to intervene in vehicles deployed with partial autonomy. This evidence is weak, however: because failures are rare, its accuracy of 73.6\% is below the 84.4\% of the trivial Select None baseline, and threshold-free metrics such as AUROC were not reported.

\paragraph{Limitations.} As an early attempt, Centaur shows promising performance, but it has several limitations. First, it is evaluated only in the non-reactive simulation of NAVSIM v1, which scores planned trajectories with principled, rule-based metrics but does not let other road users react; it has not been tested in reactive closed-loop simulation or on a vehicle. NAVSIM has since been extended to version~2, which is based on pseudo-simulation~\cite{cao2025pseudo}; Centaur has not been evaluated under this protocol. Extending it to a closed-loop setting with an affordable inference budget is a promising direction to explore. Second, test-time compute is quite limited in autonomous driving, which is an efficiency-sensitive task. The measured latency of TTT currently includes the gradient calculation (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:latency}), and it is worth exploring ways of unifying TTT and model inference to minimise the compute overhead. Third, because the objective only rewards confidence, TTT can correct outlier scores but cannot suppress a confident original prediction, as the failure case in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:vis} shows. Relatedly, Centaur is the best of the evaluated planners overall on \texttt{navsafe}, but not in every category. On YLD it regresses from the 71.6 of Hydra-MDP to 59.3, below both Hydra-SE (84.6) and TransFuser (61.6), a regression that matters for pedestrians and other vulnerable road users. It also trails Hydra-SE (64.4) and LTF (62.8) on BWTH (60.2), and LTF on YLLT (65.0 \vs 70.2). On \texttt{navtest}, its ego progress is slightly lower than that of Hydra-MDP (85.9 \vs 86.5). Fourth, \texttt{navsafe} is small: five of its ten categories contain between 5 and 12 frames, so per-category scores should be interpreted with care. No confidence intervals are reported, and because the aggregation behind the Overall score is not specified, that score cannot be recomputed from the category scores (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navsafe}). Finally, the formulation requires a planner whose output exposes a distribution over trajectories, either through trajectory scoring or through an uncertainty-aware regression head (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:regression}), and only the score decoder is adapted while the perception backbone stays frozen. Test-time self-correction therefore currently acts on the action stage alone; whether perception and reasoning modules can be corrected in the same way is part of the broader architectural question (RQ4) addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{centaur:sec:summary}

This chapter addressed the first part of the third research question (RQ3a): how an end-to-end driving policy can stay reliable when the deployment distribution departs from training, without hand-engineered rules, cost functions or extra labels. We proposed Centaur, which uses test-time training to let a deployed trajectory-scoring planner correct itself. Its objective is Cluster Entropy, an unsupervised and interpretable uncertainty measure that aggregates the predicted scores of sampled candidate trajectories over five driving directions and computes the entropy of this distribution. Before each inference step, Centaur takes a single gradient step on the score decoder, using gradients averaged over a buffer of the four most recent frames, so that gradient computation can run asynchronously to inference. We also released \texttt{navsafe}, 229 safety-critical \texttt{navtest} frames in ten NHTSA-inspired categories that lie in the periphery of the test distribution.

In a single training run on \texttt{navtest} with NAVSIM v1.0, Centaur raises the PDMS of Hydra-MDP from 90.3 to 92.6, with the largest sub-score gain in time-to-collision (from 93.9 to 98.0) and a slight loss in ego progress (from 86.5 to 85.9), whereas SafetyNet-style fallback layers reduce the PDMS to between 63.1 and 65.9 because ego progress falls below 15. With a mean PDMS of 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 over three seeds, Centaur ranked first among the three-seed entries on the official NAVSIM v1.1 \texttt{navtest} leaderboard at the time of the paper's submission (March 2025), without ensembling and without tuning training hyper-parameters beyond the Hydra-MDP defaults. Its lead over its Semantic Entropy variant Hydra-SE is, however, only 0.23 PDMS, and every uncertainty measure used for TTT on Hydra-MDP gains between 1.2 and 2.3 PDMS. At a fixed threshold, Cluster Entropy also gives the highest true-positive rate among the compared measures for flagging failures (62.8\%), although its accuracy (73.6\%) is below that of the trivial Select None baseline (84.4\%). On \texttt{navsafe}, Centaur obtains the best overall score of the evaluated planners, improving it from 56.47 to 74.14, but it regresses on yielding (from 71.6 to 59.3) and remains far from the 94.65 of human trajectories. TTT also transfers to the regression planner TransFuser (+2.3 PDMS with Semantic Entropy), while the expert-designed cost optimisation of UniAD lowers its PDMS from 83.4 to 75.2. The main limitations are the evaluation in non-reactive simulation only, the added test-time compute, the small margin over other entropy-style objectives, and the inability of entropy minimisation to overturn a confident but wrong prediction.

Driving policies thus benefit from test-time self-correction, at least in non-reactive simulation: a planner that can measure its own uncertainty can turn it into a training signal after deployment. In robot manipulation, however, the gap between training and deployment is even wider. Human demonstrations are inconsistent, data and compute are limited, and hardware behaves differently at deployment than during data collection. Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} tackles this setting.
