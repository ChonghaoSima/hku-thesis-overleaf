\chapter{Act I: Robust End-to-End Driving via Test-Time Training}\label{ch:centaur}
\begin{contribution}
This chapter is based on the preprint ``Centaur: Robust End-to-End Autonomous Driving with Test-Time Training''~\cite{sima2024centaur}, of which the author is an equal-contribution first author with Kashyap Chitta and the primary contact. The work was carried out during the author's internship at NVIDIA Autonomous Vehicle Applied Research. The author proposed applying test-time training to end-to-end planning; designed Cluster Entropy; implemented Centaur on Hydra-MDP and conducted the NAVSIM experiments; designed and built the \texttt{navsafe} benchmark, including the scenario selection and its human verification; and conducted the analyses of failure detection, the ablations and the visualisations. The writing of the paper was led by co-authors.
\end{contribution}

Chapters~\ref{ch:occnet} and~\ref{ch:drivelm} addressed the first two stages of the perceive--reason--act loop: how a physical agent can build a geometry-open 3D representation of its surroundings, and how the knowledge of vision-language models can be grounded in driving through structured reasoning. The final and most direct test of a physical agent, however, is acting. The trajectory produced by an end-to-end planner is executed on a public road, so any error in it becomes a potential safety incident. This is also where the curse of reality is felt most acutely. However large its training set, a deployed vehicle keeps meeting rare, safety-critical situations that it has not seen during training, such as an unfamiliar roundabout, a yellow light that forces a choice between rushing and waiting, or a pedestrian who must be yielded to. This problem has been described as the curse of rarity for autonomous vehicles~\cite{liu2024curse}.

In current practice, a learned planner is therefore rarely trusted on its own. It is either wrapped in a \emph{fallback layer}, which checks the planned trajectory for rule violations and replaces it with a pre-defined safe action when necessary, or its decisions are adjusted at test time to minimise a pre-defined \emph{cost function} computed from additional predictions such as road layouts and detected obstacles. Both safeguards are engineered by hand. Fallback layers require considerable domain expertise to design, and cost functions depend on explicit representations that are costly to annotate. Neither can learn and improve with new training data, and both can make a vehicle overly conservative. The experiments in this chapter do not evaluate such safeguards in general: they compare test-time adaptation with one uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories that we implemented, and with the cost optimisation of UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad}. This motivates the first part of the third research question of this thesis (RQ3a): \emph{how can a learned policy stay reliable when the deployment distribution departs from training, in end-to-end driving and without hand-engineered rules, cost functions or extra labels?} Put concretely, can a planner recognise that it is uncertain about the situation in front of it and correct itself at test time, without any labels? We answer a restricted form of this question. It concerns per-frame decisions on logged states, because the evaluation never lets the planner's errors compound (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:setup}), and it avoids rules at test time only, because the planner that we adapt distils rule-based scores during training (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:prelim}).

This chapter answers the question with \emph{Centaur} (\textbf{C}luster \textbf{En}tropy for \textbf{T}est-time tr\textbf{A}ining using \textbf{U}nce\textbf{r}tainty), which adapts a trained planner through test-time training (TTT) instead of overriding it. Centaur measures the uncertainty of the planner's own decisions and, before each inference step, applies a single gradient update that reduces this uncertainty, using gradients computed on a small buffer of previously observed frames. Because only past frames are needed, the gradient computation could run asynchronously and in parallel with inference; in our implementation it runs sequentially, and its cost is included in the measured latency. The chapter makes three contributions. First, to our knowledge, Centaur was the first application of test-time training to an end-to-end planner on NAVSIM at the time of publication (March 2025). Earlier test-time training in driving adapted trajectory predictors rather than planners~\cite{park2024t4p}, and test-time search changes the output trajectory rather than the network weights~\cite{yang2024diffusiones}. Second, it introduces \emph{Cluster Entropy}, a simple and interpretable uncertainty measure for trajectory-scoring planners, which groups candidate trajectories by lateral driving direction and computes the entropy of the resulting five-way distribution of scores. Third, it introduces \texttt{navsafe}, a set of 229 safety-critical \texttt{navtest} frames in ten scenario categories, defined with the help of the pre-crash scenario typology of the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA)~\cite{najm2007pre} and selected after sorting the \texttt{navtest} clips by the performance of Hydra-MDP, the planner that Centaur adapts. The base planner, Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp}, and the NAVSIM benchmark~\cite{dauner2024navsim} are prior work by others; the test-time training procedure, Cluster Entropy and \texttt{navsafe} are the contributions of this chapter.

All results are obtained on logged states in the non-reactive simulation of NAVSIM v1~\cite{dauner2024navsim}. Each frame starts from the logged, human-driven state of the ego vehicle, the planned trajectory is scored over a 4-second horizon while the other road users follow their logged motion, and the next frame is again a logged state. The planner's errors therefore cannot compound, so neither behavioural shift in the closed-loop sense of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} nor drift during deployment is tested. In a single training run evaluated on the \texttt{navtest} split with NAVSIM v1.0, Centaur raises the PDM score (PDMS) of the state-of-the-art planner Hydra-MDP from 90.3 to 92.6, against 94.8 for the logged human trajectories. Averaged over three training seeds, Centaur scores 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 PDMS on the official NAVSIM v1.1 \texttt{navtest} leaderboard, ranking first among the entries submitted with three seeds at the time of the paper's submission (March 2025); no matched three-seed Hydra-MDP baseline without TTT was evaluated, so the size of the TTT effect under seed variation is not known. On \texttt{navsafe}, Centaur obtains the highest reported overall score of the evaluated planners, 74.14 against 56.47 for Hydra-MDP. This comparison favours any modification of Hydra-MDP, because sorting the clips by Hydra-MDP's performance enriches \texttt{navsafe} for frames on which Hydra-MDP scores low, and the aggregation behind the overall score still has to be confirmed (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe_results}). Centaur also regresses on the yielding category (from 71.6 to 59.3) and remains far from the 94.65 of human trajectories.

The chapter also builds on its predecessors. NAVSIM builds upon data from OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene}, described in Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}, and nuPlan~\cite{caesar2021nuplan}, and the five direction anchors used by Cluster Entropy follow the five steering categories of DriveLM~\cite{sima2024drivelm} (Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}); DriveLM's five speed categories are not used.

The remainder of the chapter is organised as follows. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:intro} explains why today's safeguards are insufficient and why defining the uncertainty of a planner is non-trivial. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:method} presents Centaur, including Cluster Entropy and the test-time training procedure. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe} introduces the \texttt{navsafe} benchmark. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:exp} reports results on \texttt{navtest} and \texttt{navsafe}, and Section~\ref{centaur:sec:analysis} analyses qualitative behaviour, generality, design choices and computational cost. Section~\ref{centaur:sec:discussion} discusses the findings, the threats to their validity and the remaining limitations, and Section~\ref{centaur:sec:summary} summarises the chapter. Additional related work, methodological details and ablations are given in Appendix~\ref{app:centaur}.

\section{Motivation}\label{centaur:sec:intro}

Safe interaction with other road users and robustness in unforeseen situations are vital as autonomous vehicles increasingly share public streets with human-driven vehicles~\cite{shalevshwartz2017rss,liu2024curse}. There is rising interest in deploying end-to-end planners in autonomous vehicles~\cite{chitta2023transfuser,hu2023uniad,weng2024paradrive,li2024hydramdp}, owing to their scalability and strong empirical performance in challenging scenarios~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey,li2023open}. In practice, however, end-to-end planners often apply fallback layers for real-world deployment, which use pre-defined safe actions to ensure safety in critical situations~\cite{chitta2023transfuser,vitelli2022safetynet,waywe2024ad2}. While useful, fallback layers require considerable domain expertise and manual engineering, do not benefit from data-driven improvements, and can constrain the performance potential of the learned algorithm.

Another approach to improving safety optimises the planned output trajectory at test time with expert-crafted cost functions~\cite{hu2023uniad,Hu2023hoplan}. It relies on explicit representations, such as drivable area maps or motion predictions of other vehicles, and assigns numerical costs to unsafe planning behaviours defined with respect to these representations, \eg driving off-road or colliding. Unfortunately, the representations needed for this approach are costly to annotate. They prevent it from fully leveraging the scalability of end-to-end learning, which typically requires only easy-to-obtain annotations such as vehicle trajectories or driving actions~\cite{Prakash2021transfuser,wu2022tcp}. Consequently, end-to-end planners are often not amenable to test-time optimisation with expert-designed cost functions. To deploy and improve end-to-end driving policies safely, we seek a data-driven approach that enhances safety without depending on fallback layers or explicit representations at test time, and that can be scaled effectively; the planner that we adapt is still trained to distil rule-based scores (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:prelim}).

Centaur achieves this through test-time training~\cite{sun2020ttt,liang2025ttasurvey}, which computes a gradient of the network weights to minimise an objective defined on a small dataset collected during deployment~\cite{wang2022cotta,liang2025ttasurvey,Gao2024fstta}. In contrast to test-time trajectory optimisation with explicit representations and expert-designed cost functions, the TTT objective of Centaur optimises the network weights to reduce planning uncertainty, which can be estimated in an unsupervised manner. Furthermore, although Centaur trains at test time, its gradient computation only needs past frames, so it could run asynchronously and in parallel with inference and add little latency; we have not implemented this asynchronous execution (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:latency}).

Defining a planning uncertainty that enables TTT is, however, non-trivial under the historically prevalent paradigm of trajectory regression in end-to-end driving~\cite{hu2023uniad,chitta2023transfuser,weng2024paradrive,li2024ego}, in which the planner directly regresses the desired trajectory from sensor inputs. Existing work on TTT focuses on classification~\cite{Gao2024fstta,wang2021tent}, where the objective is typically the entropy of the prediction, a quantity that is not easy to compute in a regression setting. An emerging class of end-to-end driving methods based on trajectory scoring~\cite{li2024hydramdp,Sadat2020P3,Casas2021mp3} instead assigns numerical scores to multiple trajectory candidates, much like a classifier. Focusing on this class, Centaur introduces Cluster Entropy, which aggregates the candidate trajectory scores of the planner into clusters based on driving direction and computes the entropy of this simple and interpretable distribution.

Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:teaser} illustrates the approach with Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp}, a trajectory-scoring planner. In the complex scene shown, the vehicle is entering a roundabout. After TTT, the distribution of high-scoring trajectories becomes more concentrated on the safer behaviour of a right turn, which helps the planner avoid illegally turning left into oncoming traffic. Besides its use for TTT, Cluster Entropy can also be used to flag frames on which the planner may fail. At a fixed threshold, it gives the highest true-positive rate among the compared uncertainty measures, but its accuracy is below that of never flagging and no threshold-free metric is available, so its value as a failure detector is not established (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:failure}).

Finally, as planners draw closer to human-level performance on existing benchmarks, a single aggregate score says little about where they still fail. To advance safe autonomous driving, we therefore construct \texttt{navsafe}, a benchmark of edge cases and challenging driving scenarios, with a semi-automated data curation pipeline that involves human annotation and verification to sample safety-critical scenarios. Evaluating state-of-the-art planners, including Centaur, on \texttt{navsafe} reveals new insights into their strengths and weaknesses, as well as a substantial gap to human-level performance. Because its frames were pre-sorted by the performance of Hydra-MDP, \texttt{navsafe} is better suited to exposing weaknesses shared by several planners than to comparing Hydra-MDP with its own variants (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}).

\section{Related Work}\label{centaur:sec:related}

\paragraph{End-to-end autonomous driving.} End-to-end autonomous driving represents a paradigm shift in autonomous vehicle technology~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey}. Its goal is to map raw sensor inputs directly to vehicle control outputs with neural networks, bypassing traditional modular components such as separate perception, planning and control systems~\cite{Prakash2021transfuser,chitta2021neat,jaeger2023hidden,zimmerlin2024tf,chen2024vadv2,jiang2023vad,hu2023uniad,hu2022model,jia2023think,wang2023drivedreamer,jia2023driveadapter,li2024hydramdp,liao2025diffusiondrive}, including the occupancy-based approach of Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}~\cite{sima2023occnet}.

\paragraph{End-to-end planner deployment.} Obtaining trustworthy outputs from an end-to-end system during deployment is challenging, given its ``black box'' nature. Rule-based planners and fallback layers are common ways to guardrail the planning output. For instance, UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad} uses test-time optimisation to refine its planning output, TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser} adopts forced braking when the LiDAR sensor encounters obstacles, and SafetyNet~\cite{vitelli2022safetynet} adopts fallback layers that ensure planning safety via sanity checks on collision and physical feasibility. These approaches either require additional annotation for the explicit representations they rely on or can make the planner overly conservative. Centaur uses no rules at test time, although its base planner distils rule-based scores during training (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:prelim}).

\paragraph{Test-time compute and training.} Leveraging test-time compute has recently emerged as an important way to strengthen reasoning capabilities~\cite{openaio1}. In addition, test-time adaptation and training techniques~\cite{liang2025ttasurvey} have shown considerable potential in computer vision~\cite{sun2020ttt,wang2021tent,hakim2023clust3,marsden2024universal}, multimodal learning~\cite{shu2022test,yoon2024c}, vision-language navigation~\cite{Gao2024fstta}, trajectory prediction~\cite{park2024t4p}, robot manipulation~\cite{hansen2021pad} and long-context large language models~\cite{sun2024learning}. The key idea of these approaches is to minimise entropy or related proxies at test time. Centaur is aligned in spirit with them. To our knowledge, it was the first application of test-time training by uncertainty minimisation to an end-to-end planner on NAVSIM at the time of publication. It differs from T4P~\cite{park2024t4p}, which applies test-time training to trajectory prediction rather than to planning, and from test-time search and optimisation of trajectories, such as Diffusion-ES~\cite{yang2024diffusiones} and the more recent TOAD~\cite{xu2026toad}, which change the output of a planner rather than its weights. Chapter~\ref{ch:background} reviews these lines of work more broadly.

Further related work on Hydra-MDP and ensembling, semantic entropy, safety-critical scenario benchmarks and evidential uncertainty is discussed in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:related}.

\subsection{Preliminaries}\label{centaur:sec:prelim}

End-to-end self-driving methods seek to obtain a policy $\pi$ that directly outputs driving decisions, such as desired trajectories $\mathbf{t}$, based on sensor inputs $\mathbf{x}$ and a navigation command $\mathbf{c}$. Trajectories take the form $\tilde{\mathbf{t}} = (\mathbf{p}_0, \mathbf{p}_1, \dots, \mathbf{p}_N)$, a sequence of waypoints, which are Bird's Eye View (BEV) positions of the vehicle over $N$ evenly spaced time steps. The policy estimates trajectories from sensor inputs $\mathbf{x}_0$ (such as camera images) and a discrete navigation command $\mathbf{c}_0$ (indicating the driver's intention in ambiguous situations, \eg left, right or straight), both captured at the current time step, indexed with $0$. Among the existing paradigms for end-to-end driving, the straightforward approach of regressing trajectories using a dataset of human driving demonstrations is referred to as \emph{trajectory regression}. Although Centaur is compatible with trajectory regression, this chapter primarily addresses its application to an emerging paradigm with higher performance in the recent literature: \emph{trajectory scoring} (\eg Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp}, VADv2~\cite{chen2024vadv2} and RAD~\cite{gao2025rad}). The methodology for applying Centaur to trajectory regression methods such as UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad} and TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser} is described in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:regression}, and the corresponding results with TransFuser are reported in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:ablations}.

\paragraph{Trajectory scoring.} In this formulation, the planner outputs a set of scalar \emph{score features} $\{s_j\}_{j=1}^k$ for a \emph{planning vocabulary} of $k$ different trajectories. The score features numerically capture different aspects of desired driving behaviour, such as avoiding collisions and making progress. They can be aggregated into a single final score via an aggregation function $\phi$. During inference, the trajectory with the largest predicted final score is selected:
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:multi-score-output}
  \{s_j\}_{j=1}^k = \{ \pi_\theta(\mathbf{t}_j, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{c}_0) \}_{j=1}^k,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:cost-select}
  \hat{\mathbf{t}}_0 = \argmax_{\mathbf{t}_j} \{\phi(s_j)\}_{j=1}^k.
\end{equation}
As a result of this design, the planner is inherently multi-modal, and the distribution of scores over the planning vocabulary provides a way to represent planning uncertainty. For this type of planner, learning the policy parameters $\theta$ can be formulated as
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:objective-score}
  \argmin_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})\sim D_{kd}} \left[\mathcal{L}_{kd}\big(e(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c}), \pi_\theta(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})\big)\right],
\end{equation}
where $e$ is an expert algorithm that can provide ground-truth score features for any sampled trajectory, and $D_{kd} = \{(\mathbf{t}, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{c}), \dots\}$ is a knowledge distillation dataset generated by sampling this expert algorithm for different inputs, typically in a driving simulator~\cite{caesar2021nuplan,dosovitskiy2017carla} or a world model~\cite{yang2024genad,gao2024vista}. In practice, the score features are normalised to the range $(0,1)$, and $\mathcal{L}_{kd}$ is implemented as a cross-entropy loss. For Hydra-MDP, the expert $e$ provides the rule-based sub-scores of the NAVSIM metric (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:setup}), so the score heads that TTT later sharpens are trained to reproduce the metric on which the planner is evaluated. Centaur uses no rules at test time, but the planner distils rule-based scores during training. The complete training loss of the Hydra-MDP implementation used in this chapter, which adds an imitation term, is given in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:scoring}.

\subsection{Cluster Entropy}\label{centaur:sec:ce}

In essence, the scoring procedure of Eqs.~\eqref{centaur:eq:multi-score-output} and~\eqref{centaur:eq:cost-select} acts as a classifier over the $k$ classes of the planning vocabulary. We formulate a new uncertainty measure, Cluster Entropy, to translate this intuition into an unsupervised training objective for end-to-end planning, which, unlike Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:objective-score}, does not require an expert algorithm. The key challenge is that the planning vocabulary is often very large, typically encompassing several thousand possible trajectories. Even in simple driving scenarios, many neighbouring trajectories in the vocabulary therefore receive nearly identical scores. Simply minimising the entropy of the distribution of scores over the vocabulary would yield an objective that encourages the model to avoid trajectories with a larger number of nearby neighbours in the vocabulary, \eg driving straight. Such a naive entropy is therefore unsuitable for uncertainty estimation in this setting. Cluster Entropy instead proceeds in three steps.

\paragraph{Trajectory candidates.} First, we sample a set of $M$ trajectory candidates $\{\mathbf{t}_m\}_{m=1}^M$ from the planning vocabulary, where $M \ll k$. The sampling is weighted to prioritise candidates that have a high average score according to the expert algorithm $e$; the sampling weights are specified in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:entropy}. The candidates are drawn once, before testing, and are shared by all frames. Because the weights are averages over \texttt{navtrain}, manoeuvres that are rare in training are less likely to be among them. The entropy over this reduced set of $M$ scores already serves as a meaningful uncertainty measure, as the Full Entropy baseline in the experiments shows, but the next two steps further improve its effectiveness and interpretability.

\paragraph{Direction-based sampling.} Next, we use domain-specific criteria to select trajectory anchors from the $M$ candidates. Following DriveLM~\cite{sima2024drivelm}, we select five anchors: \texttt{sharp left}, \texttt{slight left}, \texttt{forward}, \texttt{slight right} and \texttt{sharp right}. The \texttt{sharp left} and \texttt{sharp right} anchors, the candidates with the largest lateral offsets, are selected first. The \texttt{slight left} and \texttt{slight right} anchors are the candidates whose lateral offsets are closest to half of those of \texttt{sharp left} and \texttt{sharp right}, and \texttt{forward} is the candidate with the smallest lateral offset. The anchors thus differ only in lateral offset. DriveLM describes a behaviour by a speed category and a steering category, with five bins each (Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}); Cluster Entropy uses only the steering categories. No anchor represents slowing down or stopping, so the five-way distribution has no outcome that separates proceeding from stopping in the same lane (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Score aggregation.} As shown in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:pipeline} (top), we aim to construct a five-way categorical distribution that captures how the predicted scores are distributed over the five driving directions. For this, we form five clusters $\mathcal{C}_1, \dots, \mathcal{C}_5$ by assigning each of the $M$ candidates to its nearest anchor in terms of $L_2$ distance. Within each cluster $\mathcal{C}_a$, the predicted scores of all its trajectories are summed to obtain an anchor score $\mathit{score}_a$. Normalising the anchor scores yields a probability distribution, and Cluster Entropy, denoted $H$, is defined as the Shannon entropy of this distribution:
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:ce}
  p_a = \frac{\mathit{score}_a}{\sum_{a'=1}^{5} \mathit{score}_{a'}}, \qquad
  H(\mathbf{x}) = -\sum_{a=1}^{5} p_a \log p_a .
\end{equation}
Since the distribution has five outcomes, $H$ ranges from $0$, when all the score mass falls into a single driving direction, to $\ln 5$, when it is spread uniformly over the five directions. Because the clusters are fixed and can differ in size, summing the scores gives more mass to the more populated clusters, so minimising $H$ may favour them. The argument made above against naive entropy therefore applies, in a weaker form, to Cluster Entropy as well; we have not analysed the resulting cluster sizes.

\subsection{Test-Time Training}\label{centaur:sec:ttt}

Equipped with this unsupervised training objective, our goal is to minimise uncertainty at test time, which is the key idea of TTT approaches in the literature. We follow the standard practice of performing a single step of gradient descent to update the network parameters $\theta$~\cite{Gao2024fstta}, using the Cluster Entropy $H(\mathbf{x})$ as the objective function. Specifically, given $H(\mathbf{x})$ calculated after a forward pass through $\pi_\theta$, we obtain the gradient $\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}$ by backpropagation. Instead of directly updating $\theta$ with this gradient, however, we store $\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}$ in a buffer of length $F=4$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:pipeline} (bottom). At time step $i$, we update the network using the average $\{\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}\}_{avg}$ of all gradients currently in the buffer, obtaining
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:ttt}
  \hat{\theta}_i = \theta - \eta \left\{\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}\right\}_{avg},
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the learning rate. Intuitively, this update suppresses the ability of the model to assign high scores in multiple clusters. This, in turn, can lower the scores of outlier predictions that differ strongly from the other trajectories in their cluster, such as the high-scoring `slight left' outlier in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:teaser}. The update can only sharpen the distribution: it moves score mass towards the clusters that already hold most of it, so when the dominant cluster is wrong, the update reinforces the error (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:discussion}). Algorithm~\ref{centaur:alg:ttt} summarises the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \DontPrintSemicolon
  \caption{Test-time training in Centaur}\label{centaur:alg:ttt}
  \KwIn{trained trajectory-scoring planner $\pi_\theta$, where $\theta$ are the score-decoder parameters (perception backbone frozen); planning vocabulary $\{\mathbf{t}_j\}_{j=1}^{k}$; $M$ trajectory candidates and their five direction anchors (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ce}); buffer length $F$; learning rate $\eta$}
  \KwOut{a planned trajectory $\hat{\mathbf{t}}_0$ at every time step}
  $\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \emptyset$ \tcp*{buffer of historical gradients}
  \ForEach{time step $i$, with current sensor inputs $\mathbf{x}_0$ and command $\mathbf{c}_0$}{
    \eIf{$\mathcal{B} \neq \emptyset$}{
      $\hat{\theta}_i \leftarrow \theta - \eta \left\{\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}\right\}_{avg}$, averaging the gradients in $\mathcal{B}$ \tcp*{Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ttt}}
    }{
      $\hat{\theta}_i \leftarrow \theta$\;
    }
    Score the vocabulary with $\pi_{\hat{\theta}_i}$ and output $\hat{\mathbf{t}}_0$ \tcp*{Eqs.~\eqref{centaur:eq:multi-score-output}--\eqref{centaur:eq:cost-select}}
    \tcp{may run asynchronously on separate hardware}
    Compute the Cluster Entropy $H(\mathbf{x}_0)$ from the predicted scores of the $M$ candidates \tcp*{Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ce}}
    Obtain $\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}$ by backpropagation and push it into $\mathcal{B}$, keeping only the $F$ most recent gradients\;
  }
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{What the procedure specifies.} As written, Algorithm~\ref{centaur:alg:ttt} and Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ttt} fix four properties of the update. First, every update starts from the trained parameters $\theta$: the adapted parameters $\hat{\theta}_i$ are used to plan at time step $i$ and are then discarded, so updates do not accumulate over time, and earlier frames influence the planner only through the $F$ gradients in the buffer. Second, the update at time step $i$ uses only gradients from earlier time steps, and the first time step is planned with $\theta$ unchanged. Third, the buffer is initialised once and is never reset. Fourth, each update is a single plain gradient step with learning rate $\eta$ on the parameters of the score decoder, with the perception backbone frozen.

Two points are not specified. The first is how the time steps of the algorithm map onto the NAVSIM evaluation, in which each \texttt{navtest} sample is scored from 1.5 seconds of logged history, \ie four frames (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}): the $F=4$ buffered frames may be the sample's own history frames or the samples that precede it in the order of evaluation. \authorcheck{state whether the $F=4$ buffered frames are the sample's own 1.5\,s input history or preceding \texttt{navtest} samples; if the latter, give the evaluation order, say whether the buffer is reset between logs, and confirm that no buffered frame post-dates the evaluated one or overlaps its 4\,s future} The second is the point at which the gradient is evaluated: $H$ is computed from the scores predicted with $\hat{\theta}_i$, but the gradient is written with respect to $\theta$. \authorcheck{confirm that the implementation resets to the trained weights $\theta$ for every sample, as in Eq.~\eqref{centaur:eq:ttt}; state whether $\partial H / \partial \theta$ is evaluated at $\theta$ or at $\hat{\theta}_i$, and which score-decoder parameters are updated} Under either reading of the buffer, the buffered frames are logged observations recorded while a human drove, so the planner's own outputs never influence the frames on which it adapts.

\paragraph{Deployment.} Because the update at time step $i$ only uses gradients of \emph{historical time steps} that are already present in the buffer, the buffer does more than smooth the update by averaging: it would allow the gradient calculation to run asynchronously, in parallel with inference and on separate hardware, so that the latency-critical inference process incurs little overhead. We have not implemented this asynchronous execution. In the experiments, the gradient calculation runs sequentially and is included in the measured latency (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:latency}).

\section{\texorpdfstring{\texttt{navsafe}}{navsafe}: A Safety-Critical Scenario Set}\label{centaur:sec:navsafe}

\begin{figure}[tbp]

This section describes the construction and highlights of \texttt{navsafe}, a challenging new benchmark consisting of edge cases for end-to-end planning. The data used in the benchmark is sampled from NAVSIM~\cite{dauner2024navsim}, which in turn builds upon data from OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene} (Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet}) and nuPlan~\cite{caesar2021nuplan}.

\paragraph{NAVSIM.} The framework on which \texttt{navsafe} is built is primarily designed to capture scenarios with substantial changes in driving behaviour, where the historical trajectory of the ego vehicle does not allow for an accurate extrapolation of the future plan. It is split into two parts, \texttt{navtrain} and \texttt{navtest}, which contain 1,192 logs (103,288 frames) and 136 logs (12,146 frames), respectively. In NAVSIM, driving agents are required to plan a trajectory as a sequence of future positions spanning a horizon of 4 seconds. The input to an agent includes 1.5 seconds of past history (resulting in 4 frames of data) from onboard camera and LiDAR sensors, as well as the vehicle's current speed, acceleration and navigation command, collectively referred to as the ego status. We retain these default NAVSIM settings for evaluation on \texttt{navsafe}.

\paragraph{Construction criteria.} Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:navsafe} illustrates the data curation process for \texttt{navsafe}. The US NHTSA holds a collection of traffic accidents reported every year across the nation, which is shared publicly via a pre-crash scenario typology for crash avoidance research~\cite{najm2007pre}. It includes a wide variety of pre-crash scenario types, ranging from common ones such as ``Lead Vehicle Stopped'' to uncommon ones such as ``Animal Crash With Prior Vehicle Maneuver''. The five most frequent types, listed in the inset table of Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:navsafe}, are ``Lead Vehicle Stopped'', ``Control Loss Without Prior Vehicle Action'', ``Vehicle(s) Turning at Non-Signalized Junctions'', ``Lead Vehicle Decelerating'' and ``Road Edge Departure Without Prior Vehicle Maneuver''~\cite{najm2007pre}. Supported by summary statistics on the types of pre-crash scenarios~\cite{thorn2018framework,najm2007pre}, \texttt{navsafe} is curated to highlight the safety-critical scenarios in which modern end-to-end planners could fail. We construct it with the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}[label={Step~\arabic*.}, start=0, leftmargin=*]
  \item Sort the video clips in the \texttt{navtest} split by the average performance of Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp}, the base planner of Centaur.
  \item Check the video clips with human annotators and try to assign frames to new or existing categories in \texttt{navsafe}.
  \item Populate the scenario types with new examples.
  \item Retrieve scenario type labels from the NHTSA file to define scenario types not yet in \texttt{navsafe}.
  \item Re-sort \texttt{navtest} and start a new cycle of the workflow.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Consequences of the construction.} Three properties of this procedure matter for how results on \texttt{navsafe} can be read. First, Step~0 sorts the clips by the performance of Hydra-MDP, the planner that Centaur adapts, before any human categorisation, so \texttt{navsafe} is enriched for frames on which Hydra-MDP scores low. Any perturbation of Hydra-MDP, such as another training seed or any test-time training objective, is therefore expected to score higher on these frames by regression to the mean, and comparisons between Hydra-MDP and its variants on \texttt{navsafe} are biased in favour of the variants. We did not evaluate a control that would measure this effect, such as an independently trained Hydra-MDP seed without TTT or a perturbation unrelated to entropy, and we did not re-derive the selection from a planner outside the comparison or from a rule fixed in advance. Second, \texttt{navsafe} is a subset of \texttt{navtest}, on which all other results of this chapter are reported and no held-out split is used (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:setup}), so it is not a held-out test set. Its frames come from the same data source as \texttt{navtrain}; they represent the tail of the logged distribution rather than conditions absent from training. Third, frames were assigned to categories by human annotators, and no inter-annotator agreement is reported for these assignments.

\paragraph{Highlights of \texttt{navsafe}.} For the benchmark, we select 229 of the 12,146 frames of \texttt{navtest}, covering 10 categories: ``Roundabout'', ``Yellow light, rush or wait?'', ``Exit ramp'', ``Unprotected left'', ``Enter ramp'', ``Uncommon traffic sign'', ``Overtaking with lane change'', ``No lane area (\eg parking lot)'', ``Bad weather'' and ``Yielding (\eg to pedestrians)''. By providing separate scores for each scenario type, \texttt{navsafe} enables a finer-grained assessment of specific planning capabilities than the single aggregate score of existing benchmarks such as \texttt{navtest}. In the CLIP-based view of Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:navsafe}, many \texttt{navsafe} frames lie away from the centre of the \texttt{navtest} distribution, but several lie inside it. This view is qualitative: we did not quantify how far \texttt{navsafe} frames lie from the training distribution, for example by percentiles of embedding distance to \texttt{navtrain}. In the terms of this thesis, \texttt{navsafe} is a stress test drawn from the tail of the logged distribution, where the curse of reality is expected to bite, rather than a test of conditions absent from training. In the experiments, we benchmark the performance of the human agent (ground-truth trajectories from NAVSIM) as well as state-of-the-art end-to-end planners on it. The frame tokens and category labels of \texttt{navsafe} are not listed in this thesis. \authorcheck{confirm whether the \texttt{navsafe} frame tokens and category labels are publicly released (Table~\ref{tab:method:artefacts} states that they are not); if they are not, list the 229 tokens and labels in Appendix~\ref{app:centaur} or remove every claim of release}

\section{Experiments}\label{centaur:sec:exp}

This section evaluates Centaur on the NAVSIM framework~\cite{dauner2024navsim}, including the official \texttt{navtest} leaderboard and the \texttt{navsafe} scenarios of Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{centaur:sec:setup}

\paragraph{Metrics.} The experiments adopt the primary metric of NAVSIM, the PDM score (PDMS), to evaluate end-to-end planning models. NAVSIM v1 computes it in non-reactive simulation, \ie by unrolling the planned trajectory over the 4-second horizon from the logged state of the ego vehicle, while the other road users follow their logged motion and do not react to it:
\begin{equation}
  \label{centaur:eq:pdms}
  \mathrm{PDMS} = \mathrm{NC} \times \mathrm{DAC} \times \left( \frac{5\,\mathrm{TTC} + 2\,\mathrm{C} + 5\,\mathrm{EP}}{12} \right),
\end{equation}
where the sub-scores NC (No at-fault Collision), DAC (Drivable Area Compliance), EP (Ego Progress), C (Comfort) and TTC (Time-to-Collision), each represented as a percentage, are combined into a single score. A detailed definition of each sub-score can be found in~\cite{dauner2024navsim}. Owing to its relevance for safety, several experiments discuss TTC in particular. TTC identifies whether the output trajectory of the planner results in near-collisions, \ie whether a forward projection at constant velocity would result in a collision within 1 second at any point along the trajectory.

\paragraph{What the evaluation tests.} Every frame starts from the logged, human-driven state, and the next frame evaluated is again a logged state, never one produced by the planner's earlier actions. Errors therefore cannot compound, and behavioural shift and drift during deployment are not tested (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:discussion}). As a reference, we also report the PDMS of the logged human trajectories. This reference is not an upper bound: EP is normalised by the progress of the privileged rule-based planner PDM-Closed~\cite{dauner2023parting}, and a planner can score higher than the human driver. PDM-Closed itself, which would be a second reference, is not reported in this chapter.

\paragraph{Base model.} The base model, Hydra-MDP of Li \etal~\cite{li2024hydramdp}, was designed specifically for NAVSIM. It takes LiDAR points as input, which are splatted onto the BEV plane and encoded by a ResNet34~\cite{he2016deep}. These features are fused with image features extracted by a \mbox{V2-99}~\cite{park2021pseudo} backbone via the sensor fusion modules of TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser}. The decoder then predicts scores for a planning vocabulary of $k=8192$ trajectories. The score functions output by the decoder are the five sub-scores (NC, DAC, EP, C and TTC) considered in the NAVSIM evaluation, learned by distillation from the rule-based NAVSIM scores (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:prelim}). Additional experiments with the more lightweight TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser}, which performs trajectory regression instead of scoring, are provided in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:ablations} and summarised in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ablations}.

\paragraph{Deployment strategy.} As a deployment baseline, we implement an uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories. We first calculate the average PDMS of each trajectory in the planning vocabulary of Hydra-MDP and select the top 20 as fallback trajectories. During inference, we measure the $L_2$ distance between the predicted trajectory and the 20 fallback trajectories. If a failure is detected, based on an uncertainty value above a threshold, the closest of these 20 fallback trajectories replaces the predicted one as the safe action. The chosen thresholds are discussed with Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class}. This baseline borrows the idea of a fallback from SafetyNet~\cite{vitelli2022safetynet}, but it is not representative of SafetyNet or of rule-based safeguards in general: SafetyNet checks the learned plan for collisions and physical feasibility and falls back to a rule-based planner, whereas our fallback is triggered by uncertainty alone and chooses among 20 fixed trajectories that do not depend on the scene. We did not evaluate a representative rule-based fallback on the same inputs, such as the PDM-Closed planner~\cite{dauner2023parting} or a collision check.

\paragraph{Uncertainty measures.} We compare Cluster Entropy with three baselines. For \textbf{KL Divergence}, we obtain the predicted categorical distribution over the planning vocabulary ($k=8192$ classes) for each score function (NC, DAC, EP, C and TTC), then calculate the KL divergence between each pair of distributions and sum over all pairs. \textbf{Full Entropy}~\cite{shannon1948entropy} is calculated like Cluster Entropy, but skips the clustering of the $M=100$ candidates into five clusters and simply computes the entropy of the full 100-way candidate score distribution. \textbf{Semantic Entropy} is our adaptation of semantic uncertainty~\cite{kuhn2023semantic} to autonomous driving. Its calculation is again similar to that of Cluster Entropy, but the candidates are clustered in a feature space in which each trajectory is represented by its predicted scores $s_j$ (details in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:entropy}). For compactness, TTT for Hydra-MDP with Semantic Entropy, the baseline used most frequently in the experiments, is referred to as \textbf{Hydra-SE}.

\paragraph{Implementation.} Except for Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all}, we adopt NAVSIM v1.0 for all training and local evaluations, and NAVSIM v1.1 for the official leaderboard submissions; the two versions differ slightly owing to minor changes in metric definitions. The NAVSIM v1.0 comparisons (Tables~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation} and~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}) are single training runs, whereas the leaderboard entries in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all} are means over three seeds. We train the base end-to-end model Hydra-MDP~\cite{li2024hydramdp} on the \texttt{navtrain} split for 20 epochs on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a total batch size of 256. The learning rate of AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2017decoupled} is set to $10^{-4}$, with weight decay disabled. For Cluster Entropy and TTT, we use $M=100$ candidate trajectories and perform one step of gradient descent with a learning rate $\eta$ of $10^{-4}$ on a dataset of $F=4$ history frames. Only the score decoder is updated during TTT; the perception backbones are kept frozen. No held-out split is used in this chapter: these TTT settings, the clustering threshold $\tau$ of Semantic Entropy (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:exp}) and the buffer-length ablation of Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ablations} are all reported on \texttt{navtest}, the split on which the results are reported, and \texttt{navsafe} is a subset of \texttt{navtest}. \authorcheck{state how $\eta$, $M$, $F$ and $\tau$ were chosen, and confirm that no split other than \texttt{navtest} was used to choose them}

In this experiment, we perform binary classification of potential failure cases using the different uncertainty estimates. This task is of critical relevance for vehicles deployed with partial autonomy, which rely on human intervention in challenging situations. We define a failure as a scenario in which the model obtains a PDMS of 0, and consider an uncertainty above a threshold to indicate a possible failure. Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class} reports the true positive rate (TPR) and accuracy of identifying such failures. Here, we select a threshold of $0.8 = 0.5\ln 5$, which is half the entropy of a uniform distribution over 5 classes. At this fixed threshold, Cluster Entropy gives the highest TPR among the compared measures (62.8\%), as well as the highest accuracy (73.6\%). This evidence is limited, however. Accuracy is uninformative at this prevalence: with 15.6\% of frames labelled as failures (the Select All baseline), never flagging a failure (Select None) already reaches 84.4\%, above every measure, including Cluster Entropy. The table also gives a single operating point per measure, and these operating points are not comparable with one another (a KL threshold of 1500 against an entropy threshold of 0.8). Threshold-free metrics, such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) or under the precision--recall curve (AUPRC), and the TPR at a fixed false-positive rate are not reported, so the table does not show that Cluster Entropy separates failures from successes better at every operating point.

Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all} shows the mean and standard deviation of the metrics for all entries on the leaderboard submitted with 3 seeds, which share the same setting as ours. At the time of the paper's submission (March 2025), Centaur ranked first among these entries, with a mean PDMS of 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33. Its lead over Hydra-SE (91.87 $\pm$ 0.19), which differs from Centaur only in its uncertainty measure, is 0.23 PDMS; Section~\ref{centaur:sec:discussion} discusses what this margin supports. The Hydra-MDP entry is a single evaluation of a three-model ensemble, so the table contains no matched three-seed baseline of Hydra-MDP without TTT: the three-seed results show where Centaur ranks, not how much TTT adds under seed variation. We achieve this without any tuning of the training hyper-parameters beyond the Hydra-MDP defaults, and without the model ensembling required by the prior Hydra-MDP result, which is based on the winning entry to the 2024 NAVSIM challenge~\cite{li2024hydramdp}. Comparisons with additional baselines on NAVSIM v1.0 are provided in Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:ablations}.

\subsection{Generality and Design Choices}\label{centaur:sec:ablations}

\paragraph{Trajectory regression planners.} Test-time training is not restricted to trajectory scoring. Equipping the regression heads of TransFuser~\cite{chitta2023transfuser} with evidential uncertainty (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:regression}) and applying TTT with Semantic Entropy yields TransFuser-SE, which raises the PDMS of TransFuser from 83.4 to 85.7 on NAVSIM v1.0 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}). This is the same gain of 2.3 PDMS that Centaur obtains over Hydra-MDP, again in single runs. TTT with evidential uncertainty reaches 84.2 PDMS, whereas the fallback again reduces ego progress to below 15 and the PDMS to around 60 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}), in rows that are subject to the same inconsistency as those of Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation} (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:consistency}).

\paragraph{Test-time optimisation with explicit representations.} The NAVSIM v1.0 comparison in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp} also includes UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad}, which refines its plan through optimisation with an expert-designed cost function. With this optimisation step, UniAD drops from 83.4 to 75.2 PDMS. Unlike the fallback in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}, the optimisation lowers every sub-score: the safety-related NC (from 97.8 to 95.4), DAC (from 91.9 to 86.0) and TTC (from 92.9 to 87.3) as well as EP (from 78.8 to 71.9) and C (from 100.0 to 99.3). It therefore does not illustrate a trade-off between safety and progress; in this single-run evaluation, it made UniAD worse on every sub-score. Incorporating an explicit 3D occupancy head into PARA-Drive~\cite{weng2024paradrive} changes its PDMS from 84.0 to 83.6, a difference too small to establish an effect in single runs. These results show only that the two hand-designed additions evaluated here did not improve PDMS on NAVSIM v1.0; they do not show that explicit representations or cost functions are harmful in general. Both additions involve occupancy, but this evaluation and the open-loop planning experiments of Chapter~\ref{ch:occnet} differ in protocol, data and planner, so the two cannot be compared directly.

\paragraph{Combining historical gradients.} Following~\cite{Gao2024fstta}, the historical gradients could alternatively be combined via a singular-value decomposition (SVD) that selects the most useful ones for updating the model. On TransFuser-SE, this variant lowers the PDMS from 85.7 to 83.8 compared with simple averaging (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}).

\paragraph{Buffer length.} The number of history frames $F$ has little effect: Hydra-SE obtains 91.4, 91.3, 91.5 and 91.8 PDMS for $F=1$, $2$, $3$ and $4$, respectively (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}). The setting used throughout, $F=4$, scores highest, but the range of 0.5 PDMS comes from single runs on \texttt{navtest}, the split on which all results are reported, so it does not establish a trend; every setting improves on Hydra-MDP (90.3).

\paragraph{Image backbone.} Replacing the \mbox{V2-99} image backbone of Hydra-MDP with ViT-L gives slightly lower performance overall, \eg 89.9 \vs 90.3 PDMS without TTT and 90.9 \vs 91.5 with TTT and KL Divergence. With the fallback and KL Divergence, the ViT-L variant scores 67.4 against 63.1 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}), but these fallback rows are subject to the inconsistency noted in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:results}.

\subsection{Computational Cost}\label{centaur:sec:latency}

Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp} reports the amortised latency per frame on \texttt{navtest}, measured on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. The base Hydra-MDP takes 243.9\,ms, compared with 103.1\,ms for TransFuser, whose efficient ResNet-34 backbone comes at a cost of around 7 PDMS. In the current implementation, the latency of TTT includes the gradient calculation and the model update: with TTT and Semantic Entropy, Hydra-MDP takes 312.5\,ms, and 357.1\,ms with KL Divergence. The latency of Centaur itself, with Cluster Entropy, was not reported. On an A100 GPU, these latencies allow about 2.8 to 4.1 planning steps per second (computed from Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}), above the 2\,Hz at which NAVSIM provides frames (four frames in 1.5 seconds); whether this rate and the overhead of TTT would be acceptable on vehicle hardware and at vehicle control rates was not tested. The overhead could be reduced by computing the gradients in parallel with inference, as described in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ttt} but not implemented, or by applying TTT only on frames whose uncertainty exceeds a threshold, which lowers the latency of Hydra-SE to 279.6\,ms at 91.3 PDMS.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{centaur:sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.} On logged states in the non-reactive simulation of NAVSIM v1, test-time training on the planner's own uncertainty improved the PDMS of Hydra-MDP from 90.3 to 92.6 in a single run on NAVSIM v1.0, with the largest sub-score gain in TTC (from 93.9 to 98.0) and a slight loss in ego progress (from 86.5 to 85.9). Over three seeds on NAVSIM v1.1, Centaur scored 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33, but there is no matched three-seed baseline of Hydra-MDP without TTT. Every TTT variant in this chapter improved its base planner, by 0.4 to 2.3 PDMS in single runs (Tables~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}, \ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp} and~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}). No rules, cost functions or labels are used at test time; the planner distils rule-based scores during training. Because errors cannot compound in this evaluation, these results concern per-frame decisions on logged states; they do not show that a planner corrects itself over time in closed loop.

\paragraph{Comparison with the fallback.} As reported, the uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories raised NC, DAC and TTC but reduced ego progress to below 15 and PDMS to between 63.1 and 65.9 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}), whereas TTT raised the safety-related sub-scores while nearly maintaining progress. Two caveats limit what this comparison shows. First, the fallback is not representative of rule-based safeguards: it swaps in one of 20 fixed trajectories when an uncertainty threshold is exceeded, whereas SafetyNet~\cite{vitelli2022safetynet}, for example, checks the learned plan for collisions and physical feasibility and falls back to a rule-based planner. Second, the fallback rows cannot be reconciled with the thresholds at which the fallback is triggered (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:results}). The cost optimisation of UniAD, the other hand-designed addition evaluated, lowered every sub-score, including NC, DAC and TTC (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}), so it is not an example of trading progress for safety either. The evidence therefore supports a narrow statement: on logged frames in non-reactive simulation, test-time training improved PDMS, whereas the fallback we implemented, as reported, lowered it. It does not show that learned adaptation outperforms rule-based safeguards in general. On \texttt{navsafe}, Centaur's reported Overall score is 17.67 points above that of Hydra-MDP, against 2.3 PDMS on \texttt{navtest}. This contrast does not show that TTT helps more on hard frames, because \texttt{navsafe} was selected on Hydra-MDP's performance (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}). TTT is not free of trade-offs either: on \texttt{navsafe}, Centaur regresses on yielding, from 71.6 to 59.3.

\paragraph{How much of the gain is due to Cluster Entropy?} The headline improvement from 90.3 to 92.6 PDMS comes from a single training run evaluated with NAVSIM v1.0 (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}). Over three seeds on the NAVSIM v1.1 leaderboard, Centaur leads Hydra-SE, its own Semantic Entropy variant, by only 0.23 PDMS (92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 \vs 91.87 $\pm$ 0.19; Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all}), less than Centaur's own standard deviation across seeds, and no significance test was run. Moreover, all four uncertainty measures used for TTT in Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}, three entropies and a KL divergence, improve Hydra-MDP by between 1.2 PDMS (KL Divergence) and 2.3 PDMS (Cluster Entropy). This suggests that much of the benefit comes from minimising an entropy-style uncertainty at test time in general, rather than from the specific form of Cluster Entropy, whose distinctive advantage is better supported for interpretability (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:qualitative}) than for accuracy.

\paragraph{Interpretability and failure detection.} Because its five clusters correspond to lateral driving directions, Cluster Entropy makes it possible to inspect which alternatives the planner is torn between (Figs.~\ref{centaur:fig:teaser} and~\ref{centaur:fig:vis}). As a failure detector, its evidence is weak. At a fixed threshold, it gives the highest true-positive rate among the compared measures (62.8\%, Table~\ref{centaur:tab:fail_class}), but its accuracy of 73.6\% is below the 84.4\% of never flagging, accuracy is uninformative at this prevalence, threshold-free metrics (AUROC, AUPRC) are not reported, and the failure rate on which the table rests must be recomputed (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:failure}). Whether Cluster Entropy could alert a human driver to intervene in vehicles with partial autonomy therefore remains open.

\paragraph{Threats to validity: what the evaluation cannot reveal.} NAVSIM v1 scores each 4-second plan from the logged, human-driven state, the other road users follow their logged motion, and the next frame is again a logged state. The planner therefore never visits states caused by its own earlier actions, so errors cannot compound, and failure modes that only appear in closed loop cannot be observed: for example, a planner that is too conservative to make progress over a route, oscillation between alternatives on consecutive frames, or feedback between the adapted planner and the data on which it adapts. For the same reason, the TTT buffer contains logged observations recorded while a human drove, not the consequences of the planner's own actions, and, because every update restarts from $\theta$ (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ttt}), the evaluation says nothing about the stability of accumulated online updates, such as drift, collapse or forgetting. In addition, no held-out split is used: the TTT settings and the ablations are reported on \texttt{navtest}, and \texttt{navsafe} is a subset of \texttt{navtest}, so the reported gains may be optimistic. Finally, the NAVSIM v1.0 comparisons, the ablations and the \texttt{navsafe} results are single runs without confidence intervals or paired tests over frames; only the leaderboard entries are means over three seeds.

\paragraph{Threats to validity: what the gain may reflect.} Two alternative explanations of the gain have not been ruled out. The first is generic re-calibration rather than scene-specific adaptation. The update is a single gradient step with $\eta=10^{-4}$, and every uncertainty measure used for TTT improves Hydra-MDP (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation}). The gain may therefore come from a generic sharpening or re-calibration of the score heads, which frames unrelated to the current scene would produce as well. Two controls would separate these explanations, and neither was run: TTT with buffers drawn from unrelated scenes, and a static variant that applies the entropy step once, on \texttt{navtrain} frames, before testing. If the static variant recovered most of the gain, Centaur would be better described as entropy-based re-calibration than as test-time adaptation. The second is circularity with the metric. Hydra-MDP's score heads are trained to predict the PDMS sub-scores NC, DAC, EP, C and TTC (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:prelim}), and the $M$ candidates are sampled with weights given by their average PDMS on \texttt{navtrain} (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:entropy}). Sharpening these heads can therefore raise PDMS partly by construction. We have not evaluated a metric that is not distilled into the heads, such as the additional metrics of NAVSIM v2~\cite{cao2025pseudo}.

\paragraph{Threats to validity: what the objective cannot represent.} The five anchors differ only in lateral offset (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ce}), so Cluster Entropy has no outcome that separates proceeding from slowing down or stopping in the same lane. It can therefore neither measure nor reduce uncertainty about the decision that yielding (YLD) and yellow-light (YLLT) scenarios require, and that rear-end scenarios such as NHTSA's ``Lead Vehicle Stopped'' require as well. This may explain why Centaur regresses on YLD (from 71.6 to 59.3) and is not the best planner on YLLT, but we have not tested this explanation, for example with anchors that also distinguish speed. Moreover, minimising entropy can only sharpen the distribution. When the dominant cluster is wrong, the update can confirm the error instead of correcting it, as unsupervised entropy objectives are known to do~\cite{wang2022cotta}, and the failure case in Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:vis} shows that TTT cannot overturn a confident prediction. We have not analysed the frames on which TTT lowers PDMS. Applying TTT only on frames whose uncertainty exceeds a threshold, as in the selective variant of Hydra-SE (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_ablation_supp}), is one possible safeguard against confirmation, but it has been evaluated only for PDMS and latency on \texttt{navtest}.

\paragraph{Threats to validity: \texttt{navsafe}.} As discussed in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:navsafe}, \texttt{navsafe} was selected by sorting on Hydra-MDP's performance, so any modification of Hydra-MDP is expected to score higher on it by regression to the mean, and no control, such as an independently trained Hydra-MDP seed without TTT, was evaluated to measure this effect. It is not held out, and whether it lies in the periphery of the data distribution is shown only qualitatively (Fig.~\ref{centaur:fig:navsafe}). Five of its ten categories contain between 5 and 12 frames, no confidence intervals are reported, and no inter-annotator agreement is reported for the category assignments. Its reported Overall scores cannot be recomputed from the category scores (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navsafe}).

\paragraph{Limitations.} As an early attempt, Centaur shows promising performance, but it has further limitations. First, it has not been tested in reactive closed-loop simulation, in the pseudo-simulation of NAVSIM version~2~\cite{cao2025pseudo} or on a vehicle; extending it to a closed-loop setting with an affordable inference budget is a promising direction to explore. Second, test-time compute is quite limited in autonomous driving, which is an efficiency-sensitive task. The measured latency of TTT currently includes the gradient calculation, the asynchronous execution described in Section~\ref{centaur:sec:ttt} has not been implemented, and the latency of Centaur itself was not reported (Section~\ref{centaur:sec:latency}); it is worth exploring ways of unifying TTT and model inference to minimise the compute overhead. Third, Centaur has the highest reported Overall score on \texttt{navsafe}, but it is not the best planner in every category. On YLD it regresses from the 71.6 of Hydra-MDP to 59.3, below both Hydra-SE (84.6) and TransFuser (61.6), a regression that matters for pedestrians and other vulnerable road users. It also trails Hydra-SE (64.4) and LTF (62.8) on BWTH (60.2), and LTF on YLLT (65.0 \vs 70.2). On \texttt{navtest}, its ego progress is slightly lower than that of Hydra-MDP (85.9 \vs 86.5). Finally, the formulation requires a planner whose output exposes a distribution over trajectories, either through trajectory scoring or through an uncertainty-aware regression head (Appendix~\ref{centaur:app:regression}), and only the score decoder is adapted while the perception backbone stays frozen. Test-time adaptation therefore currently acts on the action stage alone; whether perception and reasoning modules can be corrected in the same way is part of the broader architectural question (RQ4) addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{centaur:sec:summary}

This chapter addressed the first part of the third research question (RQ3a): how an end-to-end driving policy can stay reliable when the deployment distribution departs from training, without hand-engineered rules, cost functions or extra labels. We answered it in a restricted form: for per-frame decisions on logged states, and without rules at test time only, since the planner distils rule-based scores during training. We proposed Centaur, which uses test-time training to let a trained trajectory-scoring planner adapt itself. Its objective is Cluster Entropy, an unsupervised and interpretable uncertainty measure that aggregates the predicted scores of sampled candidate trajectories over five lateral driving directions and computes the entropy of this distribution. Before each inference step, Centaur takes a single gradient step on the score decoder, starting from the trained weights and using gradients averaged over a buffer of the four most recent frames; the gradient computation could therefore run asynchronously to inference, although we have not implemented this. We also introduced \texttt{navsafe}, 229 safety-critical \texttt{navtest} frames in ten NHTSA-inspired categories, selected after sorting \texttt{navtest} clips by the performance of Hydra-MDP.

All evaluations use logged states in the non-reactive simulation of NAVSIM v1, so errors cannot compound, and neither behavioural shift nor drift during deployment is tested. In a single training run on \texttt{navtest} with NAVSIM v1.0, Centaur raises the PDMS of Hydra-MDP from 90.3 to 92.6, with the largest sub-score gain in time-to-collision (from 93.9 to 98.0) and a slight loss in ego progress (from 86.5 to 85.9). As reported, the uncertainty-triggered fallback to prior trajectories that we implemented lowers the PDMS to between 63.1 and 65.9 because ego progress falls below 15, but these rows are inconsistent with the fallback's thresholds and must be recomputed. With a mean PDMS of 92.10 $\pm$ 0.33 over three seeds, Centaur ranked first among the three-seed entries on the official NAVSIM v1.1 \texttt{navtest} leaderboard at the time of the paper's submission (March 2025), without ensembling and without tuning training hyper-parameters beyond the Hydra-MDP defaults; there is, however, no matched three-seed baseline of Hydra-MDP without TTT. Its lead over its Semantic Entropy variant Hydra-SE is only 0.23 PDMS, and every TTT variant in the chapter improved its base planner by 0.4 to 2.3 PDMS in single runs, so the gain is better attributed to entropy-style test-time training in general than to Cluster Entropy in particular. At a fixed threshold, Cluster Entropy also gives the highest true-positive rate among the compared measures for flagging failures (62.8\%), but its accuracy (73.6\%) is below that of the trivial Select None baseline (84.4\%), no threshold-free metric is available, and the failure rate must be recomputed. On \texttt{navsafe}, Centaur obtains the highest reported Overall score of the evaluated planners (74.14, against 56.47 for Hydra-MDP), a comparison that favours variants of Hydra-MDP by construction and whose aggregation must be confirmed; it regresses on yielding (from 71.6 to 59.3) and remains far from the 94.65 of human trajectories. TTT also transfers to the regression planner TransFuser (+2.3 PDMS with Semantic Entropy), while the expert-designed cost optimisation of UniAD lowers every sub-score and its PDMS from 83.4 to 75.2. The main limitations are the evaluation on logged states only, the absence of a held-out split, the possibility that the gain reflects a generic re-calibration of score heads that distil the evaluation metric itself, the lateral-only clusters, which cannot represent uncertainty between stopping and proceeding, the added test-time compute, and the inability of entropy minimisation to overturn a confident but wrong prediction.

On logged frames in non-reactive simulation, a trajectory-scoring planner thus benefited from test-time adaptation: a planner that can measure its own uncertainty can turn it into a training signal at test time. Whether the benefit survives in closed loop, where the planner's own actions shape what it sees next, remains open. In robot manipulation, the gap between training and deployment is even wider. Human demonstrations are inconsistent, data and compute are limited, and hardware behaves differently at deployment than during data collection. Chapter~\ref{ch:kai0} tackles this setting.
