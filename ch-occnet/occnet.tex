A physical agent can act only on what it perceives. Before an autonomous vehicle decides to brake, overtake or wait, it has to know which parts of the space around it are occupied, and by what. For years the perception stack of autonomous driving answered this question with a fixed vocabulary: foreground objects were enclosed in 3D bounding boxes drawn from a closed set of categories, and the static background was flattened into a bird's-eye-view (BEV) map. Both choices encode assumptions about the world: that every obstacle that matters belongs to a known category, that a cuboid approximates its shape well enough, and that little of importance happens along the height axis. The curse of reality is that deployment keeps breaking these assumptions. A construction vehicle extends its crane arm far beyond its box, a truck carries a pipe that protrudes from its rear, an overpass or a traffic light hangs above the road, and barriers and vegetation of every shape line the street. A closed box vocabulary oversimplifies or misses exactly these entities, and a BEV map, having discarded height, cannot describe what lies above the ground.

This chapter addresses the first research question of the thesis (RQ1): how should a physical agent represent its 3D surroundings so that the representation stays valid for the open set of objects and structures met in the real world, while remaining learnable from cameras and useful for planning? Our answer is to describe the scene as \emph{3D occupancy}, a geometry-aware representation that quantises the physical 3D space around the vehicle into a structured grid of voxels, each carrying a semantic label and, for foreground objects, a flow vector. Occupancy does not presuppose the shape of an object, it describes foreground objects and background structures in the same grid, and it can be predicted from cameras alone. Its geometry is therefore open, but its semantics are not: every occupied voxel receives one of a fixed set of classes. The central claim of the chapter is that such a geometry-open representation can serve as a general \emph{scene descriptor}: a single intermediate output that supports several driving tasks beyond detection, planning included. We test this claim on one dataset, nuScenes, with open-loop planning metrics and single training runs. The chapter does not test robustness to distribution shift, and the irregular objects it studies are unusual shapes of classes seen in training, not unseen categories.

The chapter makes four contributions. First, we present OccNet\footnote{Not to be confused with the Occupancy Networks of Mescheder \etal~\cite{mescheder2019occupancy}, sometimes also abbreviated OccNet, which represent the surface of a single object implicitly as the decision boundary of a neural classifier.}, a multi-view, vision-centric network that reconstructs occupancy from surround-view images. It builds on a BEV encoder and recovers the height dimension progressively with a cascade voxel decoder, which exploits temporal context through voxel-based attention made affordable by a 3D deformable attention. Second, we build OpenOcc, a dense occupancy benchmark on nuScenes that labels foreground objects and background stuff alike and annotates flow for foreground objects. Third, we use OccNet and OpenOcc to test occupancy as a scene descriptor across the driving stack: semantic scene completion, camera-only LiDAR segmentation, 3D object detection, BEV segmentation and motion planning. On OpenOcc, occupancy features give higher scene-completion accuracy than BEV features and than the strongest baseline we retrained on OpenOcc, and occupancy describes irregular vehicles better than voxelised boxes. In open-loop evaluation on nuScenes, planning on predicted occupancy gave slightly lower collision rates than planning on boxes predicted by OccNet's detection head (0.06--0.24 percentage points, single runs; not statistically established). Fourth, we describe OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene}, which scales occupancy supervision from about 5.5 hours of nuScenes driving to more than 120 hours of nuPlan driving. Its logs and sensor data were later used by NAVSIM, by the end-to-end driving and predictive world-model tracks of the CVPR 2024 Autonomous Grand Challenge, and by the NAVSIM-v2 track of its CVPR 2025 edition; NAVSIM does not use its occupancy labels.

Two components of OccNet come from earlier work. It reuses the BEV encoder of BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer}, which the author co-authored, and its 3D deformable attention is a direct extension of deformable attention~\cite{Zhu2021DeformableDD} to three dimensions, close in spirit to the deformable voxel queries of the concurrent VoxFormer~\cite{li2023voxformer}. What this chapter adds is the cascade that recovers height progressively from BEV features, voxel-level temporal self-attention, the OpenOcc benchmark with its flow annotations, and the use of a single occupancy descriptor across perception and planning tasks.

The rest of the chapter is organised as follows. Section~\ref{occnet:sec:intro} motivates occupancy against the representations it replaces, and Section~\ref{occnet:sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{occnet:sec:method} presents OccNet and the task heads that consume its occupancy descriptor, and Section~\ref{occnet:sec:benchmark} introduces the OpenOcc benchmark and its annotation pipeline. \section{Motivation}\label{occnet:sec:intro}
\suppressfloats[t]

For a human driver, describing the surrounding scene in 3D comes almost for free. A driver would say ``there is a car about 5 metres to my left'', or ``there is a truck carrying a huge protruding gas pipe on the rear, around 50 metres ahead''. Being able to describe the real world in such a ``there is'' form is essential for safe autonomous driving. It is non-trivial for a vision-centric system because of the diversity of entities in a street scene: vehicles such as cars, SUVs and construction trucks, static barriers, pedestrians, and background buildings and vegetation.

  \centering

Defining the 3D world as occupancy~(d) describes such obstacles in more detail. This chapter envisions occupancy as a general scene descriptor~(e) for driving tasks beyond detection, such as planning.}
  \label{occnet:fig:motivation}
\end{figure}

Quantising the 3D scene into structured cells with semantic labels attached, which we term 3D occupancy, is an intuitive way to produce such a description, and industry has advocated related forms: Mobileye represents the scene with VIDAR (``visual LiDAR''), a pseudo-LiDAR point cloud computed from multiple cameras~\cite{mobile2020ces}, and Tesla with occupancy~\cite{tesla_ai_day} (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:motivation}(a)--(b)).\footnote{Mobileye's VIDAR is unrelated to ViDAR~\cite{yang2024vidar}, the visual point-cloud forecasting model on which the predictive world-model track of Section~\ref{occnet:sec:openscene} is based.} \authorcheck{confirm permission to reproduce the Mobileye and Tesla images of Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:motivation}(a)--(b) for deposit in the HKU Scholars Hub} Compared with a 3D box, which oversimplifies the shape of an object, occupancy is geometry-aware: it depicts objects and background of different shapes as collections of cubes with different geometric structures. As Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:motivation}(c)--(d) illustrates, a 3D box can describe only the main body of a construction vehicle, whereas occupancy preserves the detail of its crane arm.

Table~\ref{occnet:tab:compare_to_other_form} compares occupancy with the representations that are widely deployed in driving. A 3D box covers only foreground objects. Point-cloud segmentation offers the finest granularity, around 0.02\,m, but requires LiDAR input and is therefore limited by cost. BEV segmentation covers objects and background, but its lack of a height dimension limits its granularity. Occupancy unifies foreground objects and background stuff in a dense 3D voxel space with a granularity of 0.25--0.5\,m, without requiring a point cloud as input. These advantages call for an investigation of whether occupancy can improve conventional perception tasks and downstream planning.

Occupancy has been discussed before, but only at an initial stage. In robotics, the occupancy grid map is a typical representation for mobile navigation~\cite{moras2015grid}, but it serves only as the search space of planning. In computer vision, 3D semantic scene completion (SSC)~\cite{song2017sscnet} can be regarded as a perception task that evaluates the idea of occupancy. Vision-based methods for this task~\cite{huang2023tpvformer,li2023voxformer,cao2022monoscene,miao2023occdepth} leave two opportunities open. Most of them do not exploit temporal information, although history frames are an intuitive geometric prior for a vision-centric model that has to reconstruct geometry-aware occupancy; the temporal variant of VoxFormer~\cite{li2023voxformer} is an exception. And, being one-stage methods~\cite{huang2023tpvformer,miao2023occdepth,cao2022monoscene}, they ignore a coarse-to-fine scheme, which can improve the 3D geometric representation at an affordable cost. Beyond model design, the community then lacked a practical way to evaluate occupancy in a full-stack spirit, as vision-centric pipelines that connect perception to planning~\cite{hu2023uniad} came to prevail.

To address these issues, we propose OccNet, a multi-view, vision-centric pipeline with a cascade voxel decoder that reconstructs 3D occupancy with the aid of temporal clues, followed by task-specific heads for a wide range of driving tasks. The core of OccNet is a compact and representative occupancy embedding of the 3D scene. Rather than generating voxel features directly from image features, or using BEV features alone as in previous work~\cite{li2022bevformer,chen2022persformer,wang2022monocular}, OccNet decodes the occupancy feature from the BEV feature in a cascade fashion. The decoder recovers the height information progressively with voxel-based temporal self-attention and spatial cross-attention, bundled with a 3D deformable attention module for efficiency. Equipped with this descriptor, OccNet supports 3D occupancy prediction, 3D detection and BEV segmentation, and it facilitates motion planning. For a fair comparison across methods, we build OpenOcc, a benchmark with dense and high-quality annotations on the nuScenes dataset~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes,fong2022panoptic}. It comprises 34,149 annotated frames with over 1.4 billion occupancy cells, each assigned one of 16 classes that describe foreground objects and background stuff. It also annotates the motion of foreground objects with flow vectors, which we provide for future use in prediction and planning; the experiments of this chapter do not evaluate flow prediction, and the planner does not use flow.

Evaluating OccNet on OpenOcc, we obtain three results, each with qualifications that Sections~\ref{occnet:sec:experiments} and~\ref{occnet:sec:discussion} make precise.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Perception.} On semantic scene completion, OccNet improves the mIoU of the strongest baseline we retrained on OpenOcc, TPVFormer~\cite{huang2023tpvformer}, by 14\% relative (26.98 \vs 23.67). From cameras alone, it segments LiDAR points about 5 mIoU points below the LiDAR-based RangeNet++~\cite{milioto2019rangenet++}. Pre-training on occupancy helps 3D detection when only 1/256 to 1/64 of the training labels are used for fine-tuning; the largest gain over a detector initialised from FCOS3D~\cite{wang2021fcos3d}, about 10 mAP points, occurs at 1/128.
  \item \emph{Planning.} In open-loop evaluation on nuScenes, planning on predicted occupancy gave slightly lower collision rates than planning on boxes predicted by OccNet's detection head (0.06--0.24 percentage points, single runs; not statistically established), without a consistent gain in L2 distance.
  \item \emph{Density of supervision.} Pre-training on dense occupancy transferred to detection better than pre-training on sparse occupancy at one of the three training-set sizes tested, and about equally at the other two.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{occnet:sec:related}

This section positions occupancy against the output representations of driving perception and against the tasks closest to it, as they stood when this work was done. Broader background on camera-based 3D perception is given in Chapter~\ref{ch:background} and in the survey of BEV perception~\cite{li2023bevdevils} (co-authored by the author), and the occupancy research that followed this work is discussed in Section~\ref{occnet:sec:subsequent}.

\paragraph{3D object detection.} Previous work~\cite{Shi2020PVRCNNPF,wang2021fcos3d,li2022bevformer,liu2023bevfusion} adopts 3D boxes as the objective of perception in driving, since the box form is well structured for downstream rule-based approaches. Such a representation abstracts objects of different shapes into standardised cuboids; it therefore cares only about foreground objects and oversimplifies their shapes. Occupancy, in contrast, is a fine-grained description of the physical world that can differentiate objects of various shapes.

\paragraph{LiDAR segmentation.} LiDAR segmentation~\cite{zhu2021cylindrical,milioto2019rangenet++} addresses point-level 3D scene understanding. It requires a point cloud as input, which is expensive and less portable. Since LiDAR inherently suffers from a limited sensing range and a sparse coverage of the scene, such a pipeline is not well suited to holistic semantic understanding of the 3D scene~\cite{song2017sscnet}.

\paragraph{BEV segmentation.} BEV segmentation~\cite{zhou2022cross,li2022hdmapnet} implicitly squeezes the height information into each cell of the BEV map. In challenging urban settings, however, explicit height information is necessary to capture entities above the ground, \eg traffic lights and overpasses. Occupancy, as an alternative, is aware of 3D geometry.

\paragraph{3D reconstruction and rendering.} Inferring the 3D geometry of objects or scenes from 2D images~\cite{Hartley2004,mildenhall2020nerf} has been a prevalent yet challenging problem in computer vision for many years. Most approaches in this domain~\cite{mueller2022instant,cao2022scenerf,Tancik2022blocknerf}, including implicit occupancy functions learned for single objects~\cite{mescheder2019occupancy}, cope with a single object or scene, which is not feasible for driving, where strong generalisation is required. Moreover, 3D reconstruction and rendering concentrate on the quality of the scene geometry and of the visual appearance, and pay less attention to model efficiency and semantic understanding.

\paragraph{Semantic scene completion.} The occupancy prediction studied in this chapter most resembles SSC~\cite{song2017sscnet}. MonoScene~\cite{cao2022monoscene} first adopted a U-Net to infer dense 3D occupancy with semantic labels from a single monocular RGB image. Several related methods appeared concurrently with our work. VoxFormer~\cite{li2023voxformer} uses depth estimation to set voxel queries in a two-stage framework. OccDepth~\cite{miao2023occdepth} also follows a depth-aware design, in a stereo setting with distillation, to predict semantic occupancy. TPVFormer~\cite{huang2023tpvformer} uses LiDAR-based sparse occupancy as supervision and proposes a tri-perspective view representation to obtain features. Wang \etal~\cite{wang2023openoccupancy} provide a carefully human-crafted occupancy benchmark for the community. Many of these works were developed on SemanticKITTI~\cite{behley2019semantickitti} and NYUv2~\cite{Silberman2012nyuv2}, with monocular or RGB-D input, a setting different from ours. Moreover, most prior single-frame methods, \eg MonoScene and TPVFormer, do not exploit temporal context; the temporal variant of VoxFormer (VoxFormer-T)~\cite{li2023voxformer} and the BEV detector BEVDet4D~\cite{huang2022bevdet4d}, one of our baselines, are exceptions. Using history voxel features had been advocated by Tesla~\cite{tesla_ai_day}, yet no technical details had been made public. At the time of publication, we positioned this work as the first to investigate occupancy as a general descriptor that can improve multiple tasks beyond detection.

\section{OccNet}\label{occnet:sec:method}
\suppressfloats[t]

OccNet obtains occupancy features from images and supports multiple driving tasks, as shown in Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:framework}. It comprises two stages, the \emph{reconstruction of occupancy} and the \emph{exploitation of occupancy}. We term the part that bridges them the \emph{occupancy descriptor}, a unified description of the driving scene.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ch-occnet/images/fig_pipeline_overview_v8_used.pdf}
  \caption[The OccNet pipeline]{\textbf{The OccNet pipeline.} OccNet obtains a representative occupancy descriptor and applies it to various driving tasks, in two stages. \textbf{I.~Reconstruction of occupancy.} Given multi-view images, a BEV encoder first generates BEV features. The voxel decoder then operates in a cascade fashion, refining voxels progressively; a 3D deformable attention unit plays the role that deformable attention plays in 2D. Temporal voxels $V'_{t-1,i}$ are also incorporated. Some connections are omitted for brevity. \textbf{II.~Exploitation of occupancy.} The occupancy descriptor supports tasks such as semantic scene completion and 3D object detection. Compacting their outputs in BEV space yields a BEV segmentation map, which is fed directly into the planning pipeline~\cite{hu2022stp3}.}
  \label{occnet:fig:framework}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Reconstruction of occupancy.} The goal of this stage is a representative occupancy descriptor that supports downstream tasks. Motivated by the fast development of BEV perception~\cite{li2022bevformer,chen2022persformer,liang2022bevfusion}, OccNet is designed to carry this progress over to voxel-wise prediction in 3D space. Two extreme designs are possible. Using the BEV feature alone in downstream tasks is the simplest architecture, but it is not suitable for height-aware tasks in 3D space. At the other extreme, constructing voxel features directly from images incurs a huge computational cost. We term these two extremes \emph{BEVNet} and \emph{VoxelNet}.\footnote{Both names denote our own baselines only. Our VoxelNet is unrelated to the LiDAR detector VoxelNet of Zhou and Tuzel~\cite{zhou2018voxelnet}.} OccNet strikes a balance between them: in our single-run comparison it is slightly more accurate than VoxelNet at a lower cost, and more accurate than BEVNet at a higher cost (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:analysis}). The reconstruction stage first extracts multi-view features $F_t$ from the surround-view images and feeds them, together with the history BEV feature $B_{t-1}$ and the current BEV query $Q_t$, into a BEV encoder. The encoder follows the structure of BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer}: $B_{t-1}$, $Q_t$ and $F_t$ pass through a spatial-temporal transformer block that produces the current BEV feature $B_t$. The image feature and the history and current BEV features are then decoded together into the occupancy descriptor by the \emph{cascade voxel decoder} of Section~\ref{occnet:sec:cascade_voxel_decoder}.

\paragraph{Exploitation of occupancy.} A wide range of driving tasks can be built on the reconstructed occupancy descriptor. Inspired by UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad} (co-authored by the author), we prefer an explicit design for each representation. 3D semantic scene completion~\cite{song2017sscnet} and 3D object detection are attached directly to the occupancy descriptor. Squeezing the 3D occupancy grid and the 3D boxes along the height axis yields a BEV segmentation map. This map is fed into the motion planning head together with a sampler conditioned on the high-level command, and the ego trajectory results from an \emph{argmin} over the sampled trajectories followed by a GRU module. Section~\ref{occnet:sec:exploitation_of_occupancy} details the task heads.

\subsection{Cascade Voxel Decoder}\label{occnet:sec:cascade_voxel_decoder}

To obtain a better voxel feature effectively and efficiently, we design a cascade structure in the decoder that recovers the height information of the voxel feature progressively.

\paragraph{From BEV to cascaded voxels.} Using the BEV feature directly, or reconstructing the voxel feature directly from the perspective view, costs performance or efficiency, respectively (see the ablation in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:efficiency}). We therefore break the reconstruction from the BEV feature $B_t\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C_{\text{BEV}}}$ to the desired voxel feature $V_t\in\mathbb{R}^{Z\times H\times W\times C_{\text{Voxel}}}$ into $N$ steps, which we call a cascade structure. Here $H$ and $W$ are the spatial dimensions of the BEV plane, $C_{\text{BEV}}$ and $C_{\text{Voxel}}$ are feature dimensions, and $Z$ is the desired height of the voxel space. We denote the intermediate voxel feature of the $i$-th step by $V'_{t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{Z_i\times H\times W\times C_i}$. Along the cascade, the height $Z_i$ grows towards $Z$ while the channel number $C_i$ shrinks from $C_{\text{BEV}}$ towards $C_{\text{Voxel}}$; the schedule used in our experiments is given in Section~\ref{occnet:sec:exp_setup}. As shown in Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:framework}, $B_{t-1}$ and $B_t$ are lifted to $V'_{t-1,i}$ and $V'_{t,i}$ by a feed-forward network and pass through the $i$-th voxel decoder, which outputs a refined $V'_{t,i}$; the later steps follow the same scheme, with an MLP transforming the feature dimension from $Z_i\times C_i$ to $Z_{i+1}\times C_{i+1}$ between consecutive steps (Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:implementation}). Each voxel decoder comprises a voxel-based temporal self-attention module, which refines $V'_{t,i}$ with the history feature $V'_{t-1,i}$, and a voxel-based spatial cross-attention module, which refines it with the image feature $F_t$. Step by step, the model increases $Z_i$ and decreases $C_i$, learning the final occupancy descriptor $V_t$ effectively and efficiently.

\paragraph{Voxel-based temporal self-attention.} Temporal information helps BEV perception~\cite{li2022bevformer}, and history frames are a natural geometric prior for occupancy. In our ablation, however, the gain is modest: two history frames, the default, add 0.27 mIoU over none, and four frames add 1.60 mIoU (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:frame_number_ssc}, single runs). Given the history voxel feature $V'_{t-1,i}$, we align it to the current occupancy feature $V'_{t,i}$ using the position of the ego vehicle. In standard self-attention, each query attends to every key and value, so the computational cost is huge, and in 3D space it grows by a factor of $Z^2$ compared with the 2D case. To alleviate this burden, we design an efficient voxel-based attention, termed \emph{3D deformable attention} (3D-DA). Applied in the voxel-based temporal self-attention, it lets each voxel query interact only with local voxels of interest, which keeps the computational cost affordable.

\paragraph{3D deformable attention.} We extend 2D deformable attention~\cite{Zhu2021DeformableDD} to 3D. Given a voxel feature $V'_{t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{Z_i\times H\times W\times C_i}$, a voxel query with feature $\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_i}$ and a 3D reference point $\boldsymbol{p}$, 3D deformable attention is computed as
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{3D-DA}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p},V'_{t,i})
  = \sum_{m=1}^{M} W_m \sum_{k=1}^{K} A_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})\, W'_m\, V'_{t,i}\big(\boldsymbol{p}+\Delta\boldsymbol{p}_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})\big),
  \label{occnet:eq:3dda}
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the number of attention heads, $K\ll Z_iHW$ is the number of sampled keys per head, and $W_m\in\mathbb{R}^{C_i\times(C_i/M)}$ and $W'_m\in\mathbb{R}^{(C_i/M)\times C_i}$ are the learnable output and value projections of head $m$. As in 2D deformable attention, the attention weights $A_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})$, normalised so that $\sum_{k}A_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})=1$, and the sampling offsets $\Delta\boldsymbol{p}_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})$ are predicted from the query feature; the feature at the sampling location $\boldsymbol{p}+\Delta\boldsymbol{p}_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})$ is computed from the voxel feature by trilinear interpolation. \authorcheck{give the values of $M$ and $K$ used in 3D-DA and how the reference points and sampling offsets are initialised}

\paragraph{Voxel-based spatial cross-attention.} In the cross-attention, the voxel feature $V'_{t,i}$ interacts with the multi-scale image features $F_t$ through 2D deformable attention~\cite{Zhu2021DeformableDD}. The $i$-th decoder samples $N_{\text{ref},i}$ 3D points directly from each voxel, projects them to the image views, and interacts with the sampled image features. Whereas a BEV query in BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer} samples its reference points along a pillar, this design maintains the height information and ensures that a voxel-wise feature is learned.

\subsection{Exploiting Occupancy for Driving Tasks}\label{occnet:sec:exploitation_of_occupancy}

OccNet depicts the scene in 3D space with a fine-grained occupancy descriptor, which can be fed into various driving tasks without excessive computational overhead.

\paragraph{Semantic scene completion.} For simplicity, an MLP head predicts the semantic label of each voxel. It is trained with the focal loss~\cite{Lin2017FocalLF} to balance the huge numerical imbalance between occupied and empty voxels. In addition, a flow head trained with an $L_1$ loss estimates the flow velocity of each occupied voxel.

\paragraph{3D object detection.} Inspired by the head design of BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer}, we compact the occupancy descriptor into BEV and apply a query-based detection head, a variant of Deformable DETR~\cite{Zhu2021DeformableDD}, to predict 3D boxes.

\paragraph{BEV segmentation.} Following the spatial-temporal fusion perception structure of ST-P3~\cite{hu2022stp3}, map representation and semantic segmentation are predicted from the BEV feature, as in 3D object detection. The BEV segmentation head comprises a drivable-area head and a lane head for the map representation, and a vehicle segmentation head and a pedestrian segmentation head for semantic segmentation.

\paragraph{Motion planning.} For planning, either the occupancy predicted by SSC or the predicted 3D boxes are transformed into a BEV segmentation map (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:framework}). The occupancy, or the set of boxes, is squeezed along the height dimension, and the semantic labels of each BEV cell are turned into a binary format, where 1 indicates that the cell is occupied and 0 that it is empty. This map enters the safety cost function, and the safety, comfort and progress costs are computed for the sampled trajectories. Compared with 3D boxes, the richer background information of occupancy leads to a more comprehensive safety cost, so the safety cost value has to be normalised between the two kinds of BEV maps. \authorcheck{specify how the safety cost was normalised between box and occupancy maps (formula or constants) and how the normalisation was chosen} This normalisation directly affects the collision rates of Section~\ref{occnet:sec:main_results}, and we did not measure their sensitivity to it. The planner uses neither the flow annotations nor predicted flow. All candidate trajectories are sampled with random velocity, acceleration and curvature. Under the guidance of a high-level command (forward, turn left or turn right), the trajectory with the lowest cost for that command is selected. A GRU refinement that uses the front-view vision feature, as in ST-P3~\cite{hu2022stp3}, then produces the final trajectory.

\section{The OpenOcc Benchmark}\label{occnet:sec:benchmark}

To evaluate occupancy fairly across methods, we introduce OpenOcc, a dense, high-quality 3D occupancy benchmark built on top of the widely used nuScenes dataset~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes,fong2022panoptic}. OpenOcc was one of the first dense occupancy benchmarks on nuScenes, concurrent with Occ3D~\cite{tian2023occ3d}, OpenOccupancy~\cite{wang2023openoccupancy} and SurroundOcc~\cite{wei2023surroundocc}, and, unlike them, it provides flow annotations for foreground objects (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:openscene}). Compared with existing counterparts such as SemanticKITTI~\cite{behley2019semantickitti}, which provides only a front camera, OpenOcc provides surround-view camera images with the corresponding 3D occupancy and flow annotations. The label conventions of these benchmarks differ in voxel size, spatial range, class set and the handling of voxels invisible to the cameras, so scores are not comparable across them; the CVPR 2023 occupancy challenge, for example, used the Occ3D-nuScenes labels rather than OpenOcc (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:subsequent}).

\subsection{Benchmark Overview}\label{occnet:sec:benchmark_overview}

OpenOcc provides dense, high-quality occupancy annotations generated from the sparse LiDAR data and the 3D boxes of nuScenes. It comprises 34,149 annotated frames covering all 700 training and 150 validation scenes. Over 1.4 billion voxels are annotated with 16 classes: 10 foreground object classes (barrier, bicycle, bus, car, construction vehicle, motorcycle, pedestrian, traffic cone, trailer and truck) and 6 background stuff classes (driveable surface, other flat, sidewalk, terrain, manmade and vegetation). In addition, the motion of foreground objects is captured by flow annotations on the object voxels. Occupancy is defined within the volume $\mathcal{V}=[-50\,\text{m},50\,\text{m}]\times[-50\,\text{m},50\,\text{m}]\times[-5\,\text{m},3\,\text{m}]$ in the LiDAR coordinate system, voxelised at a resolution of $\Delta s=0.5$\,m into $200\times200\times16$ voxels.

\subsection{Generating High-Quality Annotations}\label{occnet:sec:annotation}

\paragraph{Independent accumulation of background and foreground.} To generate a dense representation, it is intuitive to accumulate all the sparse LiDAR points of the key frames and intermediate frames~\cite{behley2019semantickitti}. Directly accumulating points from intermediate frames by coordinate transformation is problematic, however, because of moving objects. We therefore split the LiDAR points into static background points and foreground points using the 3D boxes, and accumulate the two separately: the static background points in the global world coordinate system, and the object points in the coordinate system of each object. Since nuScenes provides no boxes for intermediate frames, we approximate them by linear interpolation between the two adjacent key frames (Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:openocc}).

\paragraph{Generation of annotations.} Given the dense background and object points, we voxelise the 3D space and label each voxel by a majority vote of the labelled points inside it. Unlike existing benchmarks that provide only occupancy labels, we also annotate the flow velocity of each voxel from the velocity of its 3D box, to facilitate downstream tasks such as motion planning. Using key frames alone would leave the generated occupancy sparse, so we further annotate voxels that contain unlabelled LiDAR points from intermediate frames, based on the surrounding labelled voxels, which increases the density. Because nuScenes has the issue of missing translation along the $z$-axis, we refine the occupancy by completing the scene, \eg by filling holes in the road, for higher quality. Finally, we mark part of the voxels as invisible from the camera views by ray tracing, which makes the benchmark more suitable for camera-based tasks. Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:occ_gt_pipeline} in Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:openocc} illustrates these steps. \authorcheck{give the pipeline parameters (box interpolation including yaw, majority-vote rule, neighbourhood used to label intermediate-frame points, hole-filling rule, ray-casting procedure) and the fraction of voxels marked invisible, and state whether any labels were verified by hand, since Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} describes the benchmarks as human-verified whereas no manual step is described here} This chapter reports no manual audit of label quality.

\section{Experiments}\label{occnet:sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{occnet:sec:exp_setup}

\paragraph{Implementation.} Following the experimental setting of BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer}, we use two backbones: ResNet50~\cite{he2016deep} initialised from ImageNet~\cite{deng2009imagenet}, and ResNet101-DCN~\cite{he2016deep} initialised from FCOS3D~\cite{wang2021fcos3d}. The BEV feature $B_t$ has $H=W=200$ and $C_{\text{BEV}}=256$. The decoder has $N=4$ occupancy feature maps $V'_{t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{Z_i\times H\times W\times C_i}$ with $Z_i=2^i$, $C_1=C_2=128$ and $C_3=C_4=64$. The voxel-based spatial cross-attention samples $N_{\text{ref},i}=4$ points in each queried voxel. By default, OccNet is trained for 24 epochs with a learning rate of $2\times10^{-4}$, and the temporal self-attention uses two history frames. The frame ablation of Table~\ref{occnet:tab:frame_number_ssc} finds four history frames better by 1.33 mIoU (20.81 \vs 19.48), so the main results do not use the best setting of that ablation. \authorcheck{state why two history frames were chosen as the default} Further implementation details, including those of BEVNet and VoxelNet, are given in Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:implementation}.

\paragraph{Tasks and metrics.} Semantic scene completion is evaluated on OpenOcc with the mean intersection-over-union (mIoU) over the 16 classes and the class-agnostic geometric $\text{IoU}_{\text{geo}}$. Both are voxel-level metrics; the ray-based RayIoU~\cite{liu2024sparseocc}, proposed later to remove the inconsistent penalty that voxel-level mIoU applies to depth errors, is not reported (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:subsequent}). LiDAR segmentation and 3D detection are evaluated on the nuScenes validation set, the latter with the official nuScenes detection metrics. BEV segmentation is evaluated by IoU, and planning is evaluated open-loop following ST-P3~\cite{hu2022stp3}, which reports the L2 distance to the ground-truth trajectory and the collision rate. On nuScenes, open-loop planning metrics can be satisfied to a large extent from the ego vehicle's own state~\cite{zhai2023rethinking,li2024ego} (Chapter~\ref{ch:background}), so they measure how plausible a plan is on logged data rather than how well a vehicle would drive. Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:metrics} defines the metrics. Every result of our own in this chapter comes from a single training run, and no confidence intervals are reported.

\paragraph{Pre-trained occupancy for 3D detection and BEV segmentation.} An OccNet trained on semantic scene completion obtains a general representation of 3D space, because the scene is reconstructed in its occupancy descriptor. The learned descriptor can therefore be transferred to downstream 3D perception tasks by fine-tuning. As Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:pretrain_for_detection} shows, when fine-tuning on 1/256, 1/128 or 1/64 of the training set, a detector initialised from OccNet outperforms one initialised from the FCOS3D~\cite{wang2021fcos3d} detector. The largest gap, about 10 mAP points (read from the plot), occurs at 1/128. Three qualifications apply. First, the comparison confounds the pre-training task with the number of pre-trained parameters: FCOS3D, a monocular detector without BEV or voxel stages, can initialise only the image feature extractor, whereas occupancy pre-training also initialises the BEV encoder and the voxel decoder, and with the ResNet101 backbone OccNet is itself initialised from FCOS3D. A detection-pretrained copy of the same architecture would be the cleaner control. Second, the FCOS3D-initialised detector reaches an mAP close to zero at 1/256 and 1/128, which suggests unstable fine-tuning at these sizes, and no fraction above 1/64, including the full training set, was tested. Third, the occupancy labels used for pre-training are generated from the 3D boxes and LiDAR of the nuScenes training set, so the experiment measures how well each initialisation transfers when few detection labels are available for fine-tuning; it is not evidence that occupancy pre-training reduces the total annotation cost. \authorcheck{state which detector architecture was fine-tuned, confirm that occupancy pre-training used the full OpenOcc training split, and add the full-data fine-tuning result if it exists} For BEV segmentation, occupancy pre-training also yields a higher IoU after fine-tuning than detection pre-training, on both semantic and map segmentation (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:bev_seg}): the main value rises from 18.18 to 19.17 and the drivable-area IoU from 44.59 to 47.21, in single runs.

\paragraph{Occupancy for planning.} With the predictions of the upstream tasks, \ie boxes or occupancy, the final trajectory is obtained through the cost filter and the GRU refinement module of ST-P3~\cite{hu2022stp3} from BEV segmentation inputs, which we obtain by rasterising the outputs of OccNet in BEV space. We compare the rasterised occupancy predicted by OccNet with the rasterised boxes predicted by OccNet's detection head, and with the direct BEV segmentation of ST-P3, which we re-ran. For comparability with ST-P3, we keep only the vehicle and pedestrian classes. The evaluation is open-loop, on nuScenes with the ST-P3 protocol: the planned trajectory is not executed but compared with the ground-truth trajectory and with the ground-truth future positions of vehicles and pedestrians, so the scene does not react to the plan. Each configuration was trained and evaluated once, and rasterised ground-truth inputs are reported for reference. As Table~\ref{occnet:tab:planning} shows, filtering trajectories with ground-truth occupancy gives the lowest collision rates. Among predicted inputs, occupancy gives the lowest collision rate at every horizon: 0.21\%, 0.55\% and 1.35\% at 1\,s, 2\,s and 3\,s, against 0.27\%, 0.68\% and 1.59\% with predicted boxes. In open-loop evaluation on nuScenes, planning on predicted occupancy thus gave slightly lower collision rates than planning on boxes predicted by OccNet's detection head (0.06--0.24 percentage points, single runs; not statistically established). The corresponding relative reductions, 15\%--22\%, come from these small absolute differences. Even if all of the roughly 6,000 validation frames were evaluated (computed from the 34,149 frames of 850 scenes in Section~\ref{occnet:sec:benchmark_overview}, assuming scenes of equal length), a difference of 0.24 percentage points would correspond to about 14 frames and one of 0.06 points to about 4; we computed neither a paired test on per-frame outcomes nor a confidence interval. \authorcheck{give the number of evaluated frames and the per-horizon collision counts for each input, and confirm that the boxes come from the jointly trained OccNet of Table~\ref{occnet:tab:joint_train}}

\paragraph{How strong is the planning evidence?} Four further observations temper the result. First, L2 does not improve consistently. With the two classes of ST-P3, predicted occupancy gives a larger L2 distance at 3\,s than predicted boxes (3.07\,m \vs 3.03\,m), which fits more conservative plans rather than better perception. The 16-class variant has the lowest L2 among predicted inputs (1.29\,m, 2.13\,m and 2.99\,m) but collides more often than the two-class variant at 2\,s and 3\,s, so no predicted input is best on both metrics. We measured neither a conservativeness indicator, such as mean planned speed or progress, nor an ego-status-only reference, which is competitive on nuScenes in open-loop evaluation~\cite{zhai2023rethinking,li2024ego}. Second, run-to-run variation is of the same order as the differences between inputs: the planner runs reported in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:occupancy_metrics} give L2 distances that differ from those of Table~\ref{occnet:tab:planning} for the same inputs by up to 0.05\,m at 3\,s (Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:experiments}). Third, the box input is OccNet's auxiliary detection head, whose mAP is 0.276 (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:joint_train}), not a strong standalone detector. The comparison is therefore between OccNet's occupancy output and OccNet's detections, not between occupancy and boxes in general; with ground-truth inputs, where detector quality plays no role, occupancy lowers the collision rate by only 0.03 to 0.20 percentage points. Fourth, the collision rates depend on the normalisation of the safety cost between box and occupancy maps (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:exploitation_of_occupancy}), whose sensitivity we did not measure.

\paragraph{Comparison with the published ST-P3.} Under the same protocol, ST-P3 reports collision rates of 0.23\%, 0.62\% and 1.27\% and L2 distances of 1.33\,m, 2.11\,m and 2.90\,m at 1\,s, 2\,s and 3\,s~\cite{hu2022stp3}, clearly better than our re-run (0.50\%, 0.88\% and 1.49\%; 1.39\,m, 2.21\,m and 3.02\,m). Against the published numbers, planning on predicted occupancy collides less at 1\,s and 2\,s (0.21\% \vs 0.23\% and 0.55\% \vs 0.62\%) but more at 3\,s (1.35\% \vs 1.27\%), and its L2 distances are larger at 2\,s and 3\,s (2.18\,m \vs 2.11\,m and 3.07\,m \vs 2.90\,m). Even the ground-truth inputs of our re-run collide more at 3\,s (1.30\% and 1.50\%) than the published ST-P3 with predicted inputs. \authorcheck{list how our ST-P3 re-run and our planner differ from the published ST-P3 (backbone, input resolution, training schedule, GRU refinement, cost normalisation)} Until these differences are explained, the comparison with BEV segmentation (collision rates lower by 9\%--58\% in relative terms) rests on a re-run that is weaker than the published model, and the defensible comparison is the like-for-like one between occupancy and boxes predicted by the same OccNet. As Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:planning} illustrates, planning with full-class occupancy keeps the trajectory within the feasible area and away from background objects in the example shown.

\paragraph{Irregular objects.} Representing irregular objects such as construction vehicles, or background stuff such as traffic signs, with 3D boxes is difficult and inaccurate, as Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:irregular} shows. To compare detection and occupancy on irregular objects, we transform 3D boxes into voxels and evaluate both tasks on truck, trailer and construction vehicle (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:irregular}). Occupancy describes these objects better, with an $\text{mIoU}_{\text{ir}}$ of 18.13 against 15.92 for boxes at $\Delta s=0.5$\,m. To study the effect of voxel size, we also generate the dataset at $\Delta s=0.25$\,m. As $\Delta s$ decreases from 0.5\,m to 0.25\,m, the gap between boxes and occupancy grows from 2.21 to 3.51 points, which is consistent with finer voxels depicting irregular shapes better.

Two properties of this comparison favour occupancy independently of object shape. First, the object points of OpenOcc are accumulated inside the (interpolated) box of each object (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:annotation}); if the ground-truth voxels of an object come only from these points, they lie within its box and cover mainly its visible surface, whereas a detected box converted to voxels fills the whole cuboid. Part of the gap in $\text{mIoU}_{\text{ir}}$ may therefore measure the mismatch between a filled cuboid and a surface-like label rather than irregular shape. \authorcheck{state how LiDAR points of an object that lie outside its box, such as the crane arm of Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:motivation}(d), are labelled and accumulated, whether object voxels are surfaces or filled volumes, and how the detected boxes were voxelised} Second, the comparison contains no regular classes, such as car and bus, as a control. Recomputing $\text{mIoU}_{\text{ir}}$ with boxes voxelised as surfaces, or restricted to visible surface voxels, and adding car and bus would separate the two effects; we have not done this.

\paragraph{Dense versus sparse occupancy.} Compared with sparse occupancy, dense occupancy depicts the complete geometry of background and foreground objects in detail (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:compare_gt}). We therefore expected dense occupancy to be better for 3D perception and motion planning, because it provides more information. Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:pretrain_for_detection} supports this only in part: pre-training on dense occupancy transfers better to detection than pre-training on sparse occupancy when fine-tuning on 1/128 of the training set (about 0.11 \vs 0.09 mAP, read from the plot), whereas the two are close at 1/256 and at 1/64, where sparse pre-training is marginally ahead in mAP. One fine-tuning curve from single runs is weak evidence, and the effect of label density on planning was not tested.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{occnet:sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Experiments Show}\label{occnet:sec:disc_show}

\paragraph{What occupancy provides.} The experiments bear on the three requirements that RQ1 places on a scene representation, with evidence of different strength. The first is validity beyond a closed vocabulary of boxes. Because occupancy does not presuppose object shape, it describes irregular vehicles better than voxelised boxes (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:irregular}), subject to the label-construction caveat of Section~\ref{occnet:sec:analysis}, and it covers the background structures that boxes ignore. The planner of Section~\ref{occnet:sec:exploitation_of_occupancy} consumes binarised occupancy, so a cell contributes to the safety cost whenever it is predicted as occupied, whatever its semantic class; with all 16 classes, the planned trajectory keeps away from background objects in the example of Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:planning}. The semantic classes, however, are a fixed set of 16: occupancy is a geometry-open descriptor with closed semantics, and its openness lies in geometry rather than in recognition. The second requirement is learnability from cameras: OccNet predicts occupancy from images alone, and when its prediction is queried at LiDAR points it segments them about 5 mIoU points below the LiDAR-based RangeNet++ (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:lidarseg}). The third is usefulness for planning: in open-loop evaluation on nuScenes, planning on predicted occupancy gave slightly lower collision rates than planning on boxes predicted by the same network (0.06--0.24 percentage points, single runs; not statistically established), without a consistent gain in L2 (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:main_results}). This is indicative rather than conclusive evidence of usefulness for planning.

\paragraph{Relation to the occupancy results of Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}.} Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} reports two NAVSIM results that involve occupancy and point the other way (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}). UniAD's test-time trajectory optimisation, which uses its predicted occupancy in a hand-designed cost, lowers its PDMS from 83.4 to 75.2 and lowers every sub-score, including no-collision (97.8 to 95.4), drivable-area compliance (91.9 to 86.0), time-to-collision (92.9 to 87.3) and ego progress (78.8 to 71.9). Adding an occupancy head to PARA-Drive changes its PDMS from 84.0 to 83.6, with a slightly higher time-to-collision score (93.0 to 93.7) and lower ego progress (79.3 to 78.3). These results come from a higher fidelity level (non-reactive simulation, L1, against open-loop evaluation, L0, here), but from single training seeds, other architectures and other uses of occupancy: in Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} occupancy is an optimisation cost or an auxiliary training target, whereas here it is the input of a sampling-based planner. Neither result tests the planner of this chapter, and this chapter does not test theirs. Taken together, the evidence of this thesis does not show that occupancy improves planning in general: whether it helps depends on how the planner uses it, and the only positive evidence is the small open-loop effect reported here.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}\label{occnet:sec:limitations}

Several limitations qualify these conclusions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Dependence on an annotated dataset.} The annotations rely on the LiDAR points and 3D boxes of nuScenes, including boxes interpolated for intermediate frames, and no manual audit of their quality is reported. Self-supervised and rendering-based supervision, which later work has pursued (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:subsequent}), could reduce this dependence.
  \item \emph{A single benchmark, built by us.} All perception results are on OpenOcc, whose label conventions differ from those of Occ3D-nuScenes, SurroundOcc and OpenOccupancy, and the baselines are the strongest we could retrain on it; the re-implemented BEV baselines appear under-tuned. We report no result on an independent benchmark and no quantitative comparison with concurrent or later methods, so the bias of a benchmark designed by the authors of a method, listed among the threats to validity in Section~\ref{sec:method:validity}, applies to every comparison in this chapter.
  \item \emph{Metrics.} Scene completion is scored with voxel-level mIoU and $\text{IoU}_{\text{geo}}$ only; RayIoU, which penalises depth errors consistently, is not reported. LiDAR segmentation is evaluated at the ground-truth LiDAR points, so it tests semantics at known surface points rather than the full geometry predicted from cameras.
  \item \emph{Resolution.} A voxel size of 0.5\,m may be too coarse for box metrics that depend on precise centres and sizes (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:joint_train}), whereas finer voxels describe irregular objects better (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:irregular}) and gave higher LiDAR segmentation scores (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:lidarseg_resolution}, subject to the inconsistency noted in Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:experiments}). The resolution of the representation therefore matters, and 0.5\,m is not fine enough for every use.
  \item \emph{Not every use of occupancy helps.} Features pre-trained on occupancy did not improve the planner over detection pre-training (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:planning_pretrain}), although the absolute values of that table are not yet reconciled with those of Table~\ref{occnet:tab:planning}. Under an occupancy-based collision metric, predicted occupancy is better than predicted boxes at 1\,s and 3\,s but worse at 2\,s (2.94\% \vs 2.85\%, Table~\ref{occnet:tab:occupancy_metrics}). TPVFormer remains better on car, truck and trailer, and a camera-only model is still well below a strong LiDAR segmentation model (60.46 \vs 76.10 mIoU for Cylinder3D).
  \item \emph{Flow is not evaluated.} OpenOcc and OpenScene annotate flow, but no experiment in this thesis evaluates flow prediction, for example by end-point error or mAVE, and the planner does not use flow. The flow annotations are a resource for later work, not a validated component.
  \item \emph{Statistics and planning protocol.} Every result comes from a single training run, without confidence intervals; frame-level bootstrap intervals could be computed from the existing predictions. Planning is evaluated open-loop with the ST-P3 protocol, which can be satisfied to a large extent from the ego vehicle's own state (Chapter~\ref{ch:background}), not in closed loop. The collision differences over boxes, 0.06--0.24 percentage points, come without a paired test, a conservativeness indicator or a sensitivity analysis of the cost normalisation.
  \item \emph{Distribution shift is not tested.} All experiments use nuScenes. Robustness to a change of city, weather or sensor set-up was not evaluated; not even the mIoU on the night and rain scenes of the nuScenes validation set, which the existing predictions would allow, is reported. Recognition beyond the 16 classes was not tested either: the irregular objects of Table~\ref{occnet:tab:irregular} are unusual shapes of known classes, not unseen categories.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Subsequent Developments (2023--2026)}\label{occnet:sec:subsequent}

\paragraph{Challenges and metrics.} Occupancy prediction became a community task soon after this work. The CVPR 2023 3D Occupancy Prediction challenge used the Occ3D-nuScenes labels~\cite{tian2023occ3d}, not OpenOcc: 0.4\,m voxels within $\pm$40\,m, scored by mIoU on voxels visible to the cameras. Its winning entry, FB-OCC, combined forward and backward view transformation with large-scale pre-training~\cite{li2023fbocc}. The Occupancy and Flow track of the CVPR 2024 Autonomous Grand Challenge used OpenOcc v2, which keeps flow annotations for foreground classes but whose voxel grid matches that of Occ3D-nuScenes rather than that of the version described in this chapter; it scored occupancy with RayIoU and flow with mAVE.\footnote{Challenge repositories: \url{https://github.com/CVPR2023-3D-Occupancy-Prediction/CVPR2023-3D-Occupancy-Prediction} and \url{https://github.com/OpenDriveLab/OccNet}.} RayIoU was proposed by Liu \etal~\cite{liu2024sparseocc} because voxel-level mIoU penalises depth errors along a camera ray inconsistently. The voxel-mIoU results of this chapter are therefore comparable neither with later RayIoU results nor with results on benchmarks with other label conventions, and they do not allow a quantitative placement of OccNet among the methods below.

\paragraph{Efficient representations.} Much of the later work addressed the cost of dense voxel volumes, which is substantial for OccNet (18G of memory and 2.6 FPS on a V100 GPU, Table~\ref{occnet:tab:efficiency}). FlashOcc keeps features in BEV and lifts only the output logits to 3D by a channel-to-height transformation~\cite{yu2023flashocc}, a design close in spirit to our BEVNet baseline. It reports higher accuracy on Occ3D-nuScenes than the voxel-level methods it compares with, at lower runtime and memory, which suggests that the accuracy gap between BEVNet and OccNet in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:efficiency} is not inherent to BEV features. PanoOcc aggregates multi-frame, multi-view information into voxel queries in a coarse-to-fine scheme, as our cascade does, and extends occupancy to panoptic segmentation~\cite{wang2024panoocc}. Sparse designs avoid computing empty voxels, with sparse latent representations~\cite{tang2024sparseocc}, fully sparse queries~\cite{liu2024sparseocc} or set prediction of occupied locations~\cite{wang2024opus}, and 3D semantic Gaussians offer a middle ground~\cite{huang2024gaussianformer,huang2025gaussianformer2}. Against this line, the cascade of OccNet is an early, dense step on the efficiency axis: it is cheaper than constructing voxel features directly, but it still computes every voxel.

\paragraph{Supervision and vocabulary.} Two limitations of this chapter, the dependence on 3D labels derived from LiDAR and the closed set of 16 classes, became research directions of their own. Rendering-based methods train occupancy networks from 2D semantic and depth labels through volume rendering~\cite{pan2024renderocc}, or without labels from video sequences through self-supervised rendering~\cite{huang2024selfocc}. Open-vocabulary methods attach language-aligned features to voxels, learned from images, language and LiDAR~\cite{vobecky2023pop3d} or by adapting 2D depth and vision-language foundation models~\cite{zheng2024veon}, so that occupancy can be queried with free-form categories. These directions address the semantic closedness that limits the answer to RQ1 (Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}), but the evidence of this thesis does not bear on them.

\paragraph{Forecasting, generation and pre-training.} Occupancy also became a state space for prediction and generation. Cam4DOcc benchmarks camera-only 4D occupancy forecasting~\cite{ma2024cam4docc}, OccWorld and OccSora forecast or generate future occupancy as world models~\cite{zheng2024occworld,wang2026occsora}, and UniScene generates semantic occupancy from a scene layout and conditions the generation of video and LiDAR on it~\cite{li2025uniscene}. These uses exploit the temporal dimension that the flow annotations of OpenOcc were meant to support but that this chapter did not evaluate. Occupancy and rendering objectives were also developed into general pre-training methods for camera and LiDAR perception~\cite{yang2024unipad,yan2025spot}, and visual point-cloud forecasting into a scalable pre-training task, ViDAR~\cite{yang2024vidar}; these works evaluate pre-training more broadly than our detection pre-training experiment (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:pretrain_for_detection}), which is confined to one detector and small fine-tuning sets. For planning, the picture remains mixed: in PARA-Drive, an occupancy head did not improve the NAVSIM score (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:disc_show}).

\paragraph{Towards a foundation model.} We concluded the original study with the hope that the occupancy framework can become the foundation model of autonomous driving. A foundation model needs data at a scale and diversity far beyond the 850 scenes of nuScenes, which motivates the benchmark described next.

\section{Scaling Up: The OpenScene Benchmark}\label{occnet:sec:openscene}

OpenOcc showed that occupancy can be learned from cameras and exploited across the driving stack, but it inherits the scale of nuScenes: like the other occupancy datasets built on nuScenes, it covers about 5.5 hours of driving (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:openscene}). For the foundation-model direction raised above, a large-scale dataset and benchmark is a must-have, and diversity matters as much as size; as we wrote in the release, ``driving behavior on a sunny day does not apply to that in dancing snowflakes''. OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene} scales occupancy supervision to more than 120 hours of driving collected in four cities. It follows the benchmark-driven research cycle of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}: rather than collecting new data, it curates an existing corpus, adds a new supervision signal, and bundles it with metrics, a baseline, a development kit and challenges. OpenScene was released as a dataset with documentation rather than as a paper. We led the project: we curated the data, generated the occupancy and flow labels, developed the development kit and integrated the dataset into the CVPR 2024 and 2025 challenges (see the statement of contribution). The figures in this section follow the public documentation, which has evolved since the first release.

\paragraph{A compact redistribution of nuPlan.} OpenScene is built on nuPlan~\cite{caesar2021nuplan}, whose raw sensor data exceed 20\,TB, a size that is hard to handle for many academic groups. OpenScene is a compact redistribution that retains only the relevant annotations and sensor data at 2\,Hz, which reduces the dataset size by more than a factor of ten; the sensor data of the trainval and test splits amount to approximately 2\,TB. Its folder layout is aligned with nuPlan, so that users who already hold nuPlan sensor data can link it instead of downloading it again. The release covers 1,521 logs and more than 120 hours of driving in Las Vegas (64\%), Singapore (15\%), Pittsburgh (12\%) and Boston (9\%), split into 1,310 trainval, 147 test and 64 mini logs. It inherits the nuPlan scenario categories, which cover dynamics (5 types, \eg high lateral acceleration), interactions (18 types, \eg waiting for pedestrians to cross), zones (8 types, \eg pickup--drop-off areas), manoeuvres (22 types, \eg unprotected cross turns) and behaviours (22 types, \eg stopping at a traffic light with a lead vehicle ahead). Each frame provides images from eight cameras and a merged LiDAR point cloud.

\paragraph{Labels.} On top of nuPlan, OpenScene provides 3D bounding boxes, occupancy and flow annotations in 3D space. The occupancy labels use 0.5\,m voxels, the voxel size of OpenOcc, within $[-50\,\text{m},50\,\text{m}]\times[-50\,\text{m},50\,\text{m}]\times[-4\,\text{m},4\,\text{m}]$, and each label accumulates LiDAR frames over a window of 20 seconds. The flow annotation gives the motion direction and velocity of each occupancy grid cell. As with OpenOcc, we added flow with decision-making in mind, since the motion information of occupancy flow ``can help bridge the gap between decision-making and scene representation'', as the release puts it; no experiment in this thesis evaluates it. The per-frame metadata include the ego pose and dynamic state, CAN bus data, the traffic-light status, a driving command (left, forward, right or unknown), camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and box annotations with velocities and with instance and track tokens. Tracks and ego states are provided at 2\,Hz, and scenes last 20\,s on average. The classes listed in the documentation are vehicle, bicycle, pedestrian, traffic cone, barrier, construction zone sign and generic object, plus background. \paragraph{Tasks and baseline.} The 2023 edition of OpenScene defined two tasks. The first is large-scale occupancy prediction, evaluated with the mIoU of Eq.~\eqref{occnet:eq:miou}. The second is an all-in-one foundation-model track scored by the OpenScene Metric, which averages nine downstream scores: 3D detection, semantic segmentation, scene completion, map construction, depth estimation, 3D lane detection, tracking, visual odometry and flow, measured with protocols from nuScenes, KITTI and Waymo. The occupancy baseline released with the benchmark is based on OccNet, with a ResNet-50 backbone. This thesis reports no baseline scores or challenge outcomes for either task.

\paragraph{Downstream use.} OpenScene became the data backbone of later benchmarks for end-to-end driving and world models (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:openscene_usage}). At the CVPR 2024 Autonomous Grand Challenge it supplied the data for two tracks. The \emph{End-to-End Driving at Scale} track was run with the NAVSIM development kit~\cite{dauner2024navsim}: a model receives camera images, LiDAR, ego states and navigation commands, and outputs a 4\,s trajectory, which is scored by the PDM Score after a 4\,s non-reactive simulation in which background actors replay the logs. The \emph{Predictive World Model} track, based on ViDAR~\cite{yang2024vidar}, asks a model to forecast point clouds for the next 3\,s from about 3\,s of past images and ego poses, and scores the forecasts by Chamfer distance. The private test sets of both tracks were provided exclusively by Motional. Version 1.1 of the dataset, released in March 2024, reorganised the metadata by nuPlan log and added raw LiDAR data; it also hosts the private test splits, including a hard split for the NAVSIM-v2 End-to-End Driving track of the CVPR 2025 Autonomous Grand Challenge. NAVSIM, on which Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} evaluates test-time training for end-to-end driving, uses the logs and sensor data that OpenScene redistributes from nuPlan; it does not use the occupancy labels. The link between this chapter and Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} is therefore the data redistribution, not occupancy supervision.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our documentation of OpenScene leaves several gaps. The number of semantic occupancy categories is still marked as to be determined in its comparison table, and the public documentation describes the annotation procedure only partially beyond the 20-second LiDAR accumulation. Occupancy labels are distributed with version 1.0, and a few frames of version 1.1 lack them. No assessment of label quality, such as a manual audit of sampled frames, is reported, and OpenScene has no accompanying peer-reviewed paper. The data are skewed towards Las Vegas (64\%), and they are licensed for non-commercial use only (CC BY-NC-SA together with the nuPlan licence).

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{occnet:sec:summary}

This chapter addressed RQ1 by proposing 3D occupancy as a geometry-open scene descriptor for autonomous driving. Instead of enclosing a closed set of object categories in boxes and flattening the background into a BEV map, occupancy quantises the space around the vehicle into voxels with semantic labels and flow, so that objects of any shape and structures above the ground are described in one grid. We presented OccNet, which reconstructs occupancy from surround-view cameras by lifting BEV features to voxels step by step with a cascade voxel decoder, voxel-based temporal self-attention and 3D deformable attention; OpenOcc, a dense occupancy benchmark on nuScenes with 34,149 frames, over 1.4 billion voxels in 16 classes, and flow for foreground objects; and OpenScene, which scales occupancy and flow supervision to more than 120 hours of nuPlan driving and whose logs and sensor data were later used by NAVSIM, by the end-to-end driving and predictive world-model tracks of the CVPR 2024 Autonomous Grand Challenge, and by the NAVSIM-v2 track of its CVPR 2025 edition.

The experiments give evidence of different strength on the three requirements of RQ1. Occupancy is learnable from cameras: OccNet reaches 26.98 mIoU and 41.08 $\text{IoU}_{\text{geo}}$ on OpenOcc, against 23.67 and 37.47 for TPVFormer, the strongest baseline we retrained on OpenOcc. From cameras alone, and queried at the LiDAR points, it reaches 60.46 mIoU in LiDAR segmentation, about 5 points below the LiDAR-based RangeNet++ (65.50) and below the published camera-only TPVFormer-Base trained with point labels (68.9). Occupancy describes irregular objects better than voxelised boxes, and its advantage grows from 2.21 to 3.51 $\text{mIoU}_{\text{ir}}$ as the voxel size shrinks from 0.5\,m to 0.25\,m, although part of this gap may reflect how object labels are constructed. For planning, in open-loop evaluation on nuScenes, planning on predicted occupancy gave slightly lower collision rates than planning on boxes predicted by the same network (0.06--0.24 percentage points, single runs; not statistically established), without a consistent gain in L2. With a ResNet50 backbone, the cascade design is slightly more accurate than a direct-voxel design at a clearly lower cost (40M instead of 72M parameters) and more accurate than a BEV-only design. The limitations are equally clear. The labels derive from an annotated dataset and its LiDAR; the semantics are a fixed set of 16 classes, so occupancy is open in geometry but not in vocabulary; flow is annotated but not evaluated; occupancy pre-training did not help the planner; all perception results are on a benchmark we built; and all experiments use a single dataset, single runs and open-loop planning metrics, without evaluating distribution shift. In Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}, two uses of occupancy in other planners did not raise the NAVSIM score, so the thesis does not establish that occupancy improves planning in general.

Occupancy tells the agent \emph{what is where}. It does not tell the agent \emph{why} a situation matters or \emph{what to do} about it. The same occupied voxels describe a pedestrian at the kerb whether or not that pedestrian intends to cross, and a stopped vehicle ahead whether it is parked or waiting at a red light, yet each case calls for a different decision. Answering such questions requires general knowledge about how traffic works, and reasoning that connects perception to the prediction of other agents and to the planning of the ego vehicle. This motivates Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm}, which grounds the general knowledge of vision-language models in driving through structured, verifiable reasoning that proceeds from perception through prediction to planning.
