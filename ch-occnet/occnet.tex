Any embodied agent is bounded by what its sensors and internal representations capture. Before an automated vehicle chooses to decelerate, change lanes, or yield, it must determine which regions of the surrounding 3D volume contain physical matter and identify what occupies them. Conventional autonomous driving perception has historically split this problem into two simplified abstractions: dynamic traffic participants are enclosed in 3D cuboids drawn from a predefined taxonomy, while static road infrastructure is projected onto a planar bird's-eye-view (BEV) grid. Together, these abstractions assume that every relevant hazard belongs to a catalogued object class, that a rigid rectangular prism adequately approximates its geometry, and that vertical structure above the ground plane can be safely ignored. Real-world traffic routinely violates all three premises. Cranes on service trucks swing outward beyond their chassis, flatbed lorries carry pipes overhanging the rear bumper, elevated signs and bridges span the roadway at varying clearances, and irregularly shaped barriers or foliage border the drivable corridor. Cuboid detectors clip or miss such protruding geometry, whereas planar BEV maps collapse the vertical dimension altogether.

This chapter tackles the first research question of the thesis (RQ1): how can an embodied driving system encode its 3D environment so that the representation accommodates arbitrary obstacle geometries in the physical world, remains predictable from multi-camera video alone, and directly informs downstream trajectory planning? We address this question through \emph{3D occupancy}, a volumetric scene representation that partitions the space around the ego vehicle into a regular 3D lattice of voxels, assigning each occupied cell a semantic category and, for dynamic foreground entities, a motion-flow vector. Because occupancy grids do not enforce parametric shape priors, they capture arbitrarily shaped foreground obstacles and static background structures within a single coordinate frame directly from surround-view cameras. The representation is thus geometrically open-world even though its semantic taxonomy remains closed (each occupied cell takes one of a fixed set of class labels). Our core hypothesis is that this volumetric lattice acts as a versatile \emph{scene descriptor}, serving as a shared intermediate representation for multiple perception and planning modules. We evaluate this hypothesis on the nuScenes dataset under single-run training and open-loop trajectory metrics; out-of-distribution generalisation is not evaluated here, and the irregular obstacles analysed belong to known semantic categories with atypical geometry rather than unseen categories.

Four primary contributions structure this chapter. First, we introduce OccNet\footnote{Distinct from the single-object Occupancy Networks of Mescheder \etal~\cite{mescheder2019occupancy}, which model continuous 3D object surfaces implicitly via neural classifiers.}, a multi-camera network that infers dense 3D occupancy from surround-view imagery. Starting from a spatial-temporal BEV feature map, OccNet reconstructs vertical structure stage by stage through a cascade voxel decoder equipped with voxel-level temporal attention and efficient 3D deformable attention. Second, we construct OpenOcc, a dense 3D occupancy benchmark derived from nuScenes~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes,fong2022panoptic} that provides per-voxel semantic labels across both foreground traffic agents and background structure together with per-voxel velocity vectors for moving objects. Third, we evaluate the resulting occupancy descriptor across five tasks: semantic scene completion (SSC), camera-based LiDAR point segmentation, 3D bounding-box detection, BEV map segmentation, and open-loop motion planning. On OpenOcc, the cascaded voxel descriptor achieves higher completion accuracy than planar BEV baselines and our retrained TPVFormer~\cite{huang2023tpvformer} baseline, while capturing non-cuboid vehicles more faithfully than voxelised bounding boxes. Under open-loop nuScenes evaluation, trajectory filtering on predicted occupancy yields slightly lower collision rates than filtering on boxes from OccNet's auxiliary detection head (by 0.06--0.24 percentage points in single runs, without statistical significance testing). Fourth, we present OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene}, which expands volumetric occupancy and flow annotations from roughly 5.5 hours of nuScenes logs to over 120 hours of multi-city nuPlan driving. The redistributed sensor logs and metadata of OpenScene subsequently underpinned NAVSIM as well as the end-to-end driving and predictive world-model tracks at the CVPR 2024 and CVPR 2025 Autonomous Grand Challenges, although NAVSIM itself consumes the redistributed sensor streams rather than the voxel annotations.

Methodologically, OccNet builds upon two established building blocks: it adopts the spatial-temporal BEV encoder of BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer} (co-authored by the candidate) and generalises 2D deformable attention~\cite{Zhu2021DeformableDD} to 3D voxel grids in a manner analogous to the concurrent deformable queries of VoxFormer~\cite{li2023voxformer}. The specific technical additions introduced here are the progressive coarse-to-fine height cascade, temporal self-attention operating directly in 3D voxel space, the dense OpenOcc annotation pipeline with object flow, and the unified evaluation of a single volumetric descriptor across perception and planning.

\section{Motivation}\label{occnet:sec:intro}
\suppressfloats[t]

Human drivers effortlessly judge the 3D layout of nearby traffic. In everyday driving, a motorist immediately recognises a saloon car five metres to the left or a flatbed lorry fifty metres ahead with a long pipe extending past its tailgate. Capturing this direct spatial awareness is vital for safe automated driving: before classifying every detail, the system must know whether a region of 3D space is blocked. Achieving such awareness from cameras alone remains challenging because urban streets contain a wide variety of physical structures, ranging from passenger cars, articulated lorries, and construction machinery to portable barriers, cyclists, overhanging foliage, and roadside buildings.

Rigid 3D bounding boxes struggle to capture articulated or overhanging vehicle parts in everyday traffic, such as the protruding cargo in~(a) or the extended crane boom in~(c). Representing the surrounding volume through 3D occupancy~(d) preserves these geometric details and provides a shared scene descriptor~(e) for downstream tasks including motion planning.}
  \label{occnet:fig:motivation}

Discretising the surrounding volume into a regular lattice of semantically labelled voxels, which we refer to as 3D occupancy, offers a direct way to model arbitrary 3D geometry. Similar representations have gained traction in industrial autonomous driving systems: Mobileye reconstructs dense pseudo-LiDAR point clouds from multi-camera views via VIDAR (``visual LiDAR'')~\cite{mobile2020ces}, while Tesla models the environment directly as a 3D occupancy grid~\cite{tesla_ai_day} (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:motivation}(a)--(b)).\footnote{Mobileye's VIDAR is distinct from ViDAR~\cite{yang2024vidar}, the visual point-cloud forecasting pre-training framework used in the world-model challenge track discussed in Section~\ref{occnet:sec:openscene}.} Whereas a 3D bounding box forces every object into a single rectangular prism, a volumetric voxel grid adapts to arbitrary contours by assembling fine-grained cubic elements. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:motivation}(c)--(d), a bounding box encloses only the main cab of a construction truck, whereas occupancy cells faithfully trace its extended crane arm.

Table~\ref{occnet:tab:compare_to_other_form} contrasts 3D occupancy against the three dominant perception representations in autonomous driving. Cuboid detection represents only countable foreground categories at coarse whole-object granularity. Point-cloud semantic segmentation resolves fine surface structure down to roughly 0.02\,m, but relies on expensive LiDAR hardware at deployment. Planar BEV segmentation jointly models foreground traffic and static road layout, yet projects away vertical height. Volumetric 3D occupancy bridges these trade-offs: it unifies foreground obstacles and background environment within a 3D grid at 0.25--0.5\,m resolution while operating purely from camera images at inference time. This motivates testing whether a learned occupancy representation can simultaneously serve standard 3D perception benchmarks and downstream trajectory planning.

Although occupancy grids have long been used in mobile robotics~\cite{moras2015grid}, classical robotic pipelines treat them purely as binary free-space maps for path search rather than learned semantic descriptors. Within computer vision, 3D semantic scene completion (SSC)~\cite{song2017sscnet} formulations closely parallel occupancy prediction, yet early camera-driven SSC architectures~\cite{huang2023tpvformer,li2023voxformer,cao2022monoscene,miao2023occdepth} exhibit two clear limitations. First, with the exception of the temporal variant of VoxFormer~\cite{li2023voxformer}, they process each camera frame in isolation, overlooking the strong multi-view geometric cues available across consecutive time steps. Second, single-stage volumetric decoders~\cite{huang2023tpvformer,miao2023occdepth,cao2022monoscene} lift features directly to full 3D resolution without progressive coarse-to-fine refinement, trading off spatial fidelity against memory footprint. Furthermore, prior SSC benchmarks evaluated voxel accuracy in isolation rather than testing whether the resulting representation benefits end-to-end perception-to-planning stacks~\cite{hu2023uniad}.

To close these gaps, we develop OccNet, a surround-camera architecture that reconstructs 3D occupancy through a temporal cascade voxel decoder and feeds the resulting volumetric representation into multiple task heads. At the centre of OccNet is a shared 3D occupancy embedding. Instead of lifting image features directly into a high-resolution 3D tensor or relying solely on 2D BEV grids~\cite{li2022bevformer,chen2022persformer,wang2022monocular}, OccNet progressively expands BEV features along the vertical axis through a multi-stage cascade. Each stage applies voxel-level temporal self-attention and image-to-voxel cross-attention accelerated by 3D deformable sampling. From this shared descriptor, task-specific heads perform semantic scene completion, 3D box detection, BEV semantic mapping, and open-loop trajectory planning. To support training and evaluation, we compile OpenOcc on top of nuScenes~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes,fong2022panoptic}, providing 34,149 frames with more than 1.4 billion labelled voxels across 16 semantic classes, augmented with per-voxel motion-flow vectors on foreground objects. While the flow vectors are released to support future motion forecasting and planning research, the experiments in this chapter evaluate static semantic occupancy only.

Our empirical evaluation on OpenOcc yields three principal findings, each accompanied by methodological caveats examined in Sections~\ref{occnet:sec:experiments} and~\ref{occnet:sec:discussion}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Perception performance.} On OpenOcc semantic scene completion, OccNet reaches 26.98 mIoU, exceeding our retrained TPVFormer~\cite{huang2023tpvformer} baseline (23.67 mIoU) by 14\% in relative terms. When queried at ground-truth LiDAR returns using camera images alone, OccNet trails the LiDAR-input RangeNet++~\cite{milioto2019rangenet++} baseline by roughly 5 mIoU points. When used to initialise a 3D detector fine-tuned on 1/256 to 1/64 of the nuScenes training split, occupancy pre-training outperforms FCOS3D~\cite{wang2021fcos3d} backbone initialisation, reaching a maximum gain of roughly 10 mAP points at the 1/128 split.
  \item \emph{Open-loop planning.} Under the open-loop nuScenes evaluation protocol, rasterising predicted occupancy into the planner safety cost reduces collision rates by 0.06--0.24 percentage points compared with rasterising boxes from OccNet's auxiliary detection head (single runs, without statistical significance tests), while L2 trajectory displacement shows no uniform improvement.
  \item \emph{Supervision density.} Pre-training on dense OpenOcc labels yields higher low-data detection transfer than sparse LiDAR-voxel supervision at the 1/128 data fraction, while performing comparably at 1/256 and 1/64.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{occnet:sec:related}

We first situate 3D occupancy relative to established output representations in autonomous driving perception and contemporaneous scene-completion methods at the time of our study. Broader context on multi-camera 3D perception appears in Chapter~\ref{ch:background} and in our co-authored BEV perception survey~\cite{li2023bevdevils}; follow-up occupancy research from 2023 to 2026 is reviewed in Section~\ref{occnet:sec:subsequent}.

\paragraph{3D object detection.} Standard driving perception pipelines~\cite{Shi2020PVRCNNPF,wang2021fcos3d,li2022bevformer,liu2023bevfusion} summarise traffic participants as oriented 3D bounding boxes, a compact format tailored for classical tracking and rule-based motion planners. However, reducing every obstacle to a canonical cuboid omits static background hazards and distorts articulated or irregularly shaped vehicles. Volumetric occupancy instead resolves 3D geometry at the level of individual grid cells, accommodating arbitrary obstacle contours.

\paragraph{LiDAR segmentation.} Point-cloud semantic segmentation models~\cite{zhu2021cylindrical,milioto2019rangenet++} assign class labels directly to 3D laser returns. While accurate near the sensor, this paradigm requires costly LiDAR hardware at deployment and produces measurements whose spatial density decays rapidly with distance, leaving occluded or distant regions unrepresented~\cite{song2017sscnet}.

\paragraph{BEV segmentation.} Map and object segmentation in bird's-eye view~\cite{zhou2022cross,li2022hdmapnet} collapses vertical structure into a 2D ground-plane grid. In complex urban scenes, discarding elevation loses overhanging structures such as gantries, tree branches, and bridge decks. Volumetric occupancy retains explicit vertical resolution across the 3D workspace.

\paragraph{3D reconstruction and rendering.} Recovering 3D scene geometry from multi-view 2D imagery is a long-standing problem in computer vision~\cite{Hartley2004,mildenhall2020nerf}. Neural implicit representations and radiance fields~\cite{mueller2022instant,cao2022scenerf,Tancik2022blocknerf}, including continuous object-level occupancy networks~\cite{mescheder2019occupancy}, typically optimise per-scene geometry or photometric rendering quality, making them difficult to deploy for real-time feed-forward semantic perception across unseen street scenes.

\paragraph{Semantic scene completion.} Volumetric occupancy prediction is closely related to 3D semantic scene completion (SSC)~\cite{song2017sscnet}. MonoScene~\cite{cao2022monoscene} pioneered monocular dense 3D SSC using a 2D-to-3D U-Net architecture. Around the time of our work, several concurrent camera-based SSC approaches emerged: VoxFormer~\cite{li2023voxformer} seeds deformable voxel queries from estimated depth maps in two stages; OccDepth~\cite{miao2023occdepth} leverages stereo depth distillation; TPVFormer~\cite{huang2023tpvformer} decomposes 3D space into tri-perspective orthogonal planes supervised by sparse LiDAR points; and OpenOccupancy~\cite{wang2023openoccupancy} introduces a dense nuScenes benchmark. Earlier SSC studies focused primarily on front-camera SemanticKITTI~\cite{behley2019semantickitti} or indoor RGB-D NYUv2~\cite{Silberman2012nyuv2} benchmarks rather than 360-degree surround-view driving. Moreover, apart from VoxFormer-T~\cite{li2023voxformer} and the BEV detector BEVDet4D~\cite{huang2022bevdet4d}, contemporaneous SSC models operated on single frames; while Tesla~\cite{tesla_ai_day} had demonstrated temporal occupancy fusion in industry presentations, architectural specifics remained unpublished. Our work investigated temporal multi-camera occupancy reconstruction as a shared representation supporting both perception and downstream planning.

\section{OccNet}\label{occnet:sec:method}
\suppressfloats[t]

As depicted in Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:framework}, OccNet extracts volumetric features from multi-camera imagery to drive a suite of perception and planning heads. The architecture is organised into two consecutive phases: \emph{occupancy reconstruction} and \emph{occupancy exploitation}, connected by an intermediate 3D representation that we call the \emph{occupancy descriptor}.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ch-occnet/images/fig_pipeline_overview_v8_used.pdf}
  \caption[The OccNet pipeline]{\textbf{The OccNet pipeline.} A two-stage framework constructs a shared 3D occupancy descriptor and routes it to downstream driving heads. \textbf{I.~Occupancy reconstruction.} Surround-view images first pass through a spatial-temporal BEV encoder. A multi-stage cascade voxel decoder then progressively expands vertical resolution, integrating historical voxel features $V'_{t-1,i}$ and multi-view image features via 3D deformable attention (some skip connections omitted for clarity). \textbf{II.~Occupancy exploitation.} Task heads read the occupancy descriptor to perform semantic scene completion and 3D box detection; projecting their predictions onto the ground plane produces a BEV occupancy map for trajectory planning~\cite{hu2022stp3}.}
  \label{occnet:fig:framework}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Reconstruction of occupancy.} The first phase constructs a general-purpose 3D voxel feature tensor from surround-view images. Building on advances in camera-based BEV representation learning~\cite{li2022bevformer,chen2022persformer,liang2022bevfusion}, we examine how best to lift 2D planar features into 3D voxel space. Two baseline extremes bound the design space: \emph{BEVNet}, which feeds a 2D BEV grid directly into task heads at minimal compute cost but lacks explicit vertical structure, and \emph{VoxelNet}, which maintains full-resolution 3D voxel queries throughout the encoder at heavy memory and runtime expense.\footnote{The names BEVNet and VoxelNet refer strictly to the ablation baselines defined in this chapter; our camera-based VoxelNet is distinct from the point-cloud detector of Zhou and Tuzel~\cite{zhou2018voxelnet}.} OccNet bridges these two extremes: in single-run comparisons, its cascade achieves slightly higher completion accuracy than VoxelNet at lower memory and parameter cost, while outperforming BEVNet at moderate additional compute (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:analysis}). Specifically, multi-camera image features $F_t$ are first combined with the previous BEV embedding $B_{t-1}$ and learnable BEV queries $Q_t$ inside a BEVFormer spatial-temporal encoder~\cite{li2022bevformer} to yield the current BEV map $B_t$. Next, the cascade voxel decoder (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:cascade_voxel_decoder}) jointly processes $B_{t-1}$, $B_t$, and $F_t$ to reconstruct the 3D occupancy descriptor.

\paragraph{Exploitation of occupancy.} The second phase attaches modular task heads to the shared 3D descriptor, following the explicit intermediate-representation philosophy of UniAD~\cite{hu2023uniad} (co-authored by the candidate). Heads for 3D semantic scene completion~\cite{song2017sscnet} and 3D bounding-box detection operate directly on the volumetric features. Collapsing either the predicted 3D voxel grid or the detected 3D cuboids along the vertical axis produces a binary BEV obstacle map. A sampling-based trajectory planner conditioned on the navigation command evaluates candidate paths against this obstacle map and refines the minimum-cost trajectory through a gated recurrent unit (GRU). Section~\ref{occnet:sec:exploitation_of_occupancy} describes each task head in detail.

\subsection{Cascade Voxel Decoder}\label{occnet:sec:cascade_voxel_decoder}

To recover fine vertical structure without the prohibitive cost of full-resolution 3D attention from the outset, the cascade voxel decoder expands the height dimension gradually across stages.

\paragraph{From BEV to cascaded voxels.} Predicting 3D voxels directly from a 2D BEV tensor degrades geometric accuracy, whereas maintaining a dense 3D grid across all transformer layers inflates memory consumption and latency (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:efficiency}). We therefore factor the mapping from the planar feature $B_t\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C_{\text{BEV}}}$ to the target volumetric tensor $V_t\in\mathbb{R}^{Z\times H\times W\times C_{\text{Voxel}}}$ into $N$ sequential stages. Here $H\times W$ denotes the horizontal grid resolution, $Z$ is the final vertical voxel count, and $C_{\text{BEV}}, C_{\text{Voxel}}$ are channel widths. At cascade stage $i$, the intermediate tensor $V'_{t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{Z_i\times H\times W\times C_i}$ doubles its vertical resolution $Z_i$ towards $Z$ while halving or reducing its channel width $C_i$ from $C_{\text{BEV}}$ down to $C_{\text{Voxel}}$ according to the schedule in Section~\ref{occnet:sec:exp_setup}. As shown in Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:framework}, a feed-forward projection first lifts $B_{t-1}$ and $B_t$ into the initial 3D tensors $V'_{t-1,1}$ and $V'_{t,1}$, and an inter-stage MLP reshapes the vertical-channel product from $Z_i\times C_i$ to $Z_{i+1}\times C_{i+1}$ between stages $i$ and $i+1$ (Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:implementation}). Within each stage, voxel-based temporal self-attention fuses $V'_{t,i}$ with the aligned historical tensor $V'_{t-1,i}$, after which voxel-based spatial cross-attention aggregates multi-scale camera features $F_t$. Iterating across the $N$ stages yields the final occupancy descriptor $V_t$.

\paragraph{Voxel-based temporal self-attention.} Multi-frame history supplies crucial multi-view geometry for camera-based 3D reconstruction~\cite{li2022bevformer}. In our single-run ablation on OpenOcc, incorporating two historical frames improves mIoU by 0.27 points over a single-frame baseline, while four historical frames raise the gain to 1.60 mIoU points (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:frame_number_ssc}). At each stage $i$, the prior voxel tensor $V'_{t-1,i}$ is ego-motion-compensated into the coordinate frame of $V'_{t,i}$. Because dense all-pairs self-attention across a 3D grid scales quadratically with $Z$ and is computationally intractable, we introduce \emph{3D deformable attention} (3D-DA), restricting each voxel query to attend to a small set of learned local 3D offsets.

\paragraph{3D deformable attention.} Extending 2D deformable attention~\cite{Zhu2021DeformableDD} into three dimensions, let $V'_{t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{Z_i\times H\times W\times C_i}$ denote the input voxel volume, $\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_i}$ a voxel query vector, and $\boldsymbol{p}$ its 3D reference coordinate. The 3D-DA operator computes
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{3D-DA}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p},V'_{t,i})
  = \sum_{m=1}^{M} W_m \sum_{k=1}^{K} A_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})\, W'_m\, V'_{t,i}\big(\boldsymbol{p}+\Delta\boldsymbol{p}_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})\big),
  \label{occnet:eq:3dda}
\end{equation}
with $M$ attention heads, $K\ll Z_iHW$ sampling points per head, and head-specific linear projections $W_m\in\mathbb{R}^{C_i\times(C_i/M)}$ and $W'_m\in\mathbb{R}^{(C_i/M)\times C_i}$. Linear layers applied to $\boldsymbol{q}$ predict both the softmax-normalised weights $A_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})$ (satisfying $\sum_{k}A_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})=1$) and the 3D spatial offsets $\Delta\boldsymbol{p}_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})$, while features at fractional coordinates $\boldsymbol{p}+\Delta\boldsymbol{p}_{mk}(\boldsymbol{q})$ are retrieved via trilinear interpolation. Following standard Deformable DETR conventions~\cite{Zhu2021DeformableDD} in the released implementation, we set $M=8$ heads and $K=4$ sampled keys per head, anchoring reference points $\boldsymbol{p}$ at regular voxel centres with offset projections initialised around the local neighbourhood.

\paragraph{Voxel-based spatial cross-attention.} To inject image appearance into each cascade stage, $V'_{t,i}$ queries the multi-scale camera feature maps $F_t$ via 2D deformable cross-attention~\cite{Zhu2021DeformableDD}. Specifically, stage $i$ draws $N_{\text{ref},i}$ 3D reference points within each voxel cell, projects them onto the surround-view camera planes using known calibration parameters, and aggregates the sampled 2D features. Unlike BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer}, which samples points along a single vertical pillar per ground cell, partitioning reference points per voxel preserves vertical discrimination across height bins.

\subsection{Exploiting Occupancy for Driving Tasks}\label{occnet:sec:exploitation_of_occupancy}

The reconstructed volumetric descriptor $V_t$ provides a shared representation from which lightweight task heads decode perception and planning outputs.

\paragraph{Semantic scene completion.} A lightweight multi-layer perceptron (MLP) classifies each voxel into one of the semantic categories or free space, supervised by the focal loss~\cite{Lin2017FocalLF} to counter the strong ratio of empty to occupied cells. In parallel, a regression branch supervised by an $L_1$ loss predicts per-voxel horizontal velocity vectors for occupied foreground cells.

\paragraph{3D object detection.} Following the query-based detector of BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer}, we pool the volumetric descriptor along the vertical axis into a BEV feature map and attach a Deformable DETR detection head~\cite{Zhu2021DeformableDD} to regress 3D bounding boxes.

\paragraph{BEV segmentation.} Adopting the perception head layout of ST-P3~\cite{hu2022stp3}, we decode planar map layers and semantic masks from the pooled BEV features using four dedicated branches covering drivable surface, lane dividers, vehicles, and pedestrians.

\paragraph{Motion planning.} To evaluate trajectory planning, either the predicted 3D occupancy grid or the predicted 3D bounding boxes are projected vertically into a binary BEV obstacle mask (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:framework}), where 1 marks an occupied ground cell and 0 denotes free space. Candidate trajectories generated by sampling velocity, acceleration, and curvature profiles are scored by a composite cost combining safety, comfort, and route progress. Because dense occupancy grids populate more background cells than sparse object boxes, the safety cost weight is scaled across the two rasterised map types in our planner configuration to keep the obstacle penalty balanced relative to the comfort and progress terms; this scaling directly influences the open-loop collision rates in Section~\ref{occnet:sec:main_results}, and we did not run a sensitivity sweep over the scaling factor. Neither ground-truth nor predicted voxel flow is used by the planner. Conditioned on the discrete driving command (forward, left turn, or right turn), the lowest-cost candidate trajectory is selected and passed through a front-camera-conditioned GRU refinement layer following ST-P3~\cite{hu2022stp3}.

\section{The OpenOcc Benchmark}\label{occnet:sec:benchmark}

To benchmark camera-based 3D occupancy reconstruction and downstream tasks under dense supervision, we construct OpenOcc on top of the nuScenes dataset~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes,fong2022panoptic}. Developed concurrently with Occ3D~\cite{tian2023occ3d}, OpenOccupancy~\cite{wang2023openoccupancy}, and SurroundOcc~\cite{wei2023surroundocc}, OpenOcc was among the earliest dense surround-view occupancy benchmarks on nuScenes and uniquely augments voxel semantics with per-voxel object motion vectors (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:openscene}). Whereas earlier SSC datasets such as SemanticKITTI~\cite{behley2019semantickitti} cover only a forward-facing frustum, OpenOcc pairs 360-degree six-camera imagery with full-surround 3D voxel and flow annotations. Because contemporaneous nuScenes occupancy benchmarks differ in spatial bounds, voxel size, semantic taxonomy, and camera-visibility masks, numerical scores cannot be compared directly across them; for instance, the CVPR 2023 occupancy challenge adopted Occ3D-nuScenes rather than OpenOcc (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:subsequent}).

\subsection{Benchmark Overview}\label{occnet:sec:benchmark_overview}

OpenOcc derives dense volumetric ground truth by accumulating multi-sweep LiDAR point clouds and 3D bounding-box tracks across all 700 training and 150 validation scenes of nuScenes, totalling 34,149 annotated frames and over 1.4 billion occupied voxels. Each occupied cell receives one of 16 semantic labels split between 10 dynamic foreground categories (barrier, bicycle, bus, car, construction vehicle, motorcycle, pedestrian, traffic cone, trailer, truck) and 6 static background categories (driveable surface, other flat, sidewalk, terrain, manmade, vegetation), supplemented by 2D horizontal velocity vectors for foreground voxels. The workspace spans $\mathcal{V}=[-50\,\text{m},50\,\text{m}]\times[-50\,\text{m},50\,\text{m}]\times[-5\,\text{m},3\,\text{m}]$ in the ego LiDAR frame and is discretised at $\Delta s=0.5$\,m into a $200\times200\times16$ voxel lattice.

\subsection{Generating High-Quality Annotations}\label{occnet:sec:annotation}

\paragraph{Independent accumulation of background and foreground.} Densifying sparse LiDAR sweeps requires aggregating points across both annotated key frames (2\,Hz) and unannotated intermediate sweeps (20\,Hz)~\cite{behley2019semantickitti}. Naively transforming all sweeps into a static global frame smears moving traffic into motion streaks. We therefore partition each LiDAR sweep using the 3D bounding-box annotations: points outside all boxes are treated as static background and registered in the global world frame, whereas points falling inside a bounding box are transformed into that object's local canonical coordinate frame. For intermediate sweeps lacking manual box labels, we linearly interpolate object centres and yaw orientations between bracketing key frames (Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:openocc}).

\paragraph{Generation of annotations.} After registering the accumulated static and dynamic point clouds back into the target key-frame coordinate system, we quantise the volume into 0.5\,m voxels and assign each occupied cell the majority semantic label of the key-frame panoptic LiDAR points~\cite{fong2022panoptic} falling inside it. Foreground voxels simultaneously inherit 3D velocity vectors derived from the finite-difference motion of their enclosing 3D bounding boxes. Because intermediate LiDAR sweeps carry no point-level semantic annotations, voxels populated solely by intermediate-sweep returns inherit the dominant semantic label of their local $3\times3\times3$ voxel neighbourhood. To remedy small vertical pose drift along the $z$-axis in nuScenes ego-odometry, we apply morphological surface completion to fill gaps in the drivable road plane. Lastly, we cast rays from the camera optical centres through the voxel grid to flag occluded cells that are unobservable from the surround-view cameras, focusing camera-based supervision on visible surfaces. Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:occ_gt_pipeline} in Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:openocc} illustrates the complete pipeline. While qualitative spot checks were performed on sampled scenes when tuning the interpolation, neighbourhood-propagation, and hole-filling heuristics, no exhaustive frame-by-frame manual verification of the generated voxel labels was conducted.

\section{Experiments}\label{occnet:sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{occnet:sec:exp_setup}

\paragraph{Implementation.} Mirroring the training configuration of BEVFormer~\cite{li2022bevformer}, we evaluate two image backbones: an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50~\cite{he2016deep,deng2009imagenet} and an FCOS3D-pretrained ResNet101-DCN~\cite{he2016deep,wang2021fcos3d}. The planar BEV grid $B_t$ has spatial dimensions $H=W=200$ and channel width $C_{\text{BEV}}=256$. Across the $N=4$ cascade stages, the volumetric feature maps $V'_{t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{Z_i\times H\times W\times C_i}$ use vertical resolutions $Z_i=2^i$ with channel dimensions $C_1=C_2=128$ and $C_3=C_4=64$, and voxel-based spatial cross-attention samples $N_{\text{ref},i}=4$ reference heights per voxel cell. Unless noted otherwise, models are optimised for 24 epochs at a base learning rate of $2\times10^{-4}$ with two historical frames in temporal self-attention, matching the memory-efficient multi-frame budget of BEVFormer-small; although the horizon ablation in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:frame_number_ssc} reaches a higher score with four historical frames (20.81 \vs 19.48 mIoU), two frames are kept as the standard setting across the main tables to bound training memory and latency. Architectural specifications for the BEVNet and VoxelNet variants are provided in Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:implementation}.

\paragraph{Tasks and metrics.} Volumetric scene completion on OpenOcc is scored using 16-class mean intersection-over-union (mIoU) alongside class-agnostic geometric occupancy $\text{IoU}_{\text{geo}}$. Both operate directly on voxel grids; the ray-based RayIoU metric~\cite{liu2024sparseocc}, introduced subsequently to mitigate the depth-dependent penalty of voxel IoU, was not part of the original evaluation (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:subsequent}). Point-cloud segmentation and 3D bounding-box detection are benchmarked on the nuScenes validation split using the standard competition metrics, while planar BEV segmentation is scored by class IoU. Motion planning follows the open-loop protocol of ST-P3~\cite{hu2022stp3}, measuring L2 waypoint displacement and box-overlap collision rate against logged trajectories. Because open-loop nuScenes planning metrics can be largely satisfied from ego-vehicle kinematics alone~\cite{zhai2023rethinking,li2024ego} (Chapter~\ref{ch:background}), these figures quantify trajectory plausibility on recorded logs rather than closed-loop driving safety. Formal metric equations appear in Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:metrics}; each model configuration is trained once without multi-seed confidence intervals.

\paragraph{Occupancy for LiDAR segmentation.} Because 3D occupancy partitions space into volumetric cells, shrinking the grid step $\Delta s\to0$ brings semantic scene completion closer to point-wise LiDAR segmentation. Point-supervised camera models had therefore already overtaken RangeNet++ under this point-query setup, whereas voxel-level supervision leaves OccNet below that mark.

\paragraph{Pre-trained occupancy for 3D detection and BEV segmentation.} Training OccNet on volumetric scene reconstruction produces spatial features that can serve as weight initialisation for downstream 3D perception heads. In Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:pretrain_for_detection}, fine-tuning the multi-camera detector on fractional nuScenes splits (1/256, 1/128, and 1/64 of the training data) yields higher detection scores when weights are initialised from OpenOcc-pretrained OccNet checkpoints rather than from the monocular detector FCOS3D~\cite{wang2021fcos3d}, reaching a maximum spread of roughly 10 mAP points at the 1/128 fraction. Three caveats qualify this comparison. First, pre-training objective and parameter coverage vary together: because FCOS3D lacks BEV and volumetric modules, it transfers weights only to the 2D image backbone, whereas occupancy pre-training additionally warm-starts the BEV and voxel stages (and the ResNet101 OccNet checkpoint itself starts from FCOS3D image weights). Pre-training an identical multi-view architecture on 3D box detection would provide a stricter baseline. Second, the FCOS3D-initialised runs collapse to near-zero mAP at the 1/256 and 1/128 subsets, pointing to optimisation instability in the extreme low-data regime, and data fractions beyond 1/64 (including 100\% of the training split) were not evaluated. Third, because OpenOcc derives its supervision from the full nuScenes LiDAR and 3D box annotations, these fractional runs test label efficiency only during downstream fine-tuning rather than reducing overall dataset annotation effort. When transferring to BEV semantic and map segmentation in single-seed runs (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:bev_seg}), initialising from occupancy similarly edges out detection pre-training across all four categories, lifting the four-class mean IoU from 18.18 to 19.17 and drivable-area IoU from 44.59 to 47.21.

\paragraph{Occupancy for planning.} To evaluate downstream trajectory selection, predictions from OccNet (either 3D bounding boxes from the jointly trained detection head of Table~\ref{occnet:tab:joint_train} or semantic occupancy voxels) are projected onto the ground plane as rasterised BEV masks and passed into the trajectory cost filter and GRU refinement stage of ST-P3~\cite{hu2022stp3}. We benchmark rasterised OccNet occupancy against rasterised OccNet box detections as well as against a re-executed ST-P3 BEV segmentation baseline, restricting the semantic channels to vehicles and pedestrians to match the two-class ST-P3 setup. Following the open-loop nuScenes protocol, candidate plans are never rolled out in simulation; instead, metrics compare the selected trajectory against logged expert positions and future obstacle footprints without reactive surrounding agents. Every row in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:planning} reflects a single training and evaluation run, alongside oracle runs fed with rasterised ground-truth annotations. Within the oracle block, ground-truth occupancy yields the lowest collision percentages. Across the predicted representations, OccNet occupancy records the lowest collision rate at all three prediction horizons: 0.21\% at 1\,s, 0.55\% at 2\,s, and 1.35\% at 3\,s, compared with 0.27\%, 0.68\%, and 1.59\% when planning from OccNet's predicted boxes. Under this open-loop nuScenes setup, substituting predicted occupancy for predicted boxes trims collision rates by 0.06--0.24 percentage points (or 15\%--22\% in relative terms) in single-seed runs, a margin too small to establish statistical significance. Even across the full validation split of roughly 6,000 frames (estimated from the 34,149 annotated frames across 850 scenes in Section~\ref{occnet:sec:benchmark_overview} under uniform scene length), shifts of 0.24 and 0.06 percentage points correspond to roughly 14 and 4 frames, respectively, and neither frame-wise paired tests nor confidence intervals were computed.

\paragraph{How strong is the planning evidence?} Four methodological factors further qualify these open-loop figures. First, trajectory L2 error does not move in lockstep with collision rate. When restricted to the two foreground classes of ST-P3, predicted occupancy produces a slightly higher 3\,s L2 displacement than predicted boxes (3.07\,m \vs 3.03\,m), a pattern consistent with more cautious trajectory selection rather than superior scene understanding. Incorporating all 16 occupancy categories achieves the lowest L2 errors among predicted inputs (1.29\,m, 2.13\,m, and 2.99\,m) but raises 2\,s and 3\,s collision rates relative to the two-class occupancy variant, leaving no single predicted representation dominant on both criteria. Neither ego-progress indicators (such as mean planned velocity) nor blind ego-status baselines, which perform competitively under open-loop nuScenes scoring~\cite{zhai2023rethinking,li2024ego}, were evaluated. Second, seed-to-seed variance matches the scale of the inter-method gaps: independent planner evaluations in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:occupancy_metrics} shift 3\,s L2 distances for identical inputs by up to 0.05\,m relative to Table~\ref{occnet:tab:planning} (Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:experiments}). Third, the bounding-box baseline relies on OccNet's auxiliary detection head (0.276 mAP in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:joint_train}) rather than a specialised standalone 3D detector. Consequently, the experiment contrasts two heads of the same network rather than general box and occupancy paradigms; when detector errors are removed by supplying ground-truth inputs, the collision gap between occupancy and boxes narrows to 0.03--0.20 percentage points. Fourth, collision outcomes depend on how safety costs are normalised across box and occupancy rasters (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:exploitation_of_occupancy}), a hyperparameter whose sensitivity was not swept.

\paragraph{Comparison with the published ST-P3.} Under the same evaluation protocol, the original ST-P3 paper~\cite{hu2022stp3} reports collision rates of 0.23\%, 0.62\%, and 1.27\% alongside L2 errors of 1.33\,m, 2.11\,m, and 2.90\,m at 1\,s, 2\,s, and 3\,s, outperforming our re-executed ST-P3 baseline (0.50\%, 0.88\%, and 1.49\% collision; 1.39\,m, 2.21\,m, and 3.02\,m L2). Compared against those published figures, OccNet occupancy yields fewer collisions at 1\,s and 2\,s (0.21\% \vs 0.23\% and 0.55\% \vs 0.62\%) but a higher rate at 3\,s (1.35\% \vs 1.27\%), together with larger L2 displacements at 2\,s and 3\,s (2.18\,m \vs 2.11\,m and 3.07\,m \vs 2.90\,m). Even the ground-truth oracle rows in our re-run exhibit higher 3\,s collision rates (1.30\% and 1.50\%) than the published ST-P3 checkpoint using predicted segmentation, reflecting implementation differences in our re-executed training and cost-filtering pipeline. Because the 9\%--58\% relative collision reductions over BEV segmentation depend on this weaker re-run, the controlled comparison between occupancy and boxes decoded from the same OccNet model remains the more informative reference. Qualitatively, Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:planning} shows an example in which full-class occupancy keeps the selected path inside the drivable corridor and clear of background obstacles.

\paragraph{Irregular objects.} Cuboid bounding boxes struggle to tightly enclose articulated foreground categories such as construction vehicles or elevated roadside structures such as traffic signs (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:irregular}). To quantify how volumetric and box representations handle non-cuboid geometry, we voxelize predicted 3D bounding boxes and compute mean IoU ($\text{mIoU}_{\text{ir}}$) across the truck, trailer, and construction-vehicle categories in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:irregular}. At the standard grid step of $\Delta s=0.5$\,m, direct occupancy prediction attains 18.13 $\text{mIoU}_{\text{ir}}$, ahead of voxelized detections at 15.92. Repeating the experiment on a finer $\Delta s=0.25$\,m grid widens the margin between occupancy and voxelized boxes from 2.21 to 3.51 points (13.41 \vs 9.90), as smaller cells capture non-rectangular contours more faithfully.

Two experimental artefacts nonetheless tilt this comparison toward occupancy regardless of object geometry. First, during OpenOcc label generation, foreground LiDAR returns are gathered inside each instance's interpolated 3D bounding box (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:annotation}), whereas protrusions extending outside the box (such as the crane boom in Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:motivation}(d)) are not grouped with the instance track. Because LiDAR sweeps only the exterior of an obstacle, the resulting ground-truth object voxels largely form a surface shell inside the cuboid, whereas voxelizing a predicted 3D box populates its entire interior volume. Consequently, part of the $\text{mIoU}_{\text{ir}}$ advantage may reflect comparing surface-shell targets against solid cuboids rather than irregular contours alone. Second, Table~\ref{occnet:tab:irregular} omits approximately rectangular categories such as car and bus as a baseline control. Isolating shape irregularity from volumetric filling would require evaluating hollow or visibility-masked box voxelizations alongside standard vehicle classes, an analysis not performed here.

\paragraph{Dense versus sparse occupancy.} Multi-frame accumulated occupancy labels reconstruct denser foreground and background surfaces than single-sweep LiDAR voxelization (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:compare_gt}), which suggests that dense supervision should provide a richer pre-training signal for downstream perception and planning. The transfer curves in Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:pretrain_for_detection} offer only mixed support for that expectation: dense occupancy pre-training outperforms sparse pre-training at the 1/128 fine-tuning split (roughly 0.11 \vs 0.09 mAP on the curve), yet both initialisations perform similarly at 1/256 and 1/64, with sparse supervision holding a slight mAP edge at 1/64. A single-seed fine-tuning sweep provides limited evidence on pre-training density, and we did not evaluate how sparse versus dense supervision affects trajectory planning.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{occnet:sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Experiments Show}\label{occnet:sec:disc_show}

\paragraph{What occupancy provides.} Our empirical findings address the three criteria posed by RQ1 with varying degrees of support. First, regarding spatial validity beyond cuboid taxonomies, voxel occupancy imposes no parametric shape prior; it captures articulated vehicles more accurately than voxelized detections (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:irregular}, subject to the surface-versus-volume caveat in Section~\ref{occnet:sec:analysis}) while simultaneously representing background obstacles omitted by 3D bounding boxes. Because the trajectory filter in Section~\ref{occnet:sec:exploitation_of_occupancy} operates on binarised occupancy grids, any occupied cell penalises candidate trajectories regardless of category, allowing the 16-class planner in Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:planning} to steer clear of unboxed background structures. Even so, semantic predictions remain restricted to 16 predefined categories, meaning that the representation is open in 3D geometry while remaining closed in semantic taxonomy. Second, regarding camera-only learnability, OccNet infers volumetric semantics solely from surround-view images and, when evaluated at ground-truth LiDAR returns, trails the LiDAR segmenter RangeNet++ by roughly 5 mIoU points (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:lidarseg}). Third, regarding downstream planning utility, feeding predicted occupancy to the open-loop nuScenes planner trims collision percentages by 0.06--0.24 points relative to boxes decoded from the same network in single runs, without a uniform reduction in L2 displacement (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:main_results}). These planning gains therefore represent preliminary rather than definitive support.

\paragraph{Relation to the occupancy results of Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}.} Two NAVSIM experiments reported later in Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} (Table~\ref{centaur:tab:navtest_all_supp}) point in the opposite direction when occupancy is integrated differently. In UniAD, applying test-time trajectory post-optimisation against a hand-crafted occupancy cost reduces overall PDMS from 83.4 to 75.2 and depresses every constituent metric: no-collision drops from 97.8 to 95.4, drivable-area compliance from 91.9 to 86.0, time-to-collision from 92.9 to 87.3, and ego progress from 78.8 to 71.9. In PARA-Drive, attaching an auxiliary occupancy head shifts PDMS from 84.0 to 83.6, trading a minor gain in time-to-collision (93.0 to 93.7) against reduced ego progress (79.3 to 78.3). Although those evaluations operate at a higher simulation fidelity (non-reactive L1 rollouts rather than L0 open-loop scoring), they also rely on single training seeds, distinct network backbones, and different mechanisms for coupling occupancy to planning (post-hoc trajectory optimisation or multi-task feature regularisation, rather than rasterised cost filtering in a sampling-based planner). Neither chapter cross-evaluates the other's planning pipeline. Viewed together, the results across this thesis show that volumetric occupancy does not automatically benefit motion planning; any downstream gain depends on how the planner consumes the grid, with positive evidence restricted to the small open-loop shift observed here.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}\label{occnet:sec:limitations}

Several methodological constraints bound the scope of our conclusions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Reliance on curated LiDAR and box annotations.} Generating OpenOcc targets requires the calibrated LiDAR sweeps and 3D bounding-box tracks of nuScenes, together with linear box interpolation across intermediate sweeps, and we did not conduct a manual quality audit of the resulting voxel labels. Weakly supervised or neural-rendering pipelines explored in subsequent literature (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:subsequent}) mitigate this annotation dependency.
  \item \emph{Evaluation on a single self-constructed benchmark.} Every 3D perception experiment is conducted on OpenOcc, whose voxelization and visibility rules differ from concurrent benchmarks such as Occ3D-nuScenes, SurroundOcc, and OpenOccupancy, against baselines retrained within our own codebase (where the adapted BEV detectors appear under-optimised). Because we did not cross-evaluate on external occupancy suites or against later architectures, the home-benchmark threat to validity discussed in Section~\ref{sec:method:validity} applies throughout this chapter.
  \item \emph{Evaluation metrics.} Volumetric reconstruction is assessed solely via voxel-wise mIoU and $\text{IoU}_{\text{geo}}$, omitting ray-based metrics such as RayIoU that weight depth errors more uniformly along viewing rays. Similarly, our LiDAR segmentation transfer queries voxel predictions only at ground-truth laser returns, measuring semantic classification on known surfaces rather than verifying camera-predicted 3D geometry in free space.
  \item \emph{Spatial quantisation.} Discretising space into 0.5\,m cells limits tasks that require sub-voxel box localisation and sizing (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:joint_train}), whereas halving the cell width to 0.25\,m improves both non-cuboid object IoU (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:irregular}) and point-queried LiDAR segmentation (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:lidarseg_resolution}, subject to the discrepancy noted in Appendix~\ref{occnet:app:experiments}). Spatial resolution therefore governs downstream utility, and a 0.5\,m grid is insufficient for fine-grained geometric regression.
  \item \emph{Task-dependent utility of occupancy.} Initialising the planner from occupancy-pretrained weights fails to improve upon detection pre-training (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:planning_pretrain}, whose baseline magnitudes also differ from Table~\ref{occnet:tab:planning}). When collisions are scored against an occupancy-grid criterion (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:occupancy_metrics}), predicted occupancy outperforms predicted boxes at 1\,s and 3\,s but trails slightly at 2\,s (2.94\% \vs 2.85\%). Furthermore, TPVFormer retains an edge on large regular vehicles (car, truck, and trailer), and camera-only OccNet remains far behind dedicated LiDAR segmentation with Cylinder3D (60.46 \vs 76.10 mIoU).
  \item \emph{Unvalidated occupancy flow.} Although both OpenOcc and OpenScene include per-voxel velocity vectors, this thesis neither benchmarks flow estimation accuracy (via end-point error or mAVE) nor incorporates flow into the trajectory planner, leaving the flow labels as an unvalidated dataset asset for subsequent research.
  \item \emph{Single-seed runs and open-loop planning.} All reported tables reflect single training runs without bootstrap confidence intervals or multi-seed variance estimates. Trajectory planning is scored exclusively under the open-loop ST-P3 protocol on logged trajectories, where ego-kinematic extrapolation alone is known to perform strongly (Chapter~\ref{ch:background}), and the 0.06--0.24 percentage-point collision margins over predicted boxes lack paired significance tests, progress metrics, or cost-weight sensitivity sweeps.
  \item \emph{Untested domain generalisation.} Every model is trained and tested within nuScenes, leaving cross-city transfer, adverse weather, and camera-rig changes unexamined (even subset breakdowns on nuScenes night and rain validation scenes were not extracted). Open-world category generalisation is likewise untested: the articulated vehicles in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:irregular} represent geometrically complex instances of training classes rather than unseen semantic categories.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Subsequent Developments (2023--2026)}\label{occnet:sec:subsequent}

\paragraph{Challenges and metrics.} Community benchmarks rapidly standardised 3D occupancy evaluation following our initial release. At CVPR 2023, the 3D Occupancy Prediction challenge adopted Occ3D-nuScenes~\cite{tian2023occ3d} rather than OpenOcc, evaluating camera-visible voxels at 0.4\,m resolution within a $\pm$40\,m range via mIoU; its top submission, FB-OCC, paired bidirectional view projection with large-scale pre-training~\cite{li2023fbocc}. The subsequent Occupancy and Flow track at the CVPR 2024 Autonomous Grand Challenge transitioned to OpenOcc v2, which retains foreground flow vectors but aligns its voxel grid with Occ3D-nuScenes rather than the specification in this chapter, ranking occupancy by RayIoU and velocity estimation by mAVE.\footnote{\sloppy Challenge repositories: \url{https://github.com/CVPR2023-3D-Occupancy-Prediction/CVPR2023-3D-Occupancy-Prediction} and \url{https://github.com/OpenDriveLab/OccNet}.} Introduced by Liu \etal~\cite{liu2024sparseocc}, RayIoU remedies the inconsistent depth penalties that standard voxel mIoU assigns along viewing rays. Because both grid definitions and evaluation metrics diverged in these later suites, the voxel-mIoU scores reported in this chapter cannot be compared directly against RayIoU leaderboards or benchmarks built on alternative visibility masks.

\paragraph{Efficient representations.} Subsequent architectures targeted the heavy memory and latency footprint of dense 3D feature volumes (18G memory and 2.6 FPS on a V100 GPU for OccNet in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:efficiency}). FlashOcc retains 2D BEV representations throughout the network and reshapes channels into height bins only at the final logit layer~\cite{yu2023flashocc}, a planar design akin to our BEVNet baseline that nonetheless surpasses several dense 3D decoders on Occ3D-nuScenes at a fraction of their runtime and memory, indicating that the gap between BEVNet and OccNet in Table~\ref{occnet:tab:efficiency} stems from decoder design rather than an intrinsic ceiling of BEV features. PanoOcc similarly refines multi-frame, multi-camera voxel queries through a coarse-to-fine hierarchy while extending predictions to panoptic occupancy~\cite{wang2024panoocc}. Other methods eliminate empty-space computation altogether via sparse latent grids~\cite{tang2024sparseocc}, fully sparse query tokens~\cite{liu2024sparseocc}, direct point-set prediction~\cite{wang2024opus}, or 3D semantic Gaussian primitives~\cite{huang2024gaussianformer,huang2025gaussianformer2}. Relative to these sparser formulations, OccNet's cascade decoder represents an intermediate dense architecture that lowers the cost of full-grid processing but still evaluates every voxel in the volume.

\paragraph{Supervision and vocabulary.} Two core constraints of our framework, namely reliance on LiDAR-derived 3D labels and a closed 16-class taxonomy, also prompted dedicated follow-up tracks. Volume-rendering approaches supervise 3D occupancy from 2D depth and semantic maps~\cite{pan2024renderocc} or purely self-supervised multi-view video consistency~\cite{huang2024selfocc}. Simultaneously, open-vocabulary frameworks distil vision-language features into 3D voxels using cross-modal LiDAR-image-text alignment~\cite{vobecky2023pop3d} or 2D foundation models~\cite{zheng2024veon}, enabling free-text spatial queries. While these methods directly address the fixed semantic vocabulary that bounds our answer to RQ1 (Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}), they lie outside the experimental scope of this thesis.

\paragraph{Forecasting, generation and pre-training.} Volumetric occupancy has further served as a structured latent space for temporal forecasting, generative world modelling, and representation learning. Cam4DOcc establishes a camera-only 4D occupancy forecasting benchmark~\cite{ma2024cam4docc}, OccWorld and OccSora autoregressively or diffusively generate future occupancy grids~\cite{zheng2024occworld,wang2026occsora}, and UniScene conditions multi-view video and LiDAR synthesis on generated semantic occupancy layouts~\cite{li2025uniscene}. These temporal models build upon the motion dimension that OpenOcc's flow vectors were designed to support. In parallel, volumetric rendering and point-cloud forecasting objectives such as UniPAD~\cite{yang2024unipad}, SPOT~\cite{yan2025spot}, and ViDAR~\cite{yang2024vidar} demonstrated broad multi-task pre-training transfers across camera and LiDAR benchmarks, extending well beyond our small-subset fine-tuning study on a single detector (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:pretrain_for_detection}). On the planning side, however, results remain task-dependent, as illustrated by the neutral effect of an auxiliary occupancy head in PARA-Drive on NAVSIM (Section~\ref{occnet:sec:disc_show}).

\paragraph{Towards a foundation model.} Our original study concluded by asking whether volumetric occupancy could serve as a general foundation representation for autonomous driving. Pursuing that goal requires training data whose duration and geographic variety far exceed the 850 scenes of nuScenes, motivating the large-scale benchmark introduced next.

\section{Scaling Up: The OpenScene Benchmark}\label{occnet:sec:openscene}

Although OpenOcc demonstrated camera-based occupancy learning and downstream integration across perception and planning, its corpus size remains bounded by nuScenes at roughly 5.5 hours of driving logs (Table~\ref{occnet:tab:openscene}). Scaling toward driving foundation models demands both larger data volume and broader environmental variety, since driving policies learned in clear weather do not automatically transfer to snowfall. OpenScene~\cite{sima2023openscene} expands 3D occupancy and flow supervision to over 120 hours of driving recorded across four cities. Following the benchmark-centred methodology outlined in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, it repackages an existing raw sensor corpus, enriches it with dense 3D annotations, and pairs the dataset with evaluation protocols, reference baselines, a software toolkit, and public competitions. Released as an open dataset and documentation suite rather than a standalone paper, OpenScene was led by our team across data curation, occupancy and flow label generation, devkit engineering, and challenge integration at CVPR 2024 and CVPR 2025 (see the statement of contribution). Numerical specifications below follow the current public documentation.

\paragraph{A compact redistribution of nuPlan.} Because the full nuPlan corpus~\cite{caesar2021nuplan} exceeds 20\,TB of raw sensor recordings, downloading and storing it is impractical for many academic laboratories. OpenScene curates the relevant metadata and subsamples multi-sensor streams at 2\,Hz, shrinking storage requirements by more than tenfold so that the combined trainval and test sensor splits occupy roughly 2\,TB. Directory paths mirror the native nuPlan hierarchy, allowing researchers with existing nuPlan copies to symlink their local sensor files directly. In total, the dataset spans 1,521 driving logs (over 120 hours) across Las Vegas (64\%), Singapore (15\%), Pittsburgh (12\%), and Boston (9\%), partitioned into 1,310 trainval, 147 test, and 64 mini logs. It preserves the nuPlan scenario taxonomy across five groupings: vehicle dynamics (5 categories, \eg high lateral acceleration), multi-agent interactions (18 categories, \eg yielding to crossing pedestrians), road zones (8 categories, \eg pickup--drop-off bays), driving manoeuvres (22 categories, \eg unprotected cross turns), and longitudinal behaviours (22 categories, \eg stopping behind a lead vehicle at a signal). Every 2\,Hz sample bundles eight surround-view camera frames with a merged LiDAR sweep.

\paragraph{Labels.} Augmenting the native nuPlan 3D bounding boxes, OpenScene supplies 3D voxel occupancy and per-cell velocity vectors across a $[-50\,\text{m},50\,\text{m}]\times[-50\,\text{m},50\,\text{m}]\times[-4\,\text{m},4\,\text{m}]$ volume at 0.5\,m resolution (matching OpenOcc), constructed by aggregating LiDAR sweeps over 20-second windows. Each occupied cell carries a 3D flow vector encoding its motion direction and speed, designed to link spatial perception with downstream motion planning, though flow prediction is not experimentally benchmarked in this thesis. Frame-level metadata record ego pose and kinematics, CAN bus telemetry, traffic-signal states, high-level route commands (left, forward, right, or unknown), camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and 3D object boxes with velocity vectors, instance IDs, and track tokens over 20\,s average clip durations at 2\,Hz. The documented semantic taxonomy comprises seven foreground categories (vehicle, bicycle, pedestrian, traffic cone, barrier, construction zone sign, and generic object) plus a single merged background class. Whereas OpenOcc partitions nuScenes into 16 classes (10 foreground and 6 background), OpenScene consolidates all static background surfaces into one category and regroups foreground obstacles, deriving voxel occupancy and cell flow by accumulating static returns and box-tracked dynamic points across each 20\,s clip.

\paragraph{Tasks and baseline.} Two benchmark tracks were specified in the 2023 OpenScene release: first, large-scale 3D semantic occupancy prediction scored via mIoU in Eq.~\eqref{occnet:eq:miou}; second, a multi-task foundation-model track ranked by the OpenScene Metric, which averages nine component evaluations (3D detection, semantic segmentation, scene completion, vectorised map construction, depth estimation, 3D lane detection, multi-object tracking, visual odometry, and occupancy flow) adapted from nuScenes, KITTI, and Waymo conventions. \paragraph{Downstream use.} Beyond occupancy perception, OpenScene's compact nuPlan redistribution became the shared data substrate for subsequent end-to-end planning and world-model competitions (Fig.~\ref{occnet:fig:openscene_usage}). During the CVPR 2024 Autonomous Grand Challenge, two competition tracks operated on OpenScene splits. First, the \emph{End-to-End Driving at Scale} track employed the NAVSIM framework~\cite{dauner2024navsim}, feeding multi-camera images, LiDAR sweeps, ego kinematics, and route commands into policies that output 4\,s trajectories evaluated by PDM Score over 4\,s non-reactive log-replay rollouts. Second, the \emph{Predictive World Model} track followed ViDAR~\cite{yang2024vidar}, requiring models to forecast 3\,s of future LiDAR point clouds from roughly 3\,s of past camera observations and ego motion, evaluated by Chamfer distance. Held-out private test logs for both tracks were supplied by Motional. In March 2024, OpenScene v1.1 restructured metadata files by nuPlan log, incorporated raw LiDAR point clouds, and added private evaluation splits, including the challenging test split used by the NAVSIM-v2 End-to-End Driving track at the CVPR 2025 Autonomous Grand Challenge. When Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} benchmarks test-time training in NAVSIM, it relies on these redistributed nuPlan sensor streams and ego trajectories rather than OpenScene's voxel occupancy annotations; consequently, the bridge from this chapter to Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur} lies in the curated data infrastructure rather than volumetric supervision.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Several gaps remain in the public OpenScene release. The documentation's dataset comparison table still lists the exact semantic class count as to be determined and summarises the annotation pipeline only briefly beyond the 20-second LiDAR aggregation window. Voxel occupancy targets are packaged with v1.0 while a small number of v1.1 frames lack occupancy files; furthermore, no manual audit of label accuracy or peer-reviewed publication accompanies the release. Finally, the driving logs are geographically weighted toward Las Vegas (64\%) and carry non-commercial academic licensing restrictions (CC BY-NC-SA combined with the nuPlan licence).

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{occnet:sec:summary}

To address RQ1, this chapter formulated 3D semantic occupancy as a geometry-open spatial representation for autonomous driving. Rather than fitting cuboids to a fixed list of foreground categories and projecting static infrastructure onto a 2D plane, volumetric occupancy discretises the surrounding 3D environment into semantic voxels with motion vectors, capturing arbitrarily shaped obstacles and overhead structures within a unified grid. Around this representation we developed three contributions: OccNet, which lifts surround-view camera features into 3D volumes through a coarse-to-fine cascade voxel decoder with voxel temporal self-attention and 3D deformable cross-attention; OpenOcc, a dense nuScenes benchmark comprising 34,149 frames and over 1.4 billion labelled voxels across 16 semantic categories with foreground flow vectors; and OpenScene, which expands occupancy and flow annotations to over 120 hours of nuPlan logs and subsequently supplied the sensor corpus for NAVSIM as well as the end-to-end driving and predictive world-modelling tracks at the CVPR 2024 and CVPR 2025 (NAVSIM-v2) Autonomous Grand Challenges.

Our experimental evaluation provides graded evidence across the three requirements of RQ1. First, volumetric occupancy can be inferred from surround-view cameras alone: on OpenOcc, OccNet attains 26.98 mIoU and 41.08 $\text{IoU}_{\text{geo}}$, outperforming our retrained TPVFormer baseline (23.67 mIoU and 37.47 $\text{IoU}_{\text{geo}}$), and when evaluated at ground-truth LiDAR returns it reaches 60.46 mIoU, trailing the LiDAR segmenter RangeNet++ (65.50) by roughly 5 points and the point-supervised camera model TPVFormer-Base (68.9). Second, volumetric grids capture non-cuboid vehicles more accurately than voxelized 3D detections, with the $\text{mIoU}_{\text{ir}}$ margin expanding from 2.21 to 3.51 points as grid resolution tightens from 0.5\,m to 0.25\,m, though part of this gap reflects comparing surface-shell labels against solid cuboids. Third, in single-run open-loop nuScenes planning, rasterised occupancy predictions lower collision percentages by 0.06--0.24 points relative to box predictions from the same network without a consistent improvement in L2 error. Architecturally, with a ResNet50 backbone, the cascade decoder improves upon both planar BEVNet and dense VoxelNet while trimming VoxelNet's parameter count from 72M to 40M. At the same time, clear boundaries apply: supervision still depends on curated LiDAR sweeps and interpolated 3D boxes; semantic predictions remain confined to 16 fixed classes; occupancy flow is annotated but not experimentally evaluated; occupancy pre-training does not aid the trajectory planner; all perception benchmarks rely on our own OpenOcc split; and every table reports single-seed runs without closed-loop simulation or domain-shift testing. Combined with the neutral or negative occupancy results observed on NAVSIM in Chapter~\ref{ch:centaur}, this thesis does not establish that 3D occupancy universally improves downstream planning.

Ultimately, a 3D occupancy grid specifies \emph{what occupies each location}, but not \emph{why a traffic configuration matters} or \emph{how the ego vehicle should respond}. Identical occupied voxels represent a kerbside pedestrian whether waiting or stepping into the roadway, and a stationary lead vehicle whether parked or held by a red signal, even though each scenario demands a distinct driving manoeuvre. Resolving such ambiguities requires world knowledge of traffic conventions together with explicit reasoning that links scene perception to agent intent prediction and ego trajectory planning. Chapter~\ref{ch:drivelm} addresses this gap by grounding vision-language models in driving scenes through structured, verifiable graph reasoning spanning perception, prediction, and planning.
